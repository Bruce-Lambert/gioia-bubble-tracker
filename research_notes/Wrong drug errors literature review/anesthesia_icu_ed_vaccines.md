# Wrong-drug errors in anesthesia/OR, ICU, ED/EMS, and vaccination (2006–2026, with landmark earlier work)

Scope note: evidence compiled from PubMed/PMC records (abstracts; full text for Nanji 2016), supplemented by web searches for grey literature (APSF, ISMP, FDA, Pennsylvania Patient Safety Authority, UK/Australian agencies). Direct fetches of apsf.org and ecri.org/ismp.org pages were blocked by the session's network allow-list, so figures from those bodies come from search snippets or secondary coverage and are flagged as such. Every numeric claim below carries first author, year, journal, setting, denominator and a PMID/DOI/URL. Where a figure could not be verified against a primary record it is placed in Gaps rather than Findings.

Terminology: "substitution" (Webster/Merry taxonomy) = wrong drug given; "syringe swap" = correctly labelled syringe picked in error; "ampoule/vial swap" = wrong ampoule drawn up (often look-alike packaging); "insertion" = unintended drug given in addition to intended.

## KQ1. Anesthesia: incidence of drug administration error per anesthetic and the share that are substitution / wrong-drug ("syringe swap") errors

### Takeaway
Facilitated self-report studies converge on roughly 1 error per 130–450 anesthetics (0.2–0.75%), while direct observation finds an error or adverse drug event in about 1 in 20 medication administrations (Nanji 2016) and 9–12 per 100 administrations including recording errors (Merry 2011). Across methods, substitution/wrong-drug errors are consistently the first- or second-largest category (≈20–30% of errors in self-report and incident databases; 48% in Thai and Japanese single-center series; 69% of patent errors reported to the French agency), and in surveys 85–94% of anesthetists admit at least one wrong-drug administration in their career.

### Inferences
- The two-order-of-magnitude gap between self-report (~0.2–0.7% of anesthetics) and observation (~5% of administrations; ~45–50% of operations) is methodological, not geographic: the same institutions (e.g., Nanji at MGH, Merry in Auckland) show it. Report writers should never pool self-report and observational rates.
- Substitution's share is remarkably stable (20–30%) across NZ, South Africa, China, USA, Australia/NZ and France, suggesting a generic human-factors mechanism (look-alike ampoules, syringe pick errors) rather than local practice.
- Where incidents are filtered for severity (Irita's "critical incidents", Lobaugh's serious-event registry, Cranshaw's litigation), wrong-drug/swap share either shrinks (Irita 4.3% of critical incidents, no deaths) or is dominated by neuromuscular blockers (Cranshaw: 16 of ~31 wrong-drug claims; awake paralysis commonest outcome).

### Gaps
- Nanji 2016's exact count of "wrong medication" errors is in a table not reproduced in the PMC text; the breakdown above (labeling 24.2%, wrong dose 22.9%, omission 17.6%) is from the Results text, and a specific wrong-drug percentage could not be verified.
- The 30% "substitution" figure from the ASA Closed Claims Project comes from an APSF newsletter citation (Caplan), not from the primary Closed Claims paper; direct access to apsf.org was blocked, so year/sample of the primary analysis is unverified.
- Hirabayashi 2005 and Sakaguchi 2008 are Japanese-language papers; only English abstracts were reviewed.
- No 2017–2026 prospective observational study with wrong-drug rates per administration (other than Nanji) was found; the recent literature is dominated by registries, surveys and single-center retrospective reviews.

## KQ2. Anesthesia: which drugs are swapped most and what harm results (neuromuscular blockers, tranexamic acid intrathecal, epinephrine/ephedrine, succinylcholine, vecuronium/Versed)

### Takeaway
Neuromuscular blockers are the single most frequently swapped class in every dataset that reports drug class (≈50% of wrong-drug administrations in Gordon 2006; 32% of errors in Abbasi 2022; 31.7% in THAI; 15 of 28 syringe swaps in Fasting 2000; 16 of ~31 wrong-drug NHS claims), producing awake paralysis, awareness and airway emergencies; tranexamic acid mistaken for bupivacaine during spinal anaesthesia is the highest-lethality swap (10 of 21 published cases died to 2018; 8 of 22 further cases 2018–2022), and vasoactive look-alikes (epinephrine for ephedrine) and the vecuronium-for-midazolam (Vaught) case illustrate the same look-alike/selection mechanisms outside the OR.

### Cited Findings

**Neuromuscular blockers (NMBs)**

- Orser 2001 (Canada survey): commonest error = muscle relaxant given instead of reversal agent — [PMID 11220422](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11220422/)
- Abeysekera 2005 (AIMS): drugs most commonly involved in drug error were NMBs, then opioids; awareness in 40 (4.4%) incidents — [PMID 15710005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15710005/)
- Osborne 1993 (AIMS, first 2,000 incidents): 16 awareness cases, 6 of them inadvertent paralysis before induction, "most commonly by 'syringe swap' when suxamethonium was given instead of fentanyl" — [PMID 8273892](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8273892/); [DOI 10.1177/0310057X9302100528](https://doi.org/10.1177/0310057X9302100528)

- RaDonda Vaught / Vanderbilt (26 Dec 2017): vecuronium given instead of Versed (midazolam) to a 75-year-old before PET scan; nurse typed "VE" into the automated dispensing cabinet, no result (system expected "midazolam"), overrode, selected vecuronium; the powder vial carried a "Warning: Paralyzing Agent" cap; barcode scanning was not available in the imaging area; patient became brain-dead; nurse convicted March 2022 of criminally negligent homicide and gross neglect — [Fox6/AP report](https://www.fox6now.com/news/former-vanderbilt-nurse-guilty-of-homicide-in-fatal-medication-error); [PMC9720757 commentary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9720757) (notes ISMP ADC best practices: sequestering paralysing agents from ADCs, tiered override prevention, 5-letter minimum search entry, barcode scanning); [Wikipedia case summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RaDonda_Vaught_homicide_case); ISMP Aug 2021 statement that the Board of Nursing action "set patient safety back by 25 years" is quoted in [PSLhub](https://www.pslhub.org/blogs/blog/1-news/?cat=1007) (secondary)
- Nanji 2016 full text: syringe swaps and ampoule swaps named as perioperative errors that "can all cause serious harm"; most frequently cited critical incidents in anesthesia are drug administration errors — [PMC4681677](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4681677/)

**Tranexamic acid (TXA) mistaken for local anaesthetic at spinal anaesthesia**
- Patel, Robertson & McConachie 2019, Anaesthesia (MEDLINE 1960–2018): 21 cases of spinal TXA; life-threatening neurological/cardiac complications in 20; 10 died; ampoule error the cause in 20 (one spinal catheter mistaken for IV catheter); all skill-based errors; all preventable by four published recommendations — [PMID 30985928](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30985928/); [DOI 10.1111/anae.14662](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.14662)
- Patel 2023, Eur J Anaesthesiol (Jul 2018–Sep 2022): 22 further intrathecal TXA errors; death in 8 (36%), permanent harm in 4 (19%); 15/22 in orthopaedics (10) and caesarean section (5); 19/21 developed refractory status epilepticus; HFACS: mistaking look-alike TXA ampoules for local anaesthetic the predominant cause — [PMID 36877159](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36877159/); [DOI 10.1097/EJA.0000000000001812](https://doi.org/10.1097/EJA.0000000000001812)
- Patel 2015, Anesth Analg (obstetric neuraxial drug errors, 29 published cases): 4 maternal deaths, all from accidental intrathecal TXA after delivery; most common human factors drug storage and similar drug appearance; 4 recommendations (read label; label all syringes; second-person or barcode check; non-Luer neuraxial connectors) — [PMID 26579659](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26579659/); [DOI 10.1213/ANE.0000000000000938](https://doi.org/10.1213/ANE.0000000000000938)
- Mohseni 2009, Anesth Analg: case of polymyoclonus seizure from TXA injected at spinal anaesthesia — [PMID 19448237](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19448237/); [DOI 10.1213/ane.0b013e3181a04d69](https://doi.org/10.1213/ane.0b013e3181a04d69)

- Related neuraxial wrong-drug reviews by the same group: potassium chloride (Patel 2021, Reg Anesth Pain Med — [PMID 34380745](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34380745/)); antibiotics (Patel 2025, Pain Ther — [PMID 39792237](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39792237/)); Liu 2017 Acta Anaesthesiol Scand review of inadvertent intrathecal injections — [PMID 27766633](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27766633/)

**Vasoactive look-alikes (epinephrine vs ephedrine; concentrated epinephrine)**
- Gordon 2006: 43 (14%) of wrong-drug administrations involved vasoactive drugs — [PMID 16909189](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16909189/)
- Sakaguchi 2008: cardiac stimulants and vasopressors among the three most-involved classes — [PMID 18536329](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18536329/)
- Amor 2012: grade-III pulmonary oedema from inappropriate adrenaline administration — [PMID 23040790](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23040790/)
- Orser & Oxorn 1994, Can J Anaesth: near-fatal cardiac arrest from inadvertent epinephrine injection (ventricular dysrhythmias, pulmonary oedema) in a healthy elective patient; recommendations included distinct ampoule labels and a standardized colour code — [PMID 7802751](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7802751/); [DOI 10.1007/BF03009804](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03009804)
- James 1998, Anaesthesia: letter "Ephedrine/Epinephrine drug label confusion" — [PMID 9659030](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9659030/); [DOI 10.1046/j.1365-2044.1998.04771.x](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2044.1998.04771.x)
- Pennsylvania Patient Safety Advisory 2006 (PA-PSRS reports): PACU nurse gave IV epinephrine when ephedrine ordered; obstetric near-miss caught by anesthesia; recommends tall-man lettering EPInephrine/ePHEDrine — [PA Patient Safety Advisory Sept 2006](https://patientsafety.pa.gov/ADVISORIES/Pages/200609_16.aspx)
- APSF Newsletter Winter 2002 "Concentrated solutions cause concern": look-alike epinephrine/ephedrine ampoules and dilution steps; suggests removing concentrated epinephrine from anesthesia carts — [APSF](https://www.apsf.org/article/concentrated-solutions-cause-concern/)
- Shaw 2014, Jt Comm J Qual Patient Saf (34 US children's hospitals): 27/34 supply epinephrine 0.1 mg/mL (1:10,000) in pharmacy/industry prefilled syringes; concentrations of several anesthetic drugs varied between and within institutions; none used bar-code medication administration — [PMID 26111307](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26111307/); [DOI 10.1016/s1553-7250(14)40060-6](https://doi.org/10.1016/s1553-7250(14)40060-6)

**Other classes**
- Opioids/sedatives: most common classes in Wake Up Safe paediatric registry (Lobaugh 2017 — [PMID 28742772](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28742772/)) and in Gariel 2018 (opioids, antibiotics — [PMID 29452813](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29452813/)); respiratory depression needing ICU was the costliest NHS claim category (13 claims, £2.75M; Cranshaw 2009 — [PMID 19849678](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19849678/))
- Propofol, phenylephrine, fentanyl the drugs most associated with errors under direct observation (Nanji 2016 — [PMC4681677](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4681677/))
- Oxytocin via epidural (Tcherepanova 2026, Hosp Pharm — [PMID 41509893](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41509893/)); TXA via epidural without sequelae (von Wendt 2026, Int J Obstet Anesth — [PMID 42385474](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42385474/)); methadone substitution fatality in a dental sedation cocktail (Kupiec 2011, J Anal Toxicol — [PMID 21871161](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21871161/))

### Inferences
- Harm from NMB swaps is usually transient but psychologically severe (awareness/awake paralysis) and occasionally fatal when airway support is unavailable (Hsieh neonates; Vaught). Harm from TXA-for-bupivacaine swaps is catastrophic in >50%, so even a low frequency makes TXA segregation and non-look-alike packaging a disproportionately high-value intervention.
- The APSF/FDA emphasis on storage (removing TXA and concentrated epinephrine from anesthesia carts) targets the ampoule-swap pathway (Llewellyn: 36.9% of errors from ampoule misidentification), whereas barcode-at-administration targets the syringe-swap pathway; both pathways co-exist and need separate countermeasures.

### Gaps
- No denominator-based incidence of intrathecal TXA error exists; all data are published case series (likely "tip of the iceberg" as Patel 2015 states).
- Succinylcholine-specific swap frequency after 2006 was not found beyond Osborne 1993/Fasting 2000 and Ducat 2000 (pre-drawn drugs); modern series report "NMBs" aggregate.
- ISMP's own analysis of the Vaught case could not be fetched (ismp.org/ecri.org blocked); figures above rely on court reporting and a PMC commentary.

## KQ3. ICU (adult, paediatric, neonatal): wrong-drug rates in medication-error studies

### Takeaway
Multinational ICU studies find administration-stage parenteral errors at 38.8–74.5 per 100 patient-days, dominated by omission and wrong-time; wrong drug is a minority category (counts not stated in the SEE abstracts), and ICU-specific evidence consistently ranks wrong dose, wrong time/rate and omission above wrong drug, with neonatal direct-observation studies identifying wrong preparation/dilution rather than wrong drug as the dominant administration error.

### Cited Findings
- Valentin 2006, Intensive Care Med (SEE study): 205 ICUs worldwide, 1,913 adults, 24-h cross-section; 584 events in 391 patients = 38.8 events per 100 patient-days (95% CI 34.7–42.9); medication-related errors in 136 patients; lines/catheters 158; equipment 112; airway 47; alarms 17 — [PMID 16874492](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16874492/); [DOI 10.1007/s00134-006-0290-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00134-006-0290-7)
- Valentin 2009, BMJ (SEE 2, parenteral administration errors): 113 ICUs in 27 countries, 1,328 adults; 861 errors affecting 441 patients = 74.5 (95% CI 69.5–79.4) per 100 patient-days; three quarters errors of omission; 12 patients (0.9%) permanent harm or death; protective: basic monitoring OR 0.19, critical incident reporting system OR 0.69, routine checks at shift change OR 0.68; risk: patients per nurse OR 1.30 per extra patient — [PMID 19282436](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19282436/); [PMC2659290](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2659290/); [DOI 10.1136/bmj.b814](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.b814). - Rothschild 2005, Crit Care Med (Critical Care Safety Study, USA, MICU + CCU, 1 year): 391 patients, 1,490 patient-days; 120 adverse events (45% preventable) and 223 serious errors; rates per 1000 patient-days 80.5 (AEs), 36.2 (preventable AEs), 149.7 (serious errors); medications involved in 61% (170/277) of serious errors; slips and lapses 53% of performance failures — [PMID 16096443](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16096443/); [DOI 10.1097/01.ccm.0000171609.91035.bd](https://doi.org/10.1097/01.ccm.0000171609.91035.bd)

- Kiekkas 2011, Am J Crit Care (review of 6 direct-observation studies of ICU nurses 1985–2008): wrong dose, wrong time/rate and omission the most common error types; antibiotics, electrolytes, cardiovascular drugs most involved; life-threatening/fatal events rare — [PMID 21196570](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21196570/); [DOI 10.4037/ajcc2011331](https://doi.org/10.4037/ajcc2011331); companion letter on self-report underestimating wrong-dose errors — [PMID 20002092](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20002092/)

**Paediatric / neonatal ICU**
- Kaushal 2001, JAMA (2 US academic hospitals, 1,120 paediatric admissions): 10,778 orders; 616 medication errors (5.7%), 115 potential ADEs (1.1%), 26 ADEs (0.24%); potential ADE rate significantly higher in NICU neonates; 79% of potential ADEs at ordering, 34% incorrect dosing — [PMID 11311101](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11311101/); [DOI 10.1001/jama.285.16.2114](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.285.16.2114)
- Chedoe 2007, Drug Saf (NICU review, 11 studies): highest rate 5.5 errors per 100 prescriptions; dose errors the most common type — [PMID 17536876](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17536876/); [DOI 10.2165/00002018-200730060-00004](https://doi.org/10.2165/00002018-200730060-00004); Chedoe 2012 (Dutch NICU, direct observation): errors fell from 49% (151/311) to 31% (87/284) of preparations/administrations after a multifaceted educational intervention, OR 0.49 — [PMID 22491014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22491014/); [DOI 10.1136/fetalneonatal-2011-300989](https://doi.org/10.1136/fetalneonatal-2011-300989)
- Stavroudis 2010, J Perinatol (MEDMARX NICU reports 1999–2005): 6,749 reports from 163 facilities; administering errors ≈ half; high-alert medications, prescribing phase and device failures associated with harm — [PMID 20043010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20043010/); [DOI 10.1038/jp.2009.186](https://doi.org/10.1038/jp.2009.186)

- Henry Basil 2022, Drug Saf (NICU administration-error meta-analysis, 20 studies): pooled prevalence 59.3% (95% CI 35.4–81.3) of opportunities in direct-observation studies; targets wrong technique, wrong drug preparation, wrong time — [PMID 36192535](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36192535/); [DOI 10.1007/s40264-022-01236-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40264-022-01236-6); Henry Basil 2024, J Adv Nurs (5 Malaysian NICUs, direct observation): 743/1,093 doses (68.0%) had ≥1 error; wrong rate 21.2%, wrong drug preparation 17.9%, wrong dose 17.0% — [PMID 38803148](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38803148/); [DOI 10.1111/jan.16247](https://doi.org/10.1111/jan.16247)
- Schnock 2023, Drug Saf (US NICU, smart pumps, 905 IV administrations): 130 errors (14.4 per 100); most frequent "selecting the wrong drug library entry" 5.3 per 100, unauthorized medication 0.7, wrong dose 0.6; no harm identified — [PMID 37889401](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37889401/); [DOI 10.1007/s40264-023-01365-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40264-023-01365-6)

### Inferences
- In ICU, wrong-drug error is a smaller fraction than in anesthesia because most administrations are nurse-executed against a written/electronic order with pharmacy involvement; the dominant ICU failure modes are timing, omission, rate and dose. The ICU wrong-drug analogue is increasingly the "wrong drug-library entry" on smart pumps (Schnock 2023), a selection error with the same look-alike/menu mechanism as ADC overrides.
- Low adoption of ISO 26825 labels in ICUs (30% original form) means the anesthesia colour-code safety layer is largely absent where anesthetic drugs (propofol, NMBs, vasopressors) are infused in ICU.

### Gaps
- The exact "wrong drug" count in Valentin 2009 (SEE 2) is not in the abstract and the full text was not retrieved; only secondary summaries of the category list were found.
- No ICU study was located that reports a wrong-drug rate per administration from direct observation in 2010–2026; wrong-drug is subsumed in "administration errors."
- Rothschild 2005's medication-specific rate per 1000 patient-days (a secondary source cites 127.8 MICU / 131.5 CCU) was not verifiable from the abstract.

## KQ4. Emergency department and EMS/prehospital: wrong-drug frequency (incl. epinephrine 1:1,000 vs 1:10,000)

### Takeaway
In EDs, wrong drug accounts for roughly 11% of pharmacist-detected prescription errors (Stasiak 2014) and a small share of observed administration errors (which are ≈3–6% of administrations and dominated by wrong dose); in EMS, self-reported wrong-medication errors are ≈4% of paramedics' errors, whereas pediatric dosing errors run at 30–35% of administrations with epinephrine the worst (60.9%), driven partly by two concentrations (1:1,000 and 1:10,000) in protocols and by weight-estimation and calculation failures.

### Inferences
- ED wrong-drug errors are mostly prescribing-phase (and intercepted by pharmacists), while ED administration errors are dominated by wrong dose; BCMA reduces ED administration errors by ~75–80% (two before-after studies) and would be expected to act on wrong-drug administrations, though neither study reports the wrong-drug subset.
- In EMS, the "epinephrine concentration" problem manifests as massive overdoses (mean 808%) when 1:1,000 is used IV/IO, i.e. a wrong-concentration/wrong-product selection error rather than a classic drug swap; it responds to removing calculations (height-based tools, single-concentration stocking) more than to education.

### Gaps
- No EMS study reporting the rate of true drug swaps (wrong agent) per administration was found; Vilke's 4% is a share of self-reported errors. Case reports of EMS ketamine/rocuronium swaps were not retrieved with the searches used.
- Patanwala 2010's wrong-drug subset and Pham 2011's national ED MEDMARX error-type distribution were not obtainable from abstracts.
- Douglass 2017 Ann Emerg Med RCT on double-checking (PMID 28669554) was identified by title only; its effect size was not extracted.

## KQ5. Vaccines: wrong-vaccine/wrong-product administration errors (VAERS, ISMP VERP, UK, Australia, COVID-19 era, DTaP/Tdap, zoster)

### Takeaway
"Wrong vaccine administered" is the third-largest VAERS error group (3,372 of 21,843 errors, 15%, 2000–2013) and the single most common ISMP VERP error type (~23–25% of reports in 2016 and 2022–2023 analyses), with recurring look-alike/sound-alike pairs (DTaP/Tdap, varicella/zoster, PCV/PPSV, influenza/hepatitis B birth dose, GSK vs Pfizer RSV vaccine in pregnancy, and influenza/COVID-19 syringes in 2021); harm is usually absent (≥90% of wrong-vaccine reports without an adverse health event), but the errors leave patients unprotected and generate clusters.

- Wrong-vaccine errors are overwhelmingly selection errors between products with shared antigens, acronyms or first letters (DTaP/Tdap, Zostavax/Varivax, PCV/PPSV, HepB/influenza in newborns, Arexvy/Abrysvo), plus co-location of look-alike prefilled syringes (influenza/COVID-19). ## KQ6. Setting-specific prevention: colour-coded labels (ISO 26825), prefilled syringes, barcode-confirmed administration, standardized trays/templates, double checks, NMB segregation — measured effects on wrong-drug errors

### Takeaway
The only randomized clinical evidence is Merry 2011 (SAFERsleep multimodal system: 9.1 vs 11.6 errors per 100 administrations, mostly recording errors); before-after and registry data show barcode-based systems reduce vial-swap errors (Bowdle 2018) and overall anaesthesia error rates by roughly a third to a half, colour-coded compartmentalised trays improve error detection in experiments (91% vs 83%), colour-coded labels alone reduced ampoule swaps but not syringe swaps (Fasting 2000), and ED barcode administration cut administration errors ~75–80%; adoption remains the binding constraint (syringe-scan compliance 8.7–25% without incentives, ISO 26825 used in original form by only 30% of ICUs).

### Inferences
- Interventions map onto specific error pathways: prefilled syringes and vial-barcode labelling address ampoule/vial swaps (Fasting 2000, Bowdle 2018, Neo 2022 all show this selectivity); syringe-barcode scanning immediately before injection is the only measure shown to address syringe swaps at the point of administration (Merry 2011; Bowdle 2018 syringe-swap decline with the bundle), and it depends on compliance that was 9–25% without audit/feedback or incentives.
- Colour coding is necessary but insufficient: it reduces ampoule swaps and speeds visual search but did not eliminate syringe swaps between same-size syringes (Fasting 2000), and its benefit is degraded by theatre lighting (Janulevicius 2026) and low standard adoption (Balzer 2012).
- The strongest "forcing function" results come from design changes that remove a choice (ISO 80369 non-Luer neuraxial connectors; single-concentration stocking; height-based dosing tools: 90% reduction in >300% dose errors), consistent with Litman's "take away the human factor" argument.

### Gaps
- No RCT or controlled study isolates the effect of colour-coded labels, prefilled syringes, or NMB segregation on wrong-drug errors alone; evidence is before-after, simulation or bundled (Webster 2010, Bowdle 2018, Ribed 2025).
- Post-2011 clinical trials of barcode-confirmed anaesthetic administration with wrong-drug outcomes were not found; Bowdle 2018 is facilitated self-report.
- ISMP/ECRI and APSF primary documents (ADC guidelines, 2010 consensus recommendation list, Vaught analysis) were not accessible from this session; only secondary descriptions are cited.
