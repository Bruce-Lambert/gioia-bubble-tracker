Items are grouped by venue family. For each item: first author, year, journal, volume/pages, design, setting, sample, exact figure with denominator, PMID/DOI, URL, and verification status. ## Key question 1 — Nursing venues: wrong-drug administration studies, LASA knowledge/perception, student training, nurse-reported LASA incidents, "five rights"/double-check studies that break out wrong drug

### Takeaway
A PubMed sweep of 16 nursing journals returned only 17 hits for "look-alike / sound-alike / wrong drug / wrong medication" in 2006–2026; the nursing literature almost never isolates LASA as a study outcome. What exists is (a) observational medication-administration-error (MAE) studies that report a "wrong drug" sub-rate (typically 2–34% of errors, denominators below), (b) nurse-survey studies in which "drugs that look alike" or "similar packaging" rank as the top perceived causes of MAEs, and (c) a small set of nurse-focused experimental drug-name perception studies that mostly sit in Human Factors/Ergonomics rather than nursing journals (see Key question 2).

Nurse-focused LASA perception/experimental studies (nurse samples; published in HF/pharmacy venues)
- Mitobe J & Higuchi T, 2020/2022, *Hum Factors* 64(3):451-465 (DOI 10.1177/0018720820946607). Two choice-reaction-time experiments, nurses vs non-health-care participants, Japan; nurses showed difficulty discriminating target drug names from similar pseudo-drug names (attributed to top-down processing from drug-name knowledge); "finger-pointing and calling" had a marginally significant error-prevention effect for drug names. PMID 32830585. PubMed-verified. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32830585/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720820946607)
- Liu K et al., 2019, *Ergonomics* (DOI 10.1080/00140139.2019.1629637). Three computer-based experiments: 30 nursing students (Exp 1), 15 nurses (Exp 2 differentiation; Exp 3 recognition memory); disfluent (hard-to-read) format did not help and impaired differentiation in Exp 1; enhanced text and longer exposure improved differentiation accuracy but not recognition memory. PMID 31173543. PubMed-verified. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31173543/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/00140139.2019.1629637)
- Wang H & Or CKL, 2019/2020, *Hum Factors* 62(7):1102-1116 (DOI 10.1177/0018720819870700). Two four-way repeated-measures experiments, 40 nurses each (Hong Kong): pharmacy-shelf mock-up drug search and eye-tracked computer search; text enhancement (Tall Man) had no significant effect; identical name formats on prescription and package sped selection; visual cue improved search; verbal provocation increased reported mental effort. PMID 31465699. PubMed-verified. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31465699/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720819870700)
- Tsuji T et al., 2015, *J Pharm Health Care Sci* 1:19 (DOI 10.1186/s40780-015-0017-4). PMID 26819730. PubMed-verified. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26819730/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40780-015-0017-4)

### Inferences
- In nursing journals, "wrong drug" is almost always a sub-code inside a broader MAE taxonomy; the only within-nursing-journal experimental test of a LASA countermeasure found was Shawahna 2012 (Tall Man in handwritten transcription). The nurse-specific LASA perception literature lives in Human Factors/Ergonomics (Mitobe 2020; Wang & Or 2019; Liu 2019) and in BMJ QS (Lohmeyer 2022).
- Where nurses are asked for causes, look-alike names/packaging rank at or near the top (Henry Basil 2023; Alzoubi 2023; Qedan 2022), but incident data rarely confirm LASA as the dominant mechanism — consistent with the general literature's estimate that name confusion accounts for a minority (c. 6–15%) of error events.

### Gaps
- No nursing-journal study was found that reports a wrong-drug *administration* rate per opportunity with LASA attribution; Jessurun 2022, Cabilan 2017 and Jones 2010 need full-text review to extract wrong-drug counts.
- Nursing-student LASA training evaluations: none retrieved in *Nurse Educ Today* / *Nurse Educ Pract* by these terms; may exist under "medication safety education" without LASA keywords.
- *J Infus Nurs*, *Oncol Nurs Forum*, *J Pediatr Nurs*, *J Nurs Manag*, *Int J Nurs Stud*: no LASA-specific items found.

## Key question 2 — Informatics and human-factors venues: pick-list/autocomplete/juxtaposition errors, LASA alert design, EHR usability, barcode and smart-pump wrong-drug catches, AMIA/MEDINFO (Stud Health Technol Inform) items

### Takeaway
Beyond the core CPOE literature, the under-retrieved items are: (a) incident-database and e-prescription analyses quantifying "incorrect medication/drug" as a share of ordering errors (16.3% of 12,830 NPSD ordering errors; 18.5% of discontinued-in-error outpatient orders; 12.8% of ED serious events); (b) smart-pump and BCMA log studies giving wrong-drug-selection catch rates (3.45% of 290,807 pump library alerts; 5.3 wrong-library-entry errors per 100 NICU administrations); (c) a small human-factors experimental literature on drug-name perception (Filik 2006, Schell 2008, Darker 2011, Irwin 2013, DeHenau 2016, Wang & Or 2019, Liu 2019, Mitobe 2020) that is split on whether Tall Man helps; and (d) informatics papers on automated LASA detection using indications or text classification (Cheng 2018 JAMIA; Wong 2013/2014; Emmerton 2020; Chen 2023).

### Inferences
- Across HF experiments, Tall Man's benefit is task-dependent: positive in change-detection/syringe-array tasks (DeHenau 2016; Lohmeyer 2022), null in visual search among proximate similar names (Irwin 2013; Wang & Or 2019), and equivalent to all-caps in word-perception tasks (Darker 2011). Proximity/adjacency in alphabetical arrays is a consistently demonstrated risk (Irwin 2013) and matches incident-analysis themes (ISMP Canada 2012 bisoprolol/bisacodyl "proximity in menus/similar mnemonics").
- Smart-pump and BCMA log data give the only large-denominator "catch" figures for wrong-drug selection (Waterson 2020: 10,017 wrong-medication selections cancelled out of 290,807 alerts); BCMA reduces wrong-drug at administration but not wrong-drug in ward preparation (Sakushima 2015; Küng 2021 addresses preparation).

## Key question 4 — Verification task 1: Did WHO's High 5s project ever have a LASA SOP, and why was it not pursued?

### Takeaway
No. The High 5s project's five original problem areas (per WHO's own Interim Report, Dec 2013) were medication reconciliation, correct-site surgery, concentrated injectable medicines, communication during patient-care handovers, and health care-associated infections; look-alike/sound-alike medication names was never one of the five SOPs. LASA was instead WHO Patient Safety Solution No. 1 (May 2007) issued by the WHO Collaborating Centre for Patient Safety Solutions — a different (non-SOP) product line. Two of the five High 5s SOPs were dropped: handovers (2010; "heavily influenced by cultural and environmental issues that were not measurable and easy to standardize") and HAI (withdrawn because WHO and others were already standardising HAI procedures).

### Inferences
- Because Ro & Jones restrict to different-active-ingredient pairs and exclude same-ingredient strength/formulation mix-ups, their "wrong drug" denominator (450) is narrower than PA-PSRS's raw "Wrong Drug" subtype (1,192) — roughly 38% of raw wrong-drug reports met the WDE definition, a useful correction factor when comparing with ISMP/MEDMARX-style counts.
- The dominance of same-class/same-stem pairs (insulins, cephalosporins, catecholamines, opioids) supports the review's theme that stem-sharing INN nomenclature and therapeutic-class adjacency, not just orthographic similarity, drive wrong-drug events.
