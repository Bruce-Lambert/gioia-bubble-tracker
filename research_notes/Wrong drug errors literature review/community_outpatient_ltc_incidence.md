# Wrong-drug medication errors outside the acute hospital: incidence and prevalence (community pharmacy, mail-order/telepharmacy, ambulatory prescribing, long-term care, home/self-administration, paediatric outpatient, veterinary), 2006–2026 with landmark earlier work

Scope note for the writer: "wrong-drug" is reported under many labels (wrong drug / wrong medicine / incorrect drug / wrong product / selection error / drug confusion / LASA error). Denominators differ (prescriptions, dispensed items, doses/opportunities for error, incident reports, resident-months). Every figure below is given with its denominator and source as reported; where a study did not break out wrong-drug separately this is stated explicitly. Bibliographic data (PMIDs, DOIs, journal/volume/pages) were verified against PubMed records retrieved during this research; full texts were read for Knudsen 2007, Avery 2013 (PRACtICe), Nanji 2011, Gandhi 2005, Odukoya 2014, Franklin 2014, Barber 2009 (CHUMS), Szczepura 2011, Wallis 2019, the Pennsylvania 2017 outpatient-pharmacy advisory and the ISMP Canada Nova Scotia poster. Several hosts (pharmaceutical-journal.com, ismp-canada.org, saskpharm.ca, drugtopics.com) could not be fetched directly in this session; figures attributed to them come from search-result snippets and are flagged "secondary".

## Key question 1: Community pharmacy — what proportion of dispensing errors are wrong-drug, and what is the rate per prescription dispensed?

### Takeaway
Direct-observation studies put the all-cause dispensing error rate in community pharmacies at roughly 1.7%–3% of prescriptions in the US (Flynn/Barker) and 0.04%–0.4% of items in UK/Danish self-report studies, with wrong-drug ("wrong medicine"/"selection") errors making up roughly 14%–28% of reported community-pharmacy incidents in large incident-report datasets (Nova Scotia 14.2%; UK NRLS ~28%, NPA 24–33%; Denmark 20.1% of transcription-stage errors reaching patients) and about 20% of reported outpatient-pharmacy errors in Pennsylvania. The best per-prescription estimate for wrong-drug errors reaching patients in US community pharmacy is "roughly one per thousand prescriptions" (Lambert group, derived from chain PSO data and the Flynn/Barker observational work; Lambert 2005 translated this to ~3.9 million wrong-drug errors/year in the US).

### Inferences
- Observation-based all-cause rates (≈1.7–3% of prescriptions, Flynn/Barker) are an order of magnitude above self-report/incident-report rates (0.01–0.4% of items, Chua/Ashcroft/Knudsen), consistent with the "medication error iceberg". Wrong-drug errors are a minority of all dispensing deviations (most are labelling) but a consistent 14–28% of reported incidents and the modal content error in several datasets.
- Combining Flynn 2003 (1.7% all-cause), Lambert's "≈1 per 1,000 prescriptions wrong drug" and the 3.9 million/3 billion estimate implies roughly 0.1–0.13% of US prescriptions dispensed with the wrong drug, i.e. wrong-drug ≈ 6–8% of all observed dispensing deviations — but the explicit Flynn 2003 wrong-drug count was not retrieved, so this is an inference.
- Transcription/order-entry (product selection in the pharmacy system, including automated product-selection failures and LASA name confusion) is the dominant origin of wrong-drug errors reaching patients (Knudsen 64.9% transcription; Nova Scotia order entry most common; Cheung 41% of IT-related incidents = wrong medicine chosen).

### Gaps
- The exact number of wrong-drug errors among the 77 errors in Flynn 2003 could not be retrieved (full text not accessible); Lambert 2005's 3.9 million/year figure cites "recent observational studies" without a page-level derivation in the abstract.
- Ashcroft 2005 "selection errors" (60.3%) pool wrong drug, wrong strength and wrong form; the wrong-drug-only share was not in the abstract.
- No Australian community-pharmacy study reporting a wrong-drug proportion with a prescription denominator was found (QUMwatch does not break it out; PDL claims data not located).
- Chua 2003 and James 2011 rank wrong drug second after wrong strength but give no counts in abstracts.
- No publicly available Walgreens/CVS/Express Scripts dataset with per-prescription wrong-drug rates was located; the chain data used by Lambert's group are anonymised PSO data with error rates scaled by an undisclosed constant.

## Key question 2: Which look-alike/sound-alike pairs most often reach patients at community pharmacy, and what chain/insurer/regulator datasets exist?

### Takeaway
The best-documented community/outpatient pairs reaching patients are traMADol↔traZODone (the single most common wrong-drug pair in Pennsylvania outpatient-pharmacy reports), metoprolol tartrate↔succinate, within-class oral-contraceptive and insulin product mix-ups, hydroxyzine↔hydralazine and glyburide↔glipizide (used as exemplars in the chain PSO datasets), rosuvastatin↔rivaroxaban (UK NPA) and methotrexate↔metolazone and Brintellix↔Brilinta (ISMP community newsletter case reports). Two anonymised US national chain datasets (each >1,000 stores) held by an AHRQ-listed PSO have been used in the Lambert/Schroeder studies but are not public.

### Cited Findings
- Pennsylvania 2017 (Straka, Gaunt, Grissinger): among 205 wrong-drug reports from outpatient pharmacies, tramadol was named in 22 (10.7%) with 15 (68.2%) being tramadol/trazodone mix-ups; metoprolol 11 (5.4%), 8 of them tartrate vs succinate; different oral-contraceptive products 16 (7.8%); different insulins 15 (7.3%); the remaining 68.8% (141) involved ≥121 different medications; nearly half (48.3%, n=99) of reports did not name both drugs. Case narratives include trazodone-for-tramadol causing a fall, nystatin dispensed for triamcinolone, and Singulair/Zyrtec cross-labelled. [Advisory](https://patientsafety.pa.gov/ADVISORIES/Pages/201706_55.aspx). ### Inferences
- The pairs that dominate reports are high-volume generics with shared stems or adjacent shelf/alphabetical placement (tramadol/trazodone, hydroxyzine/hydralazine, glyburide/glipizide, rosuvastatin/rivaroxaban, metoprolol salts), not the novel brand pairs that dominate pre-approval name screening.
- Chain-level numerators and denominators exist (PSO holds them) and have been used in peer-reviewed work, but only relative/scaled rates are public.

### Gaps
- No public ISMP MERP tabulation giving the proportion of community/ambulatory reports that are wrong-drug was located; ISMP's community newsletter content is case-based.
- Walgreens, CVS, Express Scripts have not published wrong-drug rates; the PSO-held chain data are anonymised.
- State board of pharmacy datasets with wrong-drug proportions were not found (only the Canadian disciplinary review).

## Key question 3: Ambulatory/primary-care prescribing — wrong-drug rates in e-prescribing and paper prescribing

### Takeaway
Wrong-drug (incorrect drug) errors are a small fraction of ambulatory prescribing errors: 4.0% of prescribing errors (10/247) and 0.17% of prescription items (10/6,048) in the English PRACtICe case-note review; US outpatient studies (Gandhi 7.6% error rate; Nanji 11.7% of e-prescriptions; Kaushal 42.5→6.6 per 100) are dominated by omitted/incomplete information and dose/frequency errors and do not report a wrong-drug category. Drop-down/pick-list mis-selection is the recognised mechanism, documented qualitatively in UK general practice and US community pharmacies, and quantitatively in the Dutch CMR (41% of IT-related community-pharmacy incidents = wrong medicine chosen).

### Inferences
- Across UK and US ambulatory data, "incorrect drug" prescribing is ~4% of prescribing errors and ≈1–2 per 1,000 items; it is rarer than dispensing-stage wrong-drug errors but, when it occurs, is often not interceptable by the pharmacy (correct label for the wrong intended drug).
- E-prescribing eliminated illegibility and reduced total error rates ~7-fold in Kaushal 2010, but Nanji 2011 (11.7%) shows system-dependent residual error dominated by omissions; wrong-drug selection via pick-lists is recognised qualitatively but seldom quantified in outpatient studies.
- The prescriber-to-pharmacy product-matching step is a distinct wrong-drug hazard: 15–20% of e-prescriptions need manual product selection and ~1 in 9 flagged mismatches is unintended (Panich; Gong).

### Gaps
- Nanji 2011, Gandhi 2005, Kaushal 2010 and Abramson 2013 do not report a wrong-drug category; no US outpatient prescribing study with an explicit wrong-drug rate per prescription was found.
- Grossman (e-prescribing) studies were not retrieved with quantitative wrong-drug data.
- No 2020–2026 ambulatory prescribing study quantifying drop-down wrong-drug selection rates was found.

## Key question 4: Long-term care / nursing homes — proportion of medication errors that are wrong-drug

### Takeaway
In nursing homes wrong-drug errors are a small share of medication errors: 4% "unauthorized drug" in the Barker 2002 36-facility observation study (which included 12 skilled nursing facilities; 19% of doses in error), 6% "wrong product" in North Carolina's web-based reporting (Pierson 2007), and wrong product carried a 2.2-fold higher likelihood of being serious (Greene 2010). The Gurwitz cohorts show preventable ADEs arise mainly at ordering and monitoring, with dispensing/administration errors less common. UK care-home studies (CHUMS, Szczepura) report high all-cause error exposure (69.5%–90% of residents) but did not isolate wrong-drug; no MEDMARX LTC-specific wrong-drug analysis was found.

## Key question 5: Home / self-administration — patient-level drug confusion, pill appearance and generic switching

### Takeaway
Self-reported medication errors in the past year affect ~70–75% of community-dwelling, polymedicated older adults (Spain 75%; Iran 69%), with "similar appearance of the medication" named as a leading cause; wrong medicine ranks third among self-administration error types in a 2021 systematic review. Generic pill-appearance changes are common (29% of post-MI patients within a year; 51% of patients ≥50 report experiencing one) and 21% of patients who experienced a change believed they had received the wrong medication; appearance discordance raises the odds of non-persistence by 27–66%.

PMID 38640114 — [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0302177](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0302177).
- Sarpatwari, Gagne, Lu, Campbell, Carman, Enger, Dutcher, Jiang & Kesselheim 2019, J Gen Intern Med 34(3):420-428 (USA, 814 commercially insured adults on generic lisinopril, fluoxetine, lamotrigine or simvastatin who experienced a colour/shape change Mar 2014–Nov 2015; response 41%): 72% relied on pill appearance to ensure they took the correct medication; 86% wanted pharmacist notification but only 37% recalled it; 21% thought they received the wrong medication; 8% adjusted medication use. PMID 30632102 — [DOI 10.1007/s11606-018-4791-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-018-4791-1).
- Barenie, Kesselheim, Gagne et al. 2020, Am J Manag Care 26(8):340-347 (USA national surveys, 2016): of 1,000 patients ≥50 on generics, 51% had experienced pill appearance changes, 82% preferred notification, 36% recalled verbal/45% sticker notification; 12% of those experiencing a change stopped or reduced use; of 710 pharmacists, 47% reported appearance changes ≥6 times/month in their pharmacy. PMID 32835461 — [DOI 10.37765/ajmc.2020.44070](https://doi.org/10.37765/ajmc.2020.44070).
- Kesselheim, Misono, Shrank, Greene, Doherty, Avorn & Choudhry 2013, JAMA Intern Med 173(3):202-8 (USA, nested case-control, antiepileptic drugs): AEDs dispensed in 37 colours and 4 shapes; 11,472 non-persistence cases vs 50,050 controls; colour discordance preceded 1.20% of cases vs 0.97% of controls (aOR 1.27, 95% CI 1.04–1.55); shape discordance OR 1.47 (0.85–2.54); seizure-diagnosis subgroup OR 1.53. PMID 23277164 — [DOI 10.1001/2013.jamainternmed.997](https://doi.org/10.1001/2013.jamainternmed.997).
- Kesselheim, Bykov, Avorn, Tong, Doherty & Choudhry 2014, Ann Intern Med 161(2):96-103 (USA, post-MI patients 2006–2011): 29% (3,286/11,513) had a pill shape or colour change within a year; 4,573 non-persistence episodes vs 19,881 controls; odds of non-persistence +34% after colour change (aOR 1.34, 95% CI 1.12–1.59) and +66% after shape change (aOR 1.66, 1.43–1.94). PMID 25023248 — [DOI 10.7326/M13-2381](https://doi.org/10.7326/M13-2381).
- Toledo et al. 2026, Pharmaceuticals (Basel) 19(8):1148 (Spain, Murcia, survey of community-pharmacy staff on caregiver-related medication errors in older adults across urban/town/rural pharmacies) — error-type frequencies not available in abstract. PMID 42653646 — [DOI 10.3390/ph19081148](https://doi.org/10.3390/ph19081148).
- Roughead EE et al. 2004, Pharmacoepidemiol Drug Saf 13(2):83-7 (Australia, 1,000 community patients receiving medication management reviews): 2,222 problems; 90% had ≥1; "one in four were using the wrong or inappropriate medication" (clinical appropriateness, not LASA confusion). PMID 14998069 — [DOI 10.1002/pds.912](https://doi.org/10.1002/pds.912).

### Inferences
- Patients use appearance as a safety check; generic appearance changes therefore both mask true wrong-drug dispensing errors and generate false "wrong drug" perceptions (21%), and both pathways degrade adherence.
- No study measures the incidence of a patient actually taking the wrong drug at home with a dose denominator; the evidence is survey-based prevalence of self-reported errors.

### Gaps
- No observational study quantifying wrong-drug self-administration per dose/per patient-year in the community was found.
- Mira 2013 does not give the proportion of errors attributable to similar appearance.
- Studies on patient recognition of medication names (e.g., ability to name their drugs) were not retrieved within this search budget.

## Key question 6: Paediatric outpatient and caregiver errors

### Takeaway
Out-of-hospital paediatric medication errors are frequent (≈63,000 US poison-centre reports/year in children <6; 26.4 per 10,000 children), dominated by double-dosing and dose errors rather than wrong drug; in English general practice children <15 have 1.87-fold higher odds of a prescribing/monitoring error, and 9.8% of Pennsylvania outpatient-pharmacy error reports involved paediatric patients. A wrong-drug-specific paediatric outpatient rate was not found.

### Cited Findings
- Smith MD et al. 2014, Pediatrics 134(5):867-76 (USA, National Poison Data System 2002–2012): 696,937 out-of-hospital medication errors in children <6 (63,358/year; 1 child every 8 minutes); 26.42 per 10,000 population; 27.0% attributed to inadvertently taking/being given medication twice; analgesics 25.2%, cough/cold 24.6%; children <1 year 25.2% of episodes; 25 deaths. PMID 25332497 — [DOI 10.1542/peds.2014-0309](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2014-0309). Secondary summaries add 17.8% incorrect dose and 8.2% unit-of-measure confusion — [UMEM Educational Pearls](https://em.umaryland.edu/educational_pearls/2625/); wrong-medication share not confirmed.
- Avery et al. 2013 BJGP (PRACtICe): odds of prescribing/monitoring error higher for age <15 years (OR 1.87, 95% CI 1.19–2.94, p=0.006). [DOI 10.3399/bjgp13X670679](https://doi.org/10.3399/bjgp13X670679).
- Pennsylvania 2017 outpatient-pharmacy advisory: 9.8% (n=102) of 1,044 events involved paediatric patients (<18 years). [Advisory](https://patientsafety.pa.gov/ADVISORIES/Pages/201706_55.aspx).
- Sabblah, van Hunsel, Taxis, Duwiejua, Seaneke & van Puijenbroek 2024, Ther Adv Drug Saf 15 (Ghana, 95 children discharged from a paediatric department, home interviews): 65 (68.4%) children had ≥1 medication error; 102/232 medications (44.0%; 95% CI 37.6–50.4) had an error; wrong time 45.1%, wrong frequency 21.6%; 12.8% serious potential harm. PMID 38293565 — [DOI 10.1177/20420986231225850](https://doi.org/10.1177/20420986231225850).
- Ghaleb et al. 2005, Qual Saf Health Care (UK Delphi definition of paediatric prescribing error) — definitional reference only. [DOI 10.1136/qshc.2005.013797](https://doi.org/10.1136/qshc.2005.013797).

### Inferences
- Paediatric outpatient risk is dosing-driven (weight-based doses, liquid measurement); wrong-drug events in children are documented anecdotally (e.g., methotrexate entered as 10 tablets in PA narratives) but not quantified.

### Gaps
- Kaushal 2007 (paediatric outpatient ADEs), Zandieh 2008 and Rinke paediatric community pharmacy work were not retrieved by the searches run; no paediatric community-pharmacy wrong-drug dispensing rate was found.
- Smith 2014 full text (with any "wrong medication" category) was not accessible.

## Key question 7: Veterinary medicine — wrong-drug error data

### Takeaway
In the largest veterinary incident-report series (Wallis 2019, 560 reports from three US hospitals, 5.3 incidents per 1,000 visits), drug errors were 55–69% of all reports and wrong drug accounted for 18.0% (62/344) of drug errors (wrong dose 57.8%), with wrong drug more frequent in the large-animal teaching hospital (34.5%) than in private small-animal practice (11.2%). European and Australian series confirm medication incidents as the most common category (40% of 2,155 reports; 19.2% of 68 M&M cases) but do not isolate wrong drug.

### Cited Findings
- Wallis J et al. 2019, Front Vet Sci 6:12 (USA; Cornell small-animal and large-animal teaching hospitals Feb 2015–Mar 2018 and a private small-animal referral/emergency hospital Jan 2016–Mar 2018): 651 reports, 560 analysed; 105,948 patient visits → 5.3 incidents per 1,000 visits (SAU 3.8; LAU 6.5; SAP 8.3); drug errors most frequent in all three (55–69% of errors); by drug-error type: wrong dose 199 (57.8%), WRONG DRUG 62 (18.0%) [SAU 37 (21.4%), LAU 10 (34.5%), SAP 15 (11.2%)], wrong time 42 (12.2%), wrong route 20 (5.8%), wrong patient 13 (3.8%); 45% harmless hits, 15.2% adverse incidents (85), 4 deaths and 3 permanent harm. PMID 30805349 — [DOI 10.3389/fvets.2019.00012](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2019.00012).
- Oxtoby, Ferguson, White & Mossop 2015, Vet Rec 177(17):438 (UK): causes from 678 claims to the Veterinary Defence Society and 9 focus groups; types from 2,978 claims 2009–2013; surgery-related mistakes most common type; cognitive limitations the main cause. PMID 26489997 — [DOI 10.1136/vr.103331](https://doi.org/10.1136/vr.103331). (Medication-error share not in abstract.)
- Schortz, Mossop, Bergström & Oxtoby 2022, Vet Rec 191(2):e1629 (mainland Europe, 130 small-animal practices, voluntary reporting): 2,155 incident reports; medication-related incidents most frequent (40%); >40% of incidents caused harm; anaesthesia incidents most severe (18% death). PMID 35413131 — [DOI 10.1002/vetr.1629](https://doi.org/10.1002/vetr.1629). A secondary blog attributes "66% medication-related, 51% incorrect dosing" to veterinary reporting data but this could not be tied to the paper — [pharmacist.com blog](https://www.pharmacist.com/Blogs/CEO-Blog/researchers-identify-high-risk-medications-and-errors-in-veterinary-care).
- Giles, Boland, Kirkwood, Ward & Quain 2025, Aust Vet J 103(5):267-275 (Australia, small-animal teaching hospital M&M meetings 2018–2023): 68 cases; oversight 26 (25.0%), drug 20 (19.2%), iatrogenic 17, staff 17; 52.9% temporary harm; 20.6% death/euthanasia/permanent harm; classification adapted from Wallis 2019. PMID 39873416 — [DOI 10.1111/avj.13426](https://doi.org/10.1111/avj.13426).
- Oxtoby & Mossop 2019, Vet Rec 184(16):501 (UK, 6 focus groups): barriers to reporting errors; informed the VDS "VetSafe" reporting tool. PMID 30837292 — [DOI 10.1136/vr.105137](https://doi.org/10.1136/vr.105137).

### Inferences
- Veterinary wrong-drug proportions (≈18% of drug errors) are similar in magnitude to human community-pharmacy incident-report shares (14–28%), but the denominators are hospital visits and voluntary reports, not dispensed items; veterinary dispensing-specific (practice pharmacy) wrong-drug rates do not exist.

### Gaps
- No veterinary study with a per-prescription or per-dose wrong-drug denominator; no data from veterinary community/online pharmacies.
- Oxtoby 2015's medication-error proportion of claims was not retrievable.

## Key question 8: 2020–2026 data, mail-order and telepharmacy

### Takeaway
Mail-order evidence remains thin and old: a highly automated US mail-service pharmacy reported 0.075% all-cause dispensing errors (16/21,252), 14 of them label-direction errors and none from the mechanical fill (Teagarden 2005); Medco's Six Sigma program reported 33% and 69% relative reductions in wrong-drug-selection and SALA nonconformances (Castle 2005); Pennsylvania data show home-delivery events over-represented among wrong-patient errors (37% of delivery events). Telepharmacy QRE rates (1.3% remote vs 0.8% comparison; 1.34% vs 1.43%) are comparable to traditional pharmacies, without a wrong-drug breakdown. 2020–2026 contributions are mainly new national observation studies (UAE, Jordan), e-prescription product-matching studies (Panich 2021; Gong 2025), a Japanese 45,483-report near-miss analysis, a 2025 systematic review, and the Lambert 2025 chain-data validation.

### Cited Findings
- Teagarden, Nagle, Aubert, Wasdyke, Courtney & Epstein 2005, Pharmacotherapy 25(11):1629-35 (USA, Medco mail-service network, Sep–Oct 2003): 16 dispensing errors among 21,252 prescriptions = 0.075% (95% CI 0.043–0.122%); 14 involved incomplete/incorrect label directions; all errors in initial processing/order entry; "no errors were associated with the mechanical stages of product dispensing"; authors are Medco employees. PMID 16232024 — [DOI 10.1592/phco.2005.25.11.1629](https://doi.org/10.1592/phco.2005.25.11.1629).
- Castle et al. 2005 (Medco home-delivery Six Sigma): wrong-drug selection external nonconformances reduced 33%, wrong directions 49%, SALA errors 69%. [DOI 10.1016/s1553-7250(05)31041-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/s1553-7250(05)31041-5).
- Pennsylvania 2017: 5.9% (62/1,044) of outpatient-pharmacy events involved delivery to the patient's home/other location; 37.1% (23/62) of delivery events were wrong-patient errors; ISMP study cited: a correctly filled prescription given to the wrong patient at point of sale ≈1 per 1,000 prescriptions; a fatal lomustine case from a mail-order pharmacy that shipped three cycles without counselling. [Advisory](https://patientsafety.pa.gov/ADVISORIES/Pages/201706_55.aspx).
- Friesner, Scott, Rathke, Peterson & Anderson 2011, J Am Pharm Assoc 51(5):580-90 (USA, North Dakota Telepharmacy Project, Jan 2005–Sep 2008, 45 months): 14 remote telepharmacies 47,078 prescriptions, 631 QREs (1.3%; 553 near misses, 78 errors reaching patients) vs 8 standard pharmacies 123,346 prescriptions, 1,002 QREs (0.8%; 887 near misses, 125 errors); overall 1.0%; compared with national 1.7% for 50 pharmacies. PMID 21896455 — [DOI 10.1331/JAPhA.2011.10115](https://doi.org/10.1331/JAPhA.2011.10115).
- Scott, Friesner, Rathke, Peterson & Anderson 2012, J Am Pharm Assoc 52(5):e97-e104 (USA, 14 remote + 10 central telepharmacy sites, 45 months): remote 47,078 prescriptions, QRE rate 1.34% vs central 62,480 prescriptions, 1.43%; remote QREs more often caught at final pharmacist check (58.2% vs 40.8%) and less often by the patient (0.17% vs 0.28%); remote sites more incorrect-directions entries (18.9% vs 13.4%). PMID 23023864 — [DOI 10.1331/JAPhA.2012.11119](https://doi.org/10.1331/JAPhA.2012.11119).
- Scott, Friesner, Rathke & Doherty-Johnsen 2014, Am J Health Syst Pharm 71(1):58-67 (USA, 17 critical-access hospitals, telepharmacy order review — hospital setting, cross-reference only): transcription errors 43.3% of 5,500+ interventions. PMID 24352183 — [DOI 10.2146/ajhp120533](https://doi.org/10.2146/ajhp120533).
- 2020–2026 community/outpatient studies with wrong-drug data (detailed in Q1/Q3): Ibrahim 2020 UAE ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.18549/PharmPract.2020.4.2111)); Abdel-Qader 2021 Jordan ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11096-020-01126-w)); Panich 2021 JAMIA ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/jamia/ocaa259)); Gong 2025 BMJ HCI ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjhci-2025-101561)); Lambert 2025 BMJ Qual Saf ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2024-017688)); Eshraghi 2025 review ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.4103/jrpp.jrpp_40_25)); Umino 2026 Japan ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11096-026-02208-x)); Zimonjić 2025 Serbia ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11096-024-01807-w)); Whitaker 2024 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/PTS.0000000000001244)).
- AlRasheed et al. 2026, Healthcare (Basel) 14(10):1346 (Jordan, telemedicine-enabled dispensing/delivery regulated 2025; 350 community pharmacists): 88.9% rated medication errors a major patient-safety concern; only 28.3% willing to participate. PMID 42194438 — [DOI 10.3390/healthcare14101346](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare14101346).
- Ilkic et al. 2024, J Am Pharm Assoc 64(4):102081 (Serbia, FMEA of digital pharmaceutical care incl. telepharmacy): 42 risks, 8 critical; inadequate/incomplete data entry among critical risks. PMID 38579967 — [DOI 10.1016/j.japh.2024.102081](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.japh.2024.102081).
- Ab Rahman et al. 2025, J Pharm Policy Pract 18:2528244 (Malaysia national MERS 2018–2022, hospitals and clinics): 265,194 medication errors; 12,049 (4.5%) transcribing errors, mostly dose errors (48.6% incorrect dose). PMID 40672148 — [DOI 10.1080/20523211.2025.2528244](https://doi.org/10.1080/20523211.2025.2528244).

### Inferences
- Automated mail-order fill shifts residual error to order-entry/label stages; wrong-drug content errors appear rare per prescription (0/21,252 in the only published audit) but wrong-patient/delivery mix-ups and lack of counselling are the characteristic mail-order wrong-drug pathway.
- No published mail-order or telepharmacy dataset from 2020–2026 with a wrong-drug rate was found; evidence for these channels is pre-2015.

### Gaps
- No 2020–2026 mail-order pharmacy dispensing-accuracy study located (PubMed and web searches); PBM/mail-order (Express Scripts, CVS Caremark, OptumRx) internal data are unpublished.
- Telepharmacy studies (2011–2012) do not break QREs down into wrong drug.
- UK NPA/MSO 2023–2025 quarterly wrong-drug percentages were not retrievable (site not fetchable); latest confirmed NPA figures are 2017–2019.
