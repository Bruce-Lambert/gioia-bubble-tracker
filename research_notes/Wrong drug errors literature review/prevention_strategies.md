# Prevention Strategies for Wrong-Drug Errors and the Evidence for Their Effectiveness (2006–2026, with landmark earlier work)

Throughout, LAB = laboratory/simulation evidence; RW = real-world error-rate evidence; REC = expert/regulatory recommendation without measured effect. ## 1. Tall Man (mixed-case) lettering

### Takeaway
Tall Man lettering (TML) is universally recommended (FDA Name Differentiation Project 2001, ISMP list, ACSQHC national list, WHO) but the empirical base is almost entirely laboratory perception tasks with mixed results, and the single large real-world evaluation (Zhong 2016, 1.68 million paediatric hospitalisations) found no detectable change in LASA error rates after the 2007 ISMP/FDA recommendation. Current verdict (Lambert 2016; Bryan 2020; Bhat 2026): modest, context-dependent benefit on visual differentiation in the lab; "marginally effective" at best; no convincing evidence it reduces real-world wrong-drug errors; a definitive trial is still lacking.

### Cited Findings

**Who recommends it / policy history**
- The FDA Office of Generic Drugs launched the "Name Differentiation Project" in 2001, identifying look-alike generic name pairs and sending letters to manufacturers encouraging revised labelling with Tall Man letters, "to minimize medication errors resulting from look-alike confusion" — [Wiklund et al. 2019, Designing for Safe Use (chapter "Use TALLman lettering")](https://consensus.app/papers/details/9c5a8f6c97705b8fb1d6c2c7a70d0d96/?utm_source=claude_desktop); the same history (Cohen 1999 proposal → FDA 2001 project) is given in [Filik et al., Proceedings of EuroCogSci](https://consensus.app/papers/details/55cb23f7e10b508086f7f7ca8313508c/?utm_source=claude_desktop).
- ISMP and FDA disseminate widely used lists of confused name pairs with recommended TML; Karet (2023, Ther Innov Regul Sci) analysed these generic-generic pairs with FDA's POCA software: mean POCA score 69.5 (±9.7), range 46–90; pairs shared a mean 2.5 identical prefix letters and 3.2 identical suffix letters; each listed name averaged 11.2 (±9.1) high-similarity conflicts with other marketed names — "more than for candidate names USAN accepts" — [Karet 2023, DOI 10.1007/s43441-023-00526-0, PMID 37171707](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43441-023-00526-0).
- ISMP's most recent list revision (ISMP Medication Safety Alert! Acute Care, 26 Jan 2023) was based on reported events plus a survey (Oct–Dec 2022) of 298 practitioners: 95% agreed industry use of TML on labels helps prevent errors; 87% could recall an instance where TML personally prevented them from making a wrong-drug error; 94% said their facility uses TML; 18% apply TML to names not on the FDA/ISMP lists. ISMP has acknowledged that "overuse of tall man lettering may reduce effectiveness since names no longer appear novel", and ISMP is participating in a four-year Northwestern University project on comparative effectiveness of text enhancements — [AHRQ PSNet summary of ISMP 2023 update](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/ismp-updates-its-list-drug-names-tall-man-mixed-case-letters-based-survey-results); [ECRI/ISMP news item](https://home.ecri.org/blogs/ecri-news/ismp-adds-seven-name-pairs-to-list-of-drug-names-with-tall-man-mixed-case-letters). (Survey = perception, not error rates.)
- Australia: the ACSQHC National Tall Man Lettering list was derived systematically — 250 confusable Australian pairs (341 names) compiled from literature and error reports; a risk matrix (four-component likelihood score × severity ratings by 31 expert reviewers) identified 35 "extreme risk" pairs; the "mid-type" Tall Man convention was applied — [Emmerton et al. 2015, J Eval Clin Pract, DOI 10.1111/jep.12247, PMID 25327501](https://doi.org/10.1111/jep.12247). The ACSQHC's own commissioned evaluation states its data "supports a standard format, but the results do not establish that the proposed application of Tall Man lettering works on its own" and that academic evidence "has been varied and inconclusive"; the list was renamed the National Mixed-Case Lettering List in 2020 and compliance is tied to the NSQHS Medication Safety Standard; the Commission warns that overuse may reduce effectiveness — [ACSQHC, Evaluating the Effect of the Australian List of Tall Man Names](https://www.safetyandquality.gov.au/sites/default/files/migrated/Evaluating-the-Effect-of-the-Australian-List-of-Tall-Man-Names.pdf); [ACSQHC National Mixed-Case Lettering List](https://www.safetyandquality.gov.au/resources/national-mixed-case-lettering-list).
- The Joint Commission requires organisations to maintain and annually review a LASA list and act to prevent interchange (originally NPSG.03.03.01, moved to Medication Management standard MM.01.02.01 around 2010; TJC no longer publishes its own list) — see Section 7 for sources. TJC does not mandate TML specifically.

**Laboratory / simulation evidence (chronological)**
- Filik 2004 (Soc Sci Med; eye-tracking RCT, visual search among product packs): participants made fewer errors (less likely to pick a similar distractor) when names carried Tall Man letters — [DOI 10.1016/j.socscimed.2004.04.008, PMID 15474212](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2004.04.008).
- Filik 2006 (Hum Factors; 3 experiments, same/different judgement and recognition memory): TML made similar names easier to distinguish "if participants are aware that this is the purpose of the intervention"; TML and/or colour did not make names less confusable in memory, though TML may increase attention — [DOI 10.1518/001872006776412199, PMID 16696255](https://doi.org/10.1518/001872006776412199).
- Gabriele 2006 (Healthc Q; design thesis, 11 acute-care nurses, word-recognition tasks on label prototypes): exploratory only; "limited in sample size" — [PMID 17087175](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17087175/).
- Schell 2008 (Appl Ergon; 102 students between-subjects + 11 pharmacists/technicians within-subjects, sequential recognition): high-similarity mismatches missed more often regardless of text enhancement; "case-based enhancements also increased errors of commission (false alarms) significantly" — [DOI 10.1016/j.apergo.2008.01.010, PMID 18329629](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2008.01.010).
- Filik 2010 (Drug Saf; Exp 1 younger/older adults same/different task, Glasgow 2008; Exp 2 healthcare practitioners e-prescribing task, England 2008–09): fewer confusion errors with TML in both groups; response times suggested TML drew attention but readers did not rely solely on it — [DOI 10.2165/11532360-000000000-00000, PMID 20635826](https://doi.org/10.2165/11532360-000000000-00000).
- Darker 2011 (Ergonomics; 133 healthcare professionals, Reicher-Wheeler task): TML improved perception accuracy vs lowercase, "however, an equivalent improvement in accuracy was obtained using entirely uppercase text", so character size may be the key determinant — [DOI 10.1080/00140139.2010.535022, PMID 21181586](https://doi.org/10.1080/00140139.2010.535022).
- Irwin 2013 (Hum Factors; RCT, 60 non-pharmacists + 28 pharmacy staff selecting from mock packets on screen): proximity of multiple similar names and time pressure degraded accuracy/RT; "There was no effect of Tall Man lettering" — [DOI 10.1177/0018720812457565, PMID 23691822](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720812457565).
- Or & Wang 2014 (Drug Saf; 40 engineering students + 40 student pharmacists, 336 pairs): all five text enhancements improved accuracy vs lowercase; boldface+TML and red colour significantly outperformed TML alone; "Tall Man lettering may not be the most effective choice" — [DOI 10.1007/s40264-014-0156-9, PMID 24718712](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40264-014-0156-9). Companion study (Work 2014; n=60, 120 pairs): enhancements shortened RT but "did not have impact on proportion of correct responses" — [DOI 10.3233/WOR-131809, PMID 24346271](https://doi.org/10.3233/WOR-131809).
- DeHenau 2016 (Appl Ergon; change-detection task, healthcare providers and laypeople): changes in confusable pairs detected more often (P<0.0001) and faster (P<0.05) with TML, benefit larger for providers; "familiarity with both drug names in a confusable pair mitigated the benefit" — [DOI 10.1016/j.apergo.2015.06.009, PMID 26360197](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2015.06.009).
- Liu 2019 (Ergonomics; 30 nursing students, 15 nurses): enhanced text and longer exposure improved visual differentiation but not recognition memory; disfluent formats impaired differentiation — [DOI 10.1080/00140139.2019.1629637](https://doi.org/10.1080/00140139.2019.1629637).
- Wang 2020 (Hum Factors; 40 nurses in a simulated pharmacy shelf + 40 nurses eye-tracked): "Text enhancement had no significant effect on the drug search"; identical name format on prescription and package, and a visual cue directing attention, improved search — [DOI 10.1177/0018720819870700](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720819870700).
- Lohmeyer 2022/2023 (BMJ Qual Saf; prospective randomised in-situ simulation, 30 ICU nurses, University Hospital Zurich, eye tracking, syringe-label selection): error rate fell from 5.3% (8/150) to 0.7% (1/150) with TML-coded labels (p<0.05); longer dwell time and more fixations on the name; effects stronger with TML in mid-to-end position — [DOI 10.1136/bmjqs-2021-014438](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2021-014438).
- Luo 2023 (AHFE proceedings; 26 outpatient pharmacists, eye-tracking): identification accuracy increased with TML; RT and fixations decreased with TML and symbol prefixing — [DOI 10.54941/ahfe1003450](https://consensus.app/papers/details/c5820709d8565295a8ce72c955867901/?utm_source=claude_desktop).

**Real-world evidence**
- Zhong, Feinstein, Patel, Dai, Feudtner 2016 (BMJ Qual Saf 25:233-40; retrospective time series, Pediatric Health Information System pharmacy data from 42 US children's hospitals, 2004–2012, 1,676,700 hospitalisations; 8 prespecified potential LASA error patterns for 12 paediatric-relevant pairs; segmented regression before/after 2007 TML implementation): "no statistically significant change was detected for either the intercept or the slope of LA-SA error rate for each of the 11 drug pairs or for the combined error rate"; moving-average sensitivity analysis showed no downward trend from 2004 onward — [DOI 10.1136/bmjqs-2015-004562, PMID 26534995](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2015-004562).
- Lambert, Schroeder, Galanter 2016 (BMJ Qual Saf 25:213-7, editorial accompanying Zhong): title "Does Tall Man lettering prevent drug name confusion errors? Incomplete and conflicting evidence suggest need for definitive study"; per secondary summaries, it argues the null result may reflect non-implementation (TML was never required by any regulator) and that TML addresses only visual-perception errors, not memory or auditory confusions — [DOI 10.1136/bmjqs-2015-004929, PMID 26700541](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2015-004929); summary via [patientsafetysolutions.com](https://www.patientsafetysolutions.com/docs/December_1_2015_TALLman_Lettering_Does_It_Work.htm). (Full text not retrievable from this session.)
- Trbovich & Easty 2016 (BMJ Qual Saf editorial): "Results from a study of a postmarket strategy for preventing drug name errors (ie, Tallman lettering) with look-alike, sound-alike (LASA) drug names did not demonstrate effectiveness in reducing medication errors" — [DOI 10.1136/bmjqs-2016-005629](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2016-005629).
- Tseng 2016 (Eur J Hosp Pharm; single Taiwanese hospital, before–after of near-miss dispensing errors, 2013 vs 2014, multi-component "preventive strategies including the introduction of tall man letters"): look-alike names were the main cause of wrong-medicine dispensing before (134/202, 66.3%); after, the proportion attributable to name confusion fell to 77/140 (55.0%, p=0.004); near-miss frequency correlated with name similarity (p<0.01). Uncontrolled, multi-component, outcome is a proportion — [DOI 10.1136/ejhpharm-2016-001019](https://doi.org/10.1136/ejhpharm-2016-001019).
- Poveda-Andrés 2022 (J Clin Pharm Ther; 1000-bed Spanish hospital, 2019–2021): implementation report — TML authorised in 13/13 information systems, 210 names modified (168 active ingredients, 42 brands); no error-rate outcome — [DOI 10.1111/jcpt.13703](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpt.13703).
- Galanter 2014 (PLoS One) notes in its introduction that TML "has had mixed success in lab experiments, but there is no published evidence of its real-world impact" and that "only barcoding is strongly evidence-based" among LASA risk-reduction strategies — [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0101977, PMC4098994](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0101977).

**Systematic reviews**
- Larmené-Beld 2018 (Eur J Clin Pharmacol; 16 studies, 13 lab + 3 healthcare settings; 11 evaluated TML): 6 studies found lower error rates with TML, 1 found significantly higher; RT effects varied; "A study in the hospital setting did not show an effect on the potential look-alike sound-alike error rate by introducing Tall Man lettering" [= Zhong]; "Color-coding had no effect on the prevention of syringe-swaps in one study" — [DOI 10.1007/s00228-018-2471-z, PMID 29754215, PMC6061459](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00228-018-2471-z).
- Bryan, Aronson, ten Hacken, Williams, Jordan 2020 (Br J Clin Pharmacol; PROSPERO CRD42016048198; searched to Dec 2016, re-run Feb 2020; RCTs/CBA/ITS): 6 studies, all laboratory; TML the only intervention tested in RCTs, "limited efficacy and no consensus"; conclusion: TML "is a marginally effective intervention to reduce LASA errors, with a number of caveats"; proposes a "quasi-placebo effect" where users benefit more if aware of TML's purpose — [DOI 10.1111/bcp.14644, PMID 33197079, PMC9328434](https://doi.org/10.1111/bcp.14644).
- Bhat et al. 2026 (Human Factors in Healthcare; 16 studies/25 experiments): "mixed results… some studies demonstrating significant improvements, whereas others report no effect or even negative outcomes"; moderators: participant group, drug familiarity, awareness of purpose, target presence, exposure time, orthographic similarity; "not effective as a standalone solution" — [DOI 10.1016/j.hfh.2026.100123](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hfh.2026.100123).
- Ostini 2012 (Int J Pharm Pract; 32 publications on LASA): "very little intervention research… most recommendations… are the result of expert deliberations and not experimental research" — [DOI 10.1111/j.2042-7174.2012.00210.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2042-7174.2012.00210.x).

### Inferences
- The lab literature splits by task: TML tends to help in same/different discrimination and change detection, but not in visual search on shelves (Irwin 2013; Wang 2020), recognition memory (Filik 2006; Liu 2019) or when all-caps is the comparator (Darker 2011). Real-world wrong-drug errors arise from memory, auditory and pick-list mechanisms as much as from visual perception, which is consistent with Zhong's null result.
- The strongest positive result in a clinical-looking setting (Lohmeyer 2022) is still a simulation with only 9 total errors; it should not be read as real-world effectiveness.
- Regulators' and ISMP's current stance (judicious use; avoid overuse) is itself an acknowledgement that the effect depends on novelty/awareness, which erodes with widespread application.

### Gaps
- No randomised or controlled real-world trial of TML exists (the "definitive study" called for in 2016 has not appeared in PubMed through 2026). Results of the ISMP/Northwestern comparative-effectiveness project were not found.
- Full text of the Lambert 2016 editorial and Zhong 2016 could not be fetched; effect-size details beyond the abstract are not recorded.
- No evidence was found on TML in electronic prescribing pick-lists specifically (as opposed to labels), despite most CPOE wrong-drug errors occurring at selection.

## 2. Barcode medication administration (BCMA) and pharmacy barcode verification

### Takeaway
Barcode verification is the intervention with the strongest real-world evidence for reducing wrong-drug errors at dispensing and administration: Poon's Brigham studies showed an 85% reduction in targeted dispensing errors and a 41% reduction in non-timing administration errors (51% reduction in potential ADEs), and several later before–after studies report wrong-drug/wrong-medication errors falling by 60–100%. Effects are heterogeneous (ICU and some unit types show no change), depend on scan compliance, and are undermined by workarounds (Koppel 2008: 15 workaround types, 31 causes) and unreadable/incorrect barcodes.

### Cited Findings

**Pharmacy dispensing**
- Poon et al. 2005/2006 (AMIA Annu Symp Proc 2005; full paper Ann Intern Med 2006, PMID 16983130; direct-observation pre–post, tertiary academic hospital pharmacy): barcode technology "significantly reduced the rate of target dispensing errors leaving the pharmacy by 85%, from 0.37% to 0.06%"; potential ADEs from dispensing errors fell 63% (0.19%→0.069%); projected ~13,000 dispensing errors and 6,000 potential ADEs prevented per year in a 735-bed hospital dispensing 6 million doses — [PMC1560475, PMID 16779372](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16779372/); Ann Intern Med version [PMID 16983130](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16983130/).
- Leung 2015 (J Patient Saf; evidence review for a safe-practice standard): "barcode-assisted dispensing systems are associated with 93% to 96% reductions in dispensing errors, and 85% reductions in potential adverse drug events in dispensing"; BCMA integrated with eMAR associated with "complete elimination of transcription errors" and administration error reductions "by up to 80%" — [DOI 10.1097/PTS.0000000000000049, PMID 24618650](https://doi.org/10.1097/PTS.0000000000000049).
- Helmons 2012 (Am J Health Syst Pharm; 386-bed academic centre; wholesaler-to-ADC direct refill with barcode-assisted refilling, 26 ADCs): refill errors (wrong drug/strength/form in pocket) fell 77%, from 62/6829 pockets (0.91%) to 8/3855 (0.21%), p<0.0001; 70% of pre-intervention wrong medications were in multiple-drug drawers — [DOI 10.2146/ajhp110503, PMID 22997119](https://doi.org/10.2146/ajhp110503).
- Denny 2026 (Am J Health Syst Pharm; QI pre–post, academic centre oral-liquid repackaging; 108,611 vs 63,684 doses): wrong-drug errors fell from 160 to 5 events (P<0.01), rate from a peak of 2.97 to 0 per 1,000 syringes; wrong-volume 294→55; wrong BUD 281→67 — [DOI 10.1093/ajhp/zxaf342, PMID 41381217](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajhp/zxaf342).
- Küng 2021 (Int J Qual Health Care; quasi-experimental pre–post, two medical/surgical units, tertiary teaching hospital, 5,932 selection/dosing instances in 79 preparation rounds; barcode-assisted preparation linked to eMAR): preparation errors 9.9%→4.5% (RRR 54.5%, P<0.001); adjusted total effect borderline (OR 0.64, P=0.051), but "significant error reductions were found in wrong medication errors (OR 0.38, P<0.010) and wrong dosage errors (OR 0.12, P=0.004)" — [DOI 10.1093/intqhc/mzab043, PMID 33783482](https://doi.org/10.1093/intqhc/mzab043).

**Administration (BCMA-eMAR)**
- Poon et al. 2010 (N Engl J Med; before–after quasi-experimental, academic medical centre, 14,041 observed administrations, 3,082 transcriptions): non-timing administration errors 11.5%→6.8% (41.4% relative reduction, P<0.001); potential ADEs (excluding timing) 3.1%→1.6% (50.8% reduction, P<0.001); transcription errors eliminated — [DOI 10.1056/NEJMsa0907115, PMID 20445181](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMsa0907115). (Abstract does not break out wrong-drug errors.)
- Paoletti 2007 (Am J Health Syst Pharm; Lancaster General; direct observation, control unit + 2 intervention units; eMAR+BCMA 2003–04): accuracy rose from 86.5% to 97%; "a 54% reduction of medication administration errors" — [DOI 10.2146/ajhp060140, PMID 17322168](https://doi.org/10.2146/ajhp060140).
- DeYoung 2009 (Am J Health Syst Pharm; adult medical ICU, direct observation 775 pre/690 post administrations): error rate 19.7%→8.7% (56% reduction, p<0.001) but "related to a reduction associated with errors of wrong administration time… There were no significant differences in other error types" (wrong drug included in categories observed) — [DOI 10.2146/ajhp080355, PMID 19498127](https://doi.org/10.2146/ajhp080355).
- Helmons 2009 (Am J Health Syst Pharm; prospective before–after, 2 med-surg units + 2 ICUs, 386-bed teaching hospital): excluding wrong-time errors, errors fell 58% in med-surg units; in ICUs "total medication errors and wrong-time errors did not change"; more nurse distractions observed post-BCMA — [DOI 10.2146/ajhp080357, PMID 19535659](https://doi.org/10.2146/ajhp080357).
- Seibert 2014 (Am J Health Syst Pharm; pretest–posttest nonequivalent groups, two community hospitals incl. ICU, ED, oncology): overall accuracy 89%→90% (p=0.0015) at hospital 1; excluding wrong-time, 92%→96% (p=0.000008) and 93%→96% (p=0.015) at hospital 2; "did not introduce new types of error" — [DOI 10.2146/ajhp130332, PMID 24429014](https://doi.org/10.2146/ajhp130332).
- Hassink 2013 (Am J Health Syst Pharm; letter reporting BCMA effect on administration errors in a Dutch community hospital; no abstract) — [DOI 10.2146/ajhp120257, PMID 23515508](https://doi.org/10.2146/ajhp120257).
- Bonkowski 2013 (Acad Emerg Med; naïve observational study, academic ED, 996 pre/982 post administrations): error rate 6.3%→1.2% (relative reduction 80.7%, p<0.0001); wrong-dose errors −90.4%; administrations without an order −72.4% (p=0.057); errors classified incl. wrong drug but no wrong-drug effect reported — [DOI 10.1111/acem.12189, PMID 24033623](https://doi.org/10.1111/acem.12189).
- Owens 2020 (J Emerg Nurs; before–after, community ED, 676 vs 656 administrations): error rate 2.96%→0.76% (relative reduction 74.2%, P<0.01) — [DOI 10.1016/j.jen.2020.07.004, PMID 32962847](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jen.2020.07.004).
- Thompson 2018 (Mayo Clin Proc Innov Qual Outcomes; staged rollout 2008–2013, voluntarily reported events): reported administration errors −43.5%; harmful errors 0.65→0.29 per 100,000 medications (−55.4%); no category E+ error caused by BCMA — [DOI 10.1016/j.mayocpiqo.2018.09.001, PMID 30560236](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mayocpiqo.2018.09.001).
- Merry 2011 (BMJ; prospective randomised open-label trial, 89 anaesthetists, 1,075 cases, 10,764 drug administrations; SAFERSleep multimodal system incl. barcode scan with auditory/visual drug-name verification before administration, prefilled syringes, colour-coded labels): errors 9.1 vs 11.6 per 100 administrations (P=0.045), mostly recording errors; administration errors lower but NS overall; when anaesthetists scanned before each administration and kept voice prompt active, 6.0 vs 9.7 per 100 (P=0.004) — [DOI 10.1136/bmj.d5543, PMID 21940742](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.d5543). Simulation replication (VASER, Anesthesiology 2017, 40 anaesthesiologists, 80 cases): 6.0 vs 11.6 per 100 (P=0.001) — [DOI 10.1097/ALN.0000000000001514, PMID 28079566](https://doi.org/10.1097/ALN.0000000000001514).

**Systematic reviews / meta-analyses**
- Keers 2014 (Drug Saf; 6 RCTs + 7 CTs of MAE interventions): BCMA with eMAR RR 0.71 (95% CI 0.53–0.95) — [DOI 10.1007/s40264-014-0152-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40264-014-0152-0).
- Shah 2016 (Can J Hosp Pharm; systematic review of BCMA with CPOE and ADCs) — record located but no abstract/full text was retrievable — [DOI 10.4212/cjhp.v69i5.1594, PMID 27826157, PMC5085324](https://doi.org/10.4212/cjhp.v69i5.1594).
- Zheng 2020 (Res Social Adm Pharm; 16 papers on ADC/BCMA/closed-loop EMMS for controlled medicines): three quantitative studies reported error reductions after BCMA, "but medications labelled with wrong barcodes and unreadable barcodes led to confusion and administration errors" — [DOI 10.1016/j.sapharm.2020.08.001, PMID 32891535](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sapharm.2020.08.001).
- Sloss 2020 (J Nurs Care Qual; 8 studies of BCMA alerts): alert types included patient mismatch, wrong medication and wrong dose; alert frequency 0.18%–42% of scans; "not all alerts were clinically meaningful" — [DOI 10.1097/NCQ.0000000000000446, PMID 32433151](https://doi.org/10.1097/NCQ.0000000000000446).
- Ciapponi 2021 Cochrane (see Section 9) lists barcoding among interventions studied; the abstract's quantitative results focus on reconciliation and CPOE.

**Workarounds and unintended consequences**
- Koppel 2008 (JAMIA; observation/shadowing at 2 hospitals, interviews at 5, FMEA, override logs): 15 types of workaround (e.g., patient ID barcodes affixed to carts, scanners, doorjambs, nurses' belt rings; pre-scanned medications for several patients on carts) and 31 causes (unreadable barcodes, malfunctioning scanners, missing wristbands, etc.); notes BCMA's error-reduction claims "have not been clearly demonstrated" at that time — [DOI 10.1197/jamia.M2616, PMID 18436903, PMC2442264](https://doi.org/10.1197/jamia.M2616).
- ISMP's 2026–2027 Targeted Medication Safety Best Practices for Hospitals add Best Practice 24: scanning machine-readable codes on patient ID bands and products — [ECRI/ISMP](https://home.ecri.org/blogs/ismp-alerts-and-articles-library/survey-shows-room-for-improvement-with-three-new-best-practices-for-hospitals-1).

### Inferences
- Wrong-drug errors are mechanistically the error type barcode verification is designed to catch (drug–order mismatch), and the studies that do report the category (Küng 2021, Denny 2026, Helmons 2012) show the largest relative reductions in wrong-drug/strength errors. But the flagship administration studies (Poon 2010; Bonkowski 2013) report aggregate non-timing errors and do not isolate wrong-drug effects.
- Reductions depend on compliant scanning (Merry 2011 dose-response; Koppel workarounds); BCMA can convert a wrong-drug error into an overridden alert if products are mislabelled or barcodes unreadable (Zheng 2020).

### Gaps
- No pooled estimate of BCMA's effect on wrong-drug errors specifically was found; Shah 2016 and Truitt 2016 could not be retrieved.
- Evidence is almost all single-site before–after; the only randomised evidence is in anaesthesia (Merry 2011).

## 3. CPOE / e-prescribing design and clinical decision support (indication-based prescribing, LASA alerts, menu design, Leapfrog)

### Takeaway
Indication-linked decision support is the best-documented CPOE strategy against wrong-drug errors: at UI-Health, indication alerts intercepted 1.4 drug-name-confusion errors per 1,000 alerted orders (176 errors in 127,458 alerts; 36 of 39 confused pairs not on ISMP's list) and 0.25 wrong-patient errors per 1,000 alerts. An indications-first prototype produced errors in 7/128 vs 38/128 sessions with vendor systems in usability testing. Generic CPOE roughly halves medication errors in pooled analyses, but Leapfrog simulations show CPOE systems still miss a third or more of harmful orders, LASA-specific alerts are rarely studied, override rates of 46–96% are typical, and no study of pick-list/menu redesign on wrong-drug rates was found.

### Cited Findings

**Indication-based alerts and indication-based prescribing**
- Galanter 2013 (JAMIA; academic centre inpatient + outpatient, 6 years): 127,320 indication alerts → 32 intercepted wrong-patient errors (0.25 per 1,000 alerts); in 59% the prescriber had both patients' charts open — [DOI 10.1136/amiajnl-2012-001555, PMID 23396543, PMC3628069](https://doi.org/10.1136/amiajnl-2012-001555).
- Galanter 2014 (PLoS One; retrospective observational, UI-Health 450-bed tertiary hospital + clinics + ED, Cerner CPOE, Apr 2006–Feb 2012, 54,499 patients): 127,458 indication alerts; 2,410 (1.9%) orders abandoned; 176 confirmed intercepted drug-name-confusion errors (interception rate 0.14 ± 0.01%, i.e., 1.4 per 1,000 alerted orders) across 39 unique pairs; top pairs fluticasone/fluticasone-nasal 100 (57%), metoprolol/metoclopramide 16, hydralazine/diphenhydramine 6, nitroprusside/nitroglycerin 6 (31.6% of nitroprusside alerts); "all but 3 (Actonel/Actos, metformin/metronidazole, hydroxyzine/hydralazine) do not appear on the most widely circulated list of confusing drug names"; 51% of pairs shared first 3 letters and accounted for 81% of errors (suggesting pick-list/auto-fill mechanisms); residents had higher interception rates (attending OR 0.35, 0.23–0.54), ED lower (OR 0.27, 0.11–0.68); authors state only barcoding is "strongly evidence-based" among LASA strategies — [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0101977, PMID 25025346, PMC4098994](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0101977).
- Rash-Foanio 2017 (Am J Health Syst Pharm; algorithm combining order not justified by diagnosis + orthographically similar drug with matching indication): identified cycloserine ordered when cyclosporine intended; chart review of all cycloserine orders over 7 years showed 11 of 16 were erroneous → a LASA alert for cycloserine/cyclosporine was placed in CPOE — [DOI 10.2146/ajhp150690, PMID 28336762](https://doi.org/10.2146/ajhp150690).
- Lambert 2019 (BMJ Qual Saf; 495-bed academic centre, Cerner, 8 years, 488,481 orders): LASA detection algorithm (name similarity + prescribing frequency + diagnostic-claim justification) generated 2,404 triggers (0.5%); 506 reviewed, 61 confirmed errors, PPV 12.1% (95% CI 10.7–13.5); sensitivity not measurable; real-time use blocked by unavailability of accurate diagnoses at order entry — [DOI 10.1136/bmjqs-2019-009420, PMID 31391313, PMC6837246](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2019-009420).
- Walton 2011 (Appl Clin Inform) and Falck 2013 (Int J Med Inform) tested indication prompts for off-label drugs and antihypertensives to improve problem-list documentation (precursors to the error-interception analyses) — [DOI 10.4338/aci-2010-11-ra-0072](https://doi.org/10.4338/aci-2010-11-ra-0072); [DOI 10.1016/j.ijmedinf.2013.07.003, PMID 23932754](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijmedinf.2013.07.003). Grauer 2022 (JAMIA) reports the problem-list effects of the same alert programme — [DOI 10.1093/jamia/ocab285, PMID 34957491](https://doi.org/10.1093/jamia/ocab285).
- Schiff, Seoane-Vazquez, Wright 2016 (N Engl J Med Perspective, "Incorporating indications into medication ordering — time to enter the age of reason"): argues for indication as a "sixth right", with LASA error reduction a stated benefit — [DOI 10.1056/NEJMp1603964](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMp1603964). (Note: the task referred to this as "JAMIA 2016"; the Perspective is NEJM 2016;375:306-9.)
- Kron 2018 (Am J Health Syst Pharm): six expert panels on design requirements for indications-based CPOE; lists avoiding LASA errors among anticipated benefits — [DOI 10.2146/ajhp170346](https://doi.org/10.2146/ajhp170346).
- Garabedian 2019 (JAMA Netw Open; randomised usability study, 32 prescribers, 256 scenarios, 2 health systems): prototype mean time 1.78 min vs 3.37 (vendor 1) and 2.93 (vendor 2); clicks 18.4 vs 46.5 and 20.1 vs 38.3 (P<.001); blinded review found medication errors in 38 of 128 sessions with vendor systems vs 7 of 128 with the indications-based prototype — [DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2019.1514, PMID 30924903, PMC6450312](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2019.1514).
- Salazar 2019 (Am J Health Syst Pharm; 4,356,086 outpatient prescriptions over 5 years, NLP of sigs): indications included in 7.41% of orders, 77% of those for PRN drugs; chronic non-PRN medications "rarely included the indication" — [DOI 10.1093/ajhp/zxz082, PMID 31361884](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajhp/zxz082).
- Cheng 2018 (JAMIA; First Databank indications mapped to 278 ISMP confused pairs): 59% of pairs had no overlap in high-level indications, 21% partial, 20% complete (half of these same-ingredient products, e.g., Adderall/Adderall XR) — [DOI 10.1093/jamia/ocy043, PMID 29800453, PMC7647046](https://doi.org/10.1093/jamia/ocy043). Seoane-Vazquez 2017 (Expert Opin Drug Saf; 33 FDA-reported LASA pairs, 432 indications): indication differentiated 26/33 pairs (78.8%), therapeutic class 72.7%, generic name 24.2% — [DOI 10.1080/14740338.2017.1358361](https://doi.org/10.1080/14740338.2017.1358361).
- Ho 2020 (Am J Health Syst Pharm; VA tertiary centre, clinical indications library): prescriptions with an indication rose from 88% to 96% — [DOI 10.1093/ajhp/zxaa065](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajhp/zxaa065).
- Feather 2023 (BMJ Qual Saf; systematic review of indication documentation/indication-based prescribing interventions in e-prescribing) — [DOI 10.1136/bmjqs-2022-015452](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2022-015452); Feather 2024 (BMJ Qual Saf; randomised cross-over simulation, London NHS trust, patient-specific indication-based tool) — [DOI 10.1136/bmjqs-2024-017733](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2024-017733); Feather 2025 (BMJ Paediatr Open; non-randomised before–after in paediatric ED and ward) — [DOI 10.1136/bmjpo-2025-003662](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjpo-2025-003662). Pairman 2025 (Int J Med Inform; SR of 24 interventional studies on indication-based prescribing): outcomes mostly indication provision/accuracy; prescribing-error outcomes rarely examined — [DOI 10.1016/j.ijmedinf.2025.106209](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijmedinf.2025.106209). Schiff, Lambert, Wright 2023 (BMJ Qual Saf editorial "Prescribing medications with indications: time to flip the script") — [DOI 10.1136/bmjqs-2023-015923, PMID 36948544](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2023-015923). Baysari 2020 (Br J Clin Pharmacol) on barriers for hospital prescribers — [DOI 10.1111/bcp.14532](https://doi.org/10.1111/bcp.14532).

**LASA-specific alerts, ML-triggered LASA alerts, alert fatigue**
- Chen 2023 (Comput Methods Programs Biomed; retrospective, outpatient academic hospital Taiwan, Jul 2019–Jun 2021, ML-based CDSS "MedGuard"): 1,206,895 prescriptions, 28,536 alerts (2.36%), 13,947 (48.9%) accepted; 470 intercepted drug errors (1.64% of alerts = 16.4 per 1,000 alerted orders), including LASA pairs — [DOI 10.1016/j.cmpb.2023.107869](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmpb.2023.107869).
- van der Sijs 2006 (JAMIA review of 17 papers): drug safety alerts overridden in 49–96% of cases — [DOI 10.1197/jamia.M1809](https://doi.org/10.1197/jamia.M1809). Poly 2020 (JMIR Med Inform; 23 studies): override 46.2–96.2%; appropriateness varies by alert type — [DOI 10.2196/15653](https://doi.org/10.2196/15653). Straichman 2017 (390,841 prescriptions; 37.1% triggered alerts; 5.3% accepted; 88.6% of overrides judged justified) — [DOI 10.1016/j.ijmedinf.2017.08.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijmedinf.2017.08.008).
- Syrowatka 2025 (BMJ Digital Health & AI; Making Healthcare Safer IV review, 34 studies 2015–2023): CPOE with medication CDS reduces medication errors (moderate certainty) and ADEs (low certainty); "alert override rates were high"; unintended consequences include CDSS-related errors, alert fatigue, over-dependence — [DOI 10.1136/bmjdhai-2025-000083](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjdhai-2025-000083). Bell 2024 (Appl Clin Inform; 69 studies): 84% reported beneficial prescribing effects; no LASA-alert category reported — [DOI 10.1055/a-2620-3244](https://doi.org/10.1055/a-2620-3244).

**Generic CPOE effect and Leapfrog CPOE Evaluation Tool**
- Nuckols 2014 (Syst Rev; 16 studies): CPOE vs paper RR 0.46 (0.35–0.60) for medication errors, RR 0.47 (0.31–0.71) for preventable ADEs; mostly pre–post designs — [DOI 10.1186/2046-4053-3-56](https://doi.org/10.1186/2046-4053-3-56). Roumeliotis 2019 (J Gen Intern Med; 38 studies 2007–2018 meta-analysis of e-prescribing strategies) — [DOI 10.1007/s11606-019-05236-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-019-05236-8). Ciapponi 2021 Cochrane: CPOE/CDSS vs paper OR 0.74 (0.31–1.79; 2 studies, n=88, moderate certainty); improved vs standard CPOE/CDSS OR 0.85 (0.74–0.97; 2 studies, n=630) — [DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD009985.pub2, PMID 34822165](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD009985.pub2).
- Leapfrog CPOE Evaluation Tool (simulated test patients and orders): 2008–2010, 214 hospitals: 52% of adult and 42.1% of paediatric test orders did not trigger appropriate warnings; 32.8% (adult) and 33.9% (paediatric) of potentially fatal orders were not flagged — [Healthcare Innovation](https://www.hcinnovationgroup.com/clinical-it/clinical-documentation/article/13013555/leapfrog-group-releases-new-cpoe-study); 2011 round (253 hospitals): missed routine orders 36.6%, fatal orders 1.1% — same source; Classen 2020 (JAMA Netw Open 3(5):e205547; 2009–2018 Leapfrog data): mean overall score 53.9%→65.6%; basic CDS categories 69.8%→85.6%; advanced 29.6%→46.1%; wide vendor variation — [Leapfrog summary](https://leapfroggroup.org/node/2555); [JAMA Netw Open article](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2766542).

### Inferences
- The Leapfrog tool's categories (drug–drug, drug–allergy, dose, renal, etc.) do not include a wrong-drug/LASA test; its "missed harmful orders" statistics therefore describe general CDS gaps, not wrong-drug detection specifically. (Inference from the category lists reported in secondary sources; the full category list was not retrieved.)
- Indication alerts work as a forcing "time-out" at order entry; their yield (≈1 interception per 700 alerts) is low per alert but comparable to the ~1 per 1,000 baseline wrong-drug order rate, and they incidentally reveal confused pairs absent from published LASA lists.
- Real-time LASA detection is constrained by the absence of coded indications at order entry (Lambert 2019), which is the central argument for indications-based prescribing.

### Gaps
- No study was found evaluating CPOE menu/pick-list redesign (list length, alphabetical adjacency, auto-complete rules) on wrong-drug error rates, despite Galanter 2014 implicating pick-list mechanisms.
- No controlled study of LASA-pair pop-up alerts (as deployed by most vendors) with override/acceptance and error outcomes was found; "Zhu" could not be identified.
- Kron 2021/2022 follow-ups were not located in PubMed under that author.

## 4. Pharmacist verification and independent double checks

### Takeaway
Double-checking at administration — nearly universal in paediatrics — has weak and inconsistent evidence: two systematic reviews (Alsulami 2012; Koyama 2020) found insufficient evidence that double vs single checking reduces errors or harm; a direct observational study of 5,140 doses found mandated checks were independent only 1% of the time and conferred no benefit (OR 0.89); and the first RCT (simulation, 2026) found independent double-checking improved detection by 11% overall but only among experienced nurses, at a cost of ~5 minutes per set. ISMP still endorses independent double checks (citing up to 95% detection) but only for select high-alert situations. Pharmacist order review and ED clinical pharmacists intercept many prescribing errors (2.9 per 100 medications in the ED), though wrong-dose rather than wrong-drug dominates recovered errors.

### Cited Findings

**Double checks**
- Alsulami 2012 (Arch Dis Child; SR to Oct 2010, 16 articles, 3 quantitative): the one clinical RCT showed errors fell from 2.98 (95% CI 2.45–3.51) to 2.12 (1.69–2.55) per 1,000 administrations with double checking; one pharmacy study: dispensing errors 9.8→6 per year; conclusion "insufficient evidence to either support or refute" — [DOI 10.1136/archdischild-2011-301093, PMID 22550322](https://doi.org/10.1136/archdischild-2011-301093). Alsulami 2014 (UK children's hospital survey, 48 nurses): only 30 had seen the written policy; 1 of 35 definitions matched policy — [DOI 10.7748/ncyp.26.9.21.e378, PMID 25369103](https://doi.org/10.7748/ncyp.26.9.21.e378).
- Koyama 2020 (BMJ Qual Saf; PROSPERO CRD42018103436; 13 studies: 10 observational, 2 RCTs, 1 simulated RCT): among 3 good-quality studies, one showed a significant association with fewer MAEs, one none, one adherence only; no study measured harm; adherence 52–97%; only 3 studies differentiated independent vs primed checking; "insufficient evidence that double versus single checking… is associated with lower rates of MAEs or reduced harm" — [DOI 10.1136/bmjqs-2019-009552, PMID 31391315, PMC7362775](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2019-009552).
- Westbrook 2020 (BMJ Qual Saf; direct observation, 298 nurses, 5,140 doses, 1,523 patients, 9 wards, Australian paediatric hospital): where mandated (3,563 administrations), 1.0% were independent, 92.5% primed, 6.5% none/incomplete; no association with MAEs (OR 0.89, 0.65–1.21) or severity (OR 0.86); where optional but performed, fewer MAEs (OR 0.71, 0.54–0.95); each check 6.4 min (107 hours per 1,000 administrations) — [DOI 10.1136/bmjqs-2020-011473, PMID 32769177, PMC7982937](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2020-011473).
- Westbrook 2026 (BMJ Qual Saf; multicentre randomised controlled simulation trial, 82 nurses, 2 paediatric hospitals, 1,160 administrations, 640 embedded error opportunities): overall detection 72.2%; IDC 77.7% vs single 66.3% (aIRR 1.11, 1.01–1.21, p=0.02); experienced nurses +26% (aIRR 1.26); early-career (<5 y) no benefit (aIRR 0.98); social loafing — early-career nurses detected 26% more errors single-checking than as first checker in IDC; IDC took 4.96 min longer per set; authors conclude IDC "may be unsuitable as a universal safety strategy" — [DOI 10.1136/bmjqs-2025-019743, PMID 41825963](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2025-019743).
- ISMP position ("Independent double checks: worth the effort if used judiciously and properly", 2019; ECRI-hosted): cites studies showing independent double checks can detect up to 95% of errors (e.g., 10% error rate → ~0.5%); recommends limiting them to select high-alert medications/high-risk tasks rather than blanket use; argues primed checks fail because of confirmation bias; ISMP responded to the Koyama review by noting most included studies covered all medications rather than selected high-alert drugs — [ECRI/ISMP](https://home.ecri.org/blogs/ismp-alerts-and-articles-library/independent-double-checks-worth-the-effort-if-used-judiciously-and-properly); [PSQH analysis](https://www.psqh.com/analysis/published-review-of-independent-double-checks-shouldnt-dissuade-providers-from-using-them-judiciously).
- Kuitunen 2021 (J Patient Saf; SR of systemic causes of IV medication errors, 11 studies): "failure in double-checking procedures, and confusion between look-alike, sound-alike medications were the leading causes" (all included studies low quality) — [DOI 10.1097/PTS.0000000000000632, PMID 32011427](https://doi.org/10.1097/PTS.0000000000000632).

**Pharmacist review / clinical pharmacists**
- Rothschild 2010 (Ann Emerg Med; observational, 4 academic EDs, 226 sessions, 787 h, 17,320 medications, 6,471 patients): 504 recovered errors = 7.8 per 100 patients, 2.9 per 100 medications; 90.3% intercepted before reaching patient; 47.8% potentially serious; most common types dosing, omission, wrong frequency (wrong drug not among the top three) — [DOI 10.1016/j.annemergmed.2009.10.012, PMID 20005011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2009.10.012).
- Patanwala 2012 (Ann Emerg Med; prospective multicentre, 4 EDs, 1,000 pharmacist-hours, 16,446 patients): 364 confirmed interceptions; 51.4% from consultative activities, 34.9% from order review; 82.4% at prescribing phase; wrong dose 44.2% most common — [DOI 10.1016/j.annemergmed.2011.11.013, PMID 22226174](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2011.11.013). Patanwala 2010 (Ann Emerg Med; prospective observation, 192 ED patients): 178 errors, 59.4% of patients had ≥1; prescribing 53.9%, administering 34.8% — [DOI 10.1016/j.annemergmed.2009.12.017, PMID 20074833](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2009.12.017).
- Lebas 2026 (Therapie; French psychiatric hospital error reports 2014–2021): wrong drug was 29.4% of errors (second to wrong dose 31.3%); 78.1% of hospital-sector errors were intercepted during pharmacist order review — [DOI 10.1016/j.therap.2026.04.002, PMID 42106308](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.therap.2026.04.002).
- Al-khani 2014 (Saudi Pharm J; retrospective review of "incorrect drug" error reports, ambulatory pharmacies, tertiary hospital Riyadh, 2011–12): explored factors that helped pharmacists identify incorrect-drug prescriptions — [DOI 10.1016/j.jsps.2013.11.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsps.2013.11.003).
- Ciapponi 2021 Cochrane: medication reconciliation by pharmacists vs other professionals may reduce medication errors (OR 0.21, 0.09–0.48; 8 studies, n=2,648; low certainty) — [DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD009985.pub2](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD009985.pub2).
- Pharmacist-prioritisation AI tools (Corny 2020; Levivien 2022) are covered in Section 8.

### Inferences
- The "95% detection" figure ISMP cites and the ~72% detection observed under simulation (Westbrook 2026) are not contradictory if the former refers to idealised independent checks; the real-world problem is that independent checks are rarely actually independent (1% in Westbrook 2020).
- Pharmacist interception evidence quantifies error recovery, not reduction in wrong-drug errors reaching patients; no study isolates pharmacist review's effect on wrong-drug orders specifically.

### Gaps
- No RCT of double-checking in live clinical settings with wrong-drug outcomes exists.
- Wrong-drug-specific interception rates by pharmacists (ED/ICU) were not reported in the retrieved studies.

## 5. Drug naming and regulatory name review (FDA DMEPA/POCA, EMA NRG, INN stems, biologic suffixes, renames)

### Takeaway
Pre-approval name review is now systematised (FDA POCA with ≥70% "highly similar" threshold; EMA Name Review Group rejecting roughly half of proposed invented names per meeting), and lab psycholinguistic tests have been validated against real-world pharmacy error data (explaining 37–45% of variance in pair error rates; r=0.50 for individual names). However, no study was found demonstrating that regulatory name review has reduced the incidence of wrong-drug errors, and post-approval renames (Kapidex→Dexilant 2010; Brintellix→Trintellix 2016) have not been evaluated for post-rename error rates.

### Cited Findings

**Similarity measurement and the FDA process**
- Lambert 1999 (Med Care; case-control, 1,127 confused pairs vs 1,127 controls; 22 computerised similarity measures): every measure was a significant risk factor; three-predictor logistic model cross-validated sensitivity 93.7%, specificity 95.9%, accuracy 94.8% — [DOI 10.1097/00005650-199912000-00005](https://doi.org/10.1097/00005650-199912000-00005).
- Kondrak & Dorr 2006 (Artif Intell Med): BI-SIM (orthographic) and ALINE (phonetic) measures; "Our system is currently used as the basis of a system developed for the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for detection of confusable drug names" — [DOI 10.1016/j.artmed.2005.07.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.artmed.2005.07.005).
- FDA POCA thresholds: combined score ≥70% = highly similar; the 2014 draft guidance set moderate 50–69% and low ≤49%; the POCA 4.3 algorithm update revised moderate to 55–69% and low to ≤54%; FDA's 2020 draft naming guidances describe a 55% cutoff and state FDA runs its own POCA review regardless of sponsor analysis — [FDA POCA update page](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-errors-related-cder-regulated-drug-products/update-phonetic-and-orthographic-computer-analysis-tool); [RAPS 2014](https://www.raps.org/resource/what-s-in-a-drug-name-fda-explains-in-new-draft-g.html); [RAPS Dec 2020](https://www.raps.org/resource/fdas-proprietary-name-guidance-seeks-to-avoid-medi.html); example FDA name review memos in Drugs@FDA, e.g. [2023 review listing 265 names retrieved at ≥55%/≥70%](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2023/216513Orig1s000NameR.pdf).
- Stockbridge 2015 (Ther Innov Regul Sci): describes FDA reviewer tools adding orthographic, phonetic and semantic similarity measures to existing computerised measures — [DOI 10.1177/2168479015570331](https://doi.org/10.1177/2168479015570331). Shah 2016 (Ther Innov Regul Sci; ISMP confused-name pairs with ≥1 proprietary name): 99% shared ≥1 of three features (same first letter, ≥3-letter string, similar length); 75% had combined POCA ≥50% — [DOI 10.1177/2168479016667161](https://doi.org/10.1177/2168479016667161). Rider 2018 (Ther Innov Regul Sci): 1–5% of prescriptions carry only "use as directed", removing a differentiating cue; lists 14 product categories where this matters for name review — [DOI 10.1177/2168479018782669](https://doi.org/10.1177/2168479018782669).
- Emmerton 2020 (Int J Med Inform; FDA open-source POCA code adapted to the Australian register): 7,750 pairs with similarity ≥0.66; computed scores correlated only modestly with manual scores (r=0.324) and risk-category agreement was not significant (kappa −0.162) — [DOI 10.1016/j.ijmedinf.2020.104119](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijmedinf.2020.104119).

**Validation of pre-approval tests against real-world errors (Lambert group)**
- Schroeder 2017 (BMJ Qual Saf; 80 participants—doctors, nurses, pharmacists, technicians, laypeople—visual, auditory and memory tests of LASA pairs): lab error rates predicted real-world confusion rates from a large outpatient pharmacy chain (best model 37% of variance); cross-validation in a second chain explained 45% of variance; notes "roughly one per thousand prescriptions results in the wrong drug being filled" in the USA and that no pre-approval test had previously been shown to predict real-world errors — [DOI 10.1136/bmjqs-2015-005099, PMID 27193033, PMC5530327](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2015-005099).
- Lambert 2025 (BMJ Qual Saf; 19 pharmacists + 18 technicians, 77 drug names): mean lab error rate correlated with log real-world error rate from a national pharmacy chain's PSO (r=0.50, p<0.0001); validates psycholinguistic tests for individual-name (not just pair) confusability — [DOI 10.1136/bmjqs-2024-017688, PMID 40037800](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2024-017688).
- Lambert 2016 (J Biopharm Stat): statistical framework (logistic regression, Poisson prediction limits, credible intervals) for declaring a proposed name "unacceptably confusing" relative to control names in memory/perception experiments — [DOI 10.1080/10543406.2015.1052481, PMID 26011079](https://doi.org/10.1080/10543406.2015.1052481). Lambert 2010 (Soc Sci Med; auditory experiments): identification accuracy rose with signal-to-noise ratio, familiarity and prescribing frequency; substituted names were almost always more frequently prescribed drugs — [DOI 10.1016/j.socscimed.2010.01.042, PMID 20207461](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2010.01.042).

**Post-approval renames**
- Kapidex (dexlansoprazole, approved Jan 2009) renamed Dexilant in April 2010 after dispensing errors from confusion with Casodex (bicalutamide) and Kadian (morphine); product unchanged, new NDC — [Takeda release 2010](https://www.takeda.com/en-us/newsroom/news-releases/2010/kapidex-dexlansoprazole-renamed-dexilant-in-us-to-avoid-name-confusion/); [Drugs.com](https://www.drugs.com/newdrugs/kapidex-dexlansoprazole-renamed-dexilant-u-s-avoid-confusion-4950.html).
- Brintellix (vortioxetine, approved Sept 2013) renamed Trintellix in June 2016 after FDA's July 2015 warning that confusion with Brilinta (ticagrelor) had caused prescribing and dispensing errors; FDA reported 12 reports in which a patient was given the wrong drug, none in which the wrong drug was consumed; FDA advised including generic name and indication during the transition — [Pharmacy Times](https://pharmacytimes.com/view/brintellix-changed-to-trintellix-to-curb-brand-name-confusion-with-brilinta); [RAPS](https://www.raps.org/resource/confusion-pushes-fda-to-change-antidepressants-br.html); [Takeda release 2016](https://www.takeda.com/en-us/newsroom/news-releases/2016/brintellix-vortioxetine-renamed-trintellix-vortioxetine-in-us-to-avoid-name-confusion/). Lizano-Díez 2020 (BMC Health Serv Res) uses the Brintellix/Brilinta case to argue for an international policy on unique names — [DOI 10.1186/s12913-020-4922-3](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-020-4922-3).

**EMA Name Review Group**
- The NRG applies the CHMP guideline on acceptability of invented names (EMA/CHMP/287710/2014, currently Rev. 7); objection categories include similarity with another invented name (largest category: 2024 totals 115 objections endorsed, 186 not endorsed), misleading therapeutic/pharmaceutical connotations, similarity with an INN, inclusion of an INN stem, unacceptable qualifiers, promotional/offensive connotations, pronunciation difficulty; at the 17–18 Feb 2026 meeting 44 names were accepted and 38 rejected — [EMA NRG overview Feb 2026](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/other/overview-invented-names-reviewed-february-2026-name-review-group-nrg-adopted-chmp-meeting-24-march-2026_en.pdf); [EMA NRG overview Nov 2024](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/chmp-annex/overview-invented-names-reviewed-november-2024-name-review-group-nrg-adopted-chmp-meeting-19-november-2024_en.pdf).

**INN stems and biologic suffixes**
- Bryan 2015 (PLoS One; 7,987 INNs analysed): compliance with WHO INN naming guidelines inconsistent; stems spelled inconsistently and unordered; INN pairs sharing a stem often have Levenshtein distance <5 and thus high confusion potential; a "tension between WHO guidelines stipulating use of stems to denote meaning, and the aim of reducing similarities" — [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0145431, PMID 26701761, PMC4689353](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0145431).
- FDA final guidance "Nonproprietary Naming of Biological Products" (12 Jan 2017): FDA-designated four-letter meaningless suffix attached by hyphen for originator, related and biosimilar products; stated purposes are pharmacovigilance and "accurate identification of these biological products by health care practitioners"; a later draft update exempts previously licensed products without suffixes — [HHS/FDA guidance PDF](https://www.hhs.gov/guidance/sites/default/files/hhs-guidance-documents/FDA/Nonproprietary-Naming-of-Biological-Products-Guidance-for-Industry.pdf); [RAPS summary](https://www.raps.org/resource/non-proprietary-naming-of-biologics-and-biosimilar.html).
- Other jurisdictions: Jayasinghe 2023 (Sri Lanka; random sample of 385 registered proprietary names): 64.2% were identical, look-alike (91 pairs) or sound-alike (80 pairs) to other registered names — [DOI 10.1007/s43441-023-00557-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43441-023-00557-7). Supapaan 2024 (Thailand; 18,108 registered tablets/capsules) — [DOI 10.18549/pharmpract.2024.3.2955](https://doi.org/10.18549/pharmpract.2024.3.2955).

### Inferences
- The Lambert group's validation work provides the methodological basis for an evidence-based pre-approval screen, but it validates the *test*, not the *regulatory decision*; whether FDA/EMA rejections have lowered the population rate of name-confusion errors remains unmeasured.
- Renames are natural experiments; the absence of published post-rename error surveillance (MedWatch, ISMP MERP) is a notable evidence gap.

### Gaps
- FDA guidance texts ("Best Practices in Developing Proprietary Names", "Contents of a Complete Submission") could not be fetched; tier wording is from secondary summaries.
- No study quantifying error-rate change after Kapidex→Dexilant or Brintellix→Trintellix was found.
- No evaluation of the biologic-suffix convention's effect on wrong-product errors was found.

## 6. Packaging, labelling, product differentiation, storage segregation and ADC configuration

### Takeaway
Packaging and storage interventions (colour differentiation, prominent active-ingredient placement, separation of LASA stock, locked-lidded ADC pockets, prefilled/ready-to-administer syringes) are heavily recommended by ISMP, WHO and anaesthesia bodies, but the measured evidence is mostly laboratory/simulation (e.g., Endestad 2016 package redesign cut confusion errors by about two-thirds; Laxton 2024 colour-coded trays) plus observational audits (Ruutiainen 2021: 70% of an ICU ADC's selection carried LASA risk and 16% were stored adjacent). Colour-coding of syringe labels has conflicting evidence (one study showed no effect on syringe swaps; Haslam 2006 found high latent error rates during a transition to international colour coding). The only randomised clinical evidence is the Merry 2011 multimodal anaesthesia system, where barcode scanning, not labels alone, drove the benefit.

### Cited Findings
- Larmené-Beld 2018 SR: of 16 included studies only 2 evaluated colour-coding and 3 other strategies; "Color-coding had no effect on the prevention of syringe-swaps in one study"; "More evidence, especially from real-life setting is needed to support safe labeling strategies" — [DOI 10.1007/s00228-018-2471-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00228-018-2471-z).
- Endestad 2016 (Hum Factors; 2 experiments, 59 + 25 volunteers, young and elderly): the commonest error was failing to recognise that two packages contained the same active ingredient (41% young, 68% elderly), reduced to 8% and 16% with redesigned packages (prominent substance name/dose in a high-contrast band); colour-related confusion errors reduced by two-thirds — [DOI 10.1177/0018720816664824](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720816664824).
- Chermak 2009 (J Am Pharm Assoc; 918 primary package labels in a community pharmacy): wide, unstandardised variety of typographic techniques (boldface on ~98% of brand labels, underlining 0.2%) — [DOI 10.1331/JAPhA.2009.07137, PMID 19443320](https://doi.org/10.1331/JAPhA.2009.07137).
- Wang 2020 (Hum Factors; simulated pharmacy shelf): identical name format on prescription and package and attention-directing visual cues sped and improved search; text enhancement did not — [DOI 10.1177/0018720819870700](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720819870700).
- Anaesthesia: Jensen 2004 (Anaesthesia; systematic review, 98 references) produced five strong recommendations incl. standardised legible labels, labelling every syringe, formal organisation of drug drawers/workspace, and checking labels with a second person or a device — [DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2044.2004.03670.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2044.2004.03670.x). Haslam 2006 (Eur J Anaesthesiol): "high latent drug administration error rates associated with the introduction of the international colour coding syringe labelling system" — [DOI 10.1017/s0265021505002097](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0265021505002097); Webster 2007 reply arguing a systems view — [DOI 10.1017/s0265021506001670](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0265021506001670). Fasting 2000 (Can J Anaesth; 55,426 procedures over 36 months; colour-coded labels plus education introduced at 18 months) — [DOI 10.1007/bf03027956](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf03027956) (effect size not captured in retrieved abstract). Laxton 2024 (Br J Anaesth; experimental psychology study of colour-coded compartmentalised syringe trays under cognitive load) reported improved error detection — [DOI 10.1016/j.bja.2023.12.033](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bja.2023.12.033); Webster 2024 BJA editorial on colour and cognition — [DOI 10.1016/j.bja.2024.02.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bja.2024.02.003). Brook 2022 (Anesthesiology) discusses prefilled syringes replacing error-prone manual preparation and the unresolved debate on plain vs colour-coded labels — [DOI 10.1097/aln.0000000000004138](https://doi.org/10.1097/aln.0000000000004138). Jelacic 2015 (Anesth Analg) describes a barcode vial-scan/label-print/pre-administration scan system to prevent vial and syringe swaps — [DOI 10.1213/ane.0000000000000256](https://doi.org/10.1213/ane.0000000000000256). Merry 2011 RCT — see Section 2.
- Ryan 2023 (Int J Clin Pharm; scoping review of look-alike medication incidents in the perioperative setting to July 2022): only 18 publications, mostly case reports/letters/QI; one controlled simulation and one RCT; interventions themed as regulation, procurement, standardised storage, labelling, environment, teamwork; "Key interventions did not involve technology-based solutions and further research is required to assess their effectiveness" — [DOI 10.1007/s11096-023-01629-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11096-023-01629-2). Stipp 2022 (Medicine): in 277 observed operations labelling errors were the most frequent observed error type — [DOI 10.1097/md.0000000000031432](https://doi.org/10.1097/md.0000000000031432).
- ADCs: Ruutiainen 2021 (Eur J Hosp Pharm; ICU ADC inventory audit): ~70% (355/488) of the ADC's selection had a LASA risk with at least one other product; 20% of these were high-alert; 16% (58/355) stored unsafely close to another LASA medicine; "automation can also increase [risk]… when placing multiple LASA medicines in the same compartment" — [DOI 10.1136/ejhpharm-2020-002531](https://doi.org/10.1136/ejhpharm-2020-002531). Helmons 2012 barcode refill (Section 2). ISMP Guidelines for the Safe Use of ADCs (2019) and ISMP LASA guidance recommend storing LASA products in separate physical locations and avoiding open-matrix drawers in favour of locked-lidded pockets or separate bins — [ECRI/ISMP ADC guidelines page](https://home.ecri.org/blogs/ismp-resources/guidelines-for-the-safe-use-of-automated-dispensing-cabinets); [ISMP LASA strategies article](https://home.ecri.org/blogs/ismp-alerts-and-articles-library/adopt-strategies-to-manage-look-alike-and-or-sound-alike-medication-name-mix-ups). McCoy 2005 (Jt Comm J Qual Patient Saf; case of Primacor given instead of potassium chloride from look-alike packaging) led to shelf reorganisation and tracking of ADC filling errors — [DOI 10.1016/s1553-7250(05)31007-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/s1553-7250(05)31007-5).
- WHO's 2023 technical document "Medication safety for look-alike, sound-alike medicines" (Medication Safety Solutions series, ISBN 9789240058897) defines look-alike (packaging, shape, colour, size) and sound-alike (name/dose/strength phonetics) medicines and compiles system strategies — [WHO publication page](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240058897).
- ISMP 2024–2025 Targeted Best Practices added a practice on preventing wrong-route errors with tranexamic acid (a vial/package look-alike problem with local anaesthetics), reflecting the packaging dimension of product mix-ups — [ECRI/ISMP](https://home.ecri.org/blogs/ismp-alerts-and-articles-library/three-new-best-practices-in-the-2024-2025-targeted-medication-safety-best-practices-for-hospitals).

### Inferences
- The evidence hierarchy for packaging/storage is inverted relative to its policy prominence: strong consensus recommendations rest on case reports, audits and a handful of lab experiments.
- Colour coding is double-edged: class-based colour can prevent cross-class swaps but may increase within-class swaps and creates transition risk (Haslam 2006).

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found on neuromuscular-blocker segregation/warning labels, standard-concentration labelling, tamper-evident/different caps, or formulary removal of LASA products with error-rate outcomes (PubMed searches returned nothing).
- The 2019 ISMP ADC guideline text itself was not retrievable; recommendations are cited via ISMP/ECRI articles.

## 7. Organisational and policy measures (Joint Commission, ISMP best practices, WHO, national lists, education, read-back, indications on prescriptions, patient engagement)

### Takeaway
Organisational requirements — a maintained LASA list (Joint Commission MM.01.02.01), ISMP's Targeted Best Practices (biennial since 2014), WHO's 2007 Patient Safety Solution on LASA names and 2017 "Medication Without Harm" challenge, and national Tall Man lists — are essentially consensus-based. Staff education shows pooled error reductions in meta-analyses of mixed quality (OR 0.38 for pharmacist-led education; Hedges' g 1.06 for nurse education) but other reviews find no effect (OR 0.72, NS) and no LASA-specific educational trial exists. No empirical studies were found on read-back of verbal orders or on patients detecting wrong-drug errors.

### Cited Findings
- Joint Commission: the 2005 NPSG required organisations to "identify and, at a minimum, annually review a list of look-alike sound-alike drugs" and implement prevention strategies (McCoy 2005, [DOI 10.1016/s1553-7250(05)31007-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/s1553-7250(05)31007-5)); by 2010 the goal (NPSG.03.03.01) had moved to Medication Management standard MM.01.02.01, which requires a list of LASA medications the organisation stores/dispenses/administers, actions to prevent interchange, and annual review; TJC eliminated the requirement to select pairs from its own website and no longer maintains a list — [PPP Magazine 2010](https://pppmag.com/article/670); [Relias Media](https://reliasmedia.com/articles/128382-joint-commission-revises-look-alike-sound-alike-list); [TJC document on high-alert/LASA medications](https://www.jointcommission.org/-/media/tjc/documents/accred-and-cert/ahc/High_Alert_Hazardous_Look_Alike_Sound_Alike_Medications.pdf).
- ISMP Targeted Medication Safety Best Practices for Hospitals: introduced 2014 with six practices, updated biennially; 2024–2025 edition comprised 22 practices including three new (tranexamic acid wrong-route, transitions of care, vaccines); 2026–2027 edition adds Best Practices 23 (IV push safety), 24 (scanning machine-readable codes on ID bands and products) and 25 (culture of safety), with Becker's reporting 15 strategies in the current set — [ECRI/ISMP 2024–25](https://home.ecri.org/blogs/ismp-alerts-and-articles-library/three-new-best-practices-in-the-2024-2025-targeted-medication-safety-best-practices-for-hospitals); [ECRI/ISMP 2026–27](https://home.ecri.org/blogs/ismp-alerts-and-articles-library/survey-shows-room-for-improvement-with-three-new-best-practices-for-hospitals-1); [Becker's](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/pharmacy/15-hospital-best-practices-for-medication-safety-ismp/). (Count discrepancy between sources noted.)
- WHO: Patient Safety Solutions vol. 1, Solution 1 (May 2007) "Look-Alike, Sound-Alike Medication Names" recommends screening new names and physically separating LASA stock — [WHO PDF](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/patient-safety/patient-safety-solutions/ps-solution1-look-alike-sound-alike-medication-names.pdf?sfvrsn=d4fb860b_8). The High 5s project (launched 2006; "5 problems, 5 countries, 5 years") published SOPs for medication reconciliation, correct-site surgery and concentrated injectable medicines — [WHO High 5s overview](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/patient-safety/high5s/high-5s-action-on-patient-safety.pdf?sfvrsn=1e623c21_6); [Leotsakos 2014, Int J Qual Health Care](https://academic.oup.com/intqhc/article/26/2/109/1803803). Medication Without Harm (third Global Patient Safety Challenge, March 2017; goal 50% reduction in severe avoidable medication harm in 5 years); technical report "Medication Safety in High-risk Situations" (WHO/UHC/SDS/2019.10, June 2019) names LASA medicines as a high-risk situation — [WHO webinar page](https://www.who.int/news-room/events/detail/2022/05/17/default-calendar/medication-safety-webinar-series-medication-safety-in-high-risk-situations); "Medication safety for look-alike, sound-alike medicines" (20 Oct 2023) — [WHO](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240058897).
- Australia: National Tall Man/Mixed-Case Lettering list mandatory under NSQHS Medication Safety Standard (Section 1). UK: a London trust used Levenshtein, bigram and trigram thresholds to prioritise 82 of 212 LASA pairs for TML in its dispensing system, citing the National Pharmacy Association LASA list — [Nagamotoo 2022, Int J Pharm Pract](https://doi.org/10.1093/ijpp/riac089.041).
- Education: Härkänen 2016 (Nurse Educ Today; 14 studies 2000–2015): all interventions improved administration safety on original p-values; pooled Hedges' g 1.06, but only 5 studies of moderate/strong quality and the only strong-quality study showed the weakest effect — [DOI 10.1016/j.nedt.2016.03.017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nedt.2016.03.017). Jaam 2021 (PLoS One; 12 studies, 115,058 participants): pharmacist-led education OR 0.38 (0.22–0.65) — [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0253588](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0253588). Berdot 2015 (Int J Nurs Stud; 7 controlled studies, 4 training-related): OR 0.72 (0.39–1.34), p=0.3, "did not find evidence that interventions can effectively decrease administration errors" — [DOI 10.1016/j.ijnurstu.2015.08.012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijnurstu.2015.08.012). Keers 2014: simulation-based nurse education RR 0.17 (0.08–0.38); pharmacist-led training RR 0.76 (0.67–0.87) — [DOI 10.1007/s40264-014-0152-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40264-014-0152-0). Sim 2025 (J Patient Saf; SEIPS-based multifaceted programme, 73 nurses): improved culture and compliance but no significant change in error rates (P=0.555) — [DOI 10.1097/pts.0000000000001345](https://doi.org/10.1097/pts.0000000000001345). Mostafa 2019 (J Public Health; Cairo emergency hospital pre–post, 1,025 vs 1,024 patients): errors 34.2%→15.3% — [DOI 10.1093/pubmed/fdy216](https://doi.org/10.1093/pubmed/fdy216). Bryan 2020 and Ostini 2012 (Section 1) note LASA recommendations rest on expert opinion.
- Indications on prescriptions/labels: Garada 2017 (BMC Health Serv Res; qualitative, 34 Australian consumers, pharmacists, prescribers): stakeholders believed indications would help match drug to purpose and reduce prescribing/dispensing errors; prescribers cited time and privacy concerns — [DOI 10.1186/s12913-017-2704-3, PMID 29141618](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-017-2704-3). Salazar 2019: indications present in 7.41% of 4.36 million prescriptions (Section 3). FDA's Brintellix communication recommended writing the generic name and indication (Section 5).
- Patient engagement: Harrison 2015 (Int J Qual Health Care) systematic review of patients' experiences of adverse events documents knowledge gaps in capturing patient-detected harm — [DOI 10.1093/intqhc/mzv075, PMID 26424702](https://doi.org/10.1093/intqhc/mzv075). Tchijevitch 2026 (Basic Clin Pharmacol Toxicol) analyses medication errors surfaced through patient complaints at a Danish hospital — [DOI 10.1111/bcpt.70233](https://doi.org/10.1111/bcpt.70233).
- Spoken orders: Lambert 2010 shows noise and unfamiliarity drive misidentification of spoken names (Section 5) — [DOI 10.1016/j.socscimed.2010.01.042](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2010.01.042). Patanwala 2012: 32.7% of ED pharmacist interceptions involved verbal orders — [DOI 10.1016/j.annemergmed.2011.11.013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2011.11.013).

### Inferences
- Education meta-analyses are dominated by uncontrolled before–after studies with self-reported or reporting-based outcomes; the better-designed studies (Berdot 2015's controlled set; Sim 2025) tend toward null. Education is best viewed as a necessary adjunct rather than a wrong-drug countermeasure in its own right.
- The regulatory shift from a prescriptive TJC LASA list to a locally derived list acknowledges that confusable pairs are context-specific (consistent with Galanter 2014's finding that most confused pairs were not on national lists).

### Gaps
- I found no WHO document describing a High 5s "LASA SOP" or its discontinuation; the claim that such an SOP was dropped could not be verified from retrievable sources (High 5s SOPs located: medication reconciliation, correct-site surgery, concentrated injectables).
- No study of read-back/teach-back for verbal medication orders with error outcomes was found.
- No study quantifying patients' or families' detection of wrong-drug errors was found.
- NHS/MHRA national guidance on LASA was not specifically researched beyond the UK trust example.

## 8. Emerging technologies: AI/ML outlier detection, NLP, computer vision, smart pumps

### Takeaway
Probabilistic outlier-detection CDS (MedAware and successors) generates low-burden alerts (0.4–2.4% of orders) of which 70–85% are judged clinically valid and roughly 40% change orders; 68% of its alerts would not have fired in a rule-based system, and one Taiwanese ML system intercepted 16.4 errors per 1,000 alerts including LASA errors. But scoping/systematic reviews (Syrowatka 2022; Johns 2023; Hêche 2026) stress that >90% of studies report only technical performance, most are high risk of bias, and real-world outcome evaluation is scarce. Computer-vision pill recognition reaches 80–92% accuracy under optimal conditions but 21–90% on real clinical images. Smart-pump evidence concerns dosing limits; no wrong-drug-specific effect was found.

### Cited Findings
- Schiff 2017 (JAMIA; 5 years of EHR data, 747,985 patients, 2 academic centres, outlier-detection system): 15,693 alerts; 300-chart review — about three-quarters valid, and 75.0% of valid alerts clinically useful; performance "critically dependent on the quality and completeness of the underlying data" — [DOI 10.1093/jamia/ocw171](https://doi.org/10.1093/jamia/ocw171).
- Rozenblum 2020 (Jt Comm J Qual Patient Saf; retrospective MedAware on 2009–2013 outpatient data, 2 centres): 10,668 alerts; 68.2% would not have been generated by the existing CDS; 92% accurate, 79.7% clinically valid on 300-chart review; estimated >$60 saved per alert, $1.3M extrapolated — [DOI 10.1016/j.jcjq.2019.09.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcjq.2019.09.008).
- Segal 2019 (JAMIA; prospective, single medical ward, tertiary centre Israel, 16 months): alerts on 0.4% of orders; 85% clinically valid, 80% useful; 43% led to order changes; 60% flagged after dispensing following changes in patient status — [DOI 10.1093/jamia/ocz135](https://doi.org/10.1093/jamia/ocz135).
- Naor 2022 (J Med Syst; 600-bed CDSS-naïve Israeli centre): retrospective 226,804 orders → 2,731 alerts (1.2%), 69% relevant; prospective 399 alerts (1.6%), 72% relevant, 41% changed prescriber behaviour — [DOI 10.1007/s10916-022-01864-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10916-022-01864-6).
- Corny 2020 (JAMIA; hybrid ML/rules prioritisation of pharmacist review, 10,716 patients/133,179 orders training; 412 patients validation): AUROC 0.81 / AUPRC 0.75 vs 0.65/0.56 for the CDS system — [DOI 10.1093/jamia/ocaa154](https://doi.org/10.1093/jamia/ocaa154). Levivien 2022 (Int J Clin Pharm; randomised single-blinded, 420 prescriptions): severe DRPs in 2.8% of low-score vs 15.3% of high-score prescriptions (p=0.0248); tool ruled out 55% of prescriptions from review — [DOI 10.1007/s11096-021-01366-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11096-021-01366-4).
- Chen 2023 MedGuard (Section 3): 16.4 intercepted errors per 1,000 alerts incl. LASA — [DOI 10.1016/j.cmpb.2023.107869](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmpb.2023.107869).
- Lambert 2019 LASA detection algorithm and Rash-Foanio 2017 (Section 3).
- Dose-outlier methods: Nagata 2021 (PLoS One; one-class SVM, 21 drugs, Kyushu University Hospital 2014–2019): detected 27/31 (87.1%) clinically identified over/underdoses — [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0260315](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0260315); dos Santos 2019 (IEEE JBHI; DDC-Outlier on 563,000 prescriptions) — [DOI 10.1109/jbhi.2018.2828028](https://doi.org/10.1109/jbhi.2018.2828028); Lee 2021 (Drug Saf; hybrid rule/DNN paediatric outpatient, precision 72.9%, recall 81.0%) — [DOI 10.1007/s40264-021-01123-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40264-021-01123-6); Ouzar 2025 (Comput Biol Med; BERT embeddings + one-class classification on MIMIC, F1 86.8%) — [DOI 10.1016/j.compbiomed.2025.110775](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compbiomed.2025.110775).
- LLMs: Ong 2025 (Cell Rep Med; prospective cross-over, 91 error scenarios, 16 specialties): pharmacist + LLM co-pilot accuracy 61% (F1 0.59), 1.5-fold better than pharmacist alone for serious-harm errors — [DOI 10.1016/j.xcrm.2025.102323](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2025.102323).
- Reviews: Syrowatka 2022 (Lancet Digit Health; 78 articles): 94% assessed technical performance only; "few studies evaluated the use of AI in clinical settings" — [DOI 10.1016/S2589-7500(21)00229-6, PMID 34836823](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2589-7500(21)00229-6). Bates 2021 (NPJ Digit Med; 392 studies across 8 harm domains) — [DOI 10.1038/s41746-021-00423-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-021-00423-6). Johns 2023 (Eur J Hosp Pharm; 13 ML studies for hospital pharmacy): 12 of 13 high risk of bias (PROBAST) — [DOI 10.1136/ejhpharm-2023-003857](https://doi.org/10.1136/ejhpharm-2023-003857). Hêche 2026 (Research Square preprint scoping review, 22 studies 2015–Apr 2025): "evidence remains fragmented, with limited generalizability, inconsistent labeling, and scarce real-world evaluation" — [DOI 10.21203/rs.3.rs-8919709/v1](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-8919709/v1). Damiani 2023 (BMJ Open; primary care, 14 studies): 10/14 reported reduced errors — [DOI 10.1136/bmjopen-2022-065301](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2022-065301).
- Computer vision: Ashraf 2026 (JMIR Med Inform; YOLO11 vs three AutoML platforms, 30 medications, up to 26,880 training images, tested on images from 3 hospitals): verification-set accuracy 80.8% (YOLO11) to 91.6% (Google Vertex); on clinical datasets accuracy fluctuated 20.6%–90% — [DOI 10.2196/79160, PMID 41961529](https://doi.org/10.2196/79160). Sai Sachin 2026 (Front Artif Intell; 400 pill classes, 20,000 images): Top-1 89.8%, Top-5 99.5%; authors stress final verification must remain with a professional — [DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1890296, PMID 42548680](https://doi.org/10.3389/frai.2026.1890296).
- Smart pumps: Schnock 2016 (BMJ Qual Saf; multihospital observational): IV errors persist despite smart pumps — [DOI 10.1136/bmjqs-2015-004465, PMID 26908900](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2015-004465); Schnock 2023 (Drug Saf; NICU observational) — [DOI 10.1007/s40264-023-01365-6, PMID 37889401](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40264-023-01365-6); Kuitunen 2022 (BMC Pediatr; simulation test cases to optimise NICU dose limits) — [DOI 10.1186/s12887-022-03183-8, PMID 35255846](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-022-03183-8); Prusch 2011 (Am J Health Syst Pharm; IV interoperability linking BCMA, eMAR and pumps): drug-library compliance 56.5%→72.1% (telemetry) and 34.1%→58.6% (med-surg); manual pump edits fell ~75%; 32% reduction in reported heparin IV errors — [DOI 10.2146/ajhp100211, PMID 21515868](https://doi.org/10.2146/ajhp100211). Hogerwaard 2023 (BMJ Open Qual) implemented BCMA on OR infusion pumps — [DOI 10.1136/bmjoq-2022-002023](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjoq-2022-002023).

### Inferences
- Outlier-detection CDS is complementary to rule-based LASA/indication alerts: it catches "a drug this patient would never plausibly receive," which is the signature of a wrong-drug order, but published evaluations report validity/usefulness rather than wrong-drug error rates.
- Pill-recognition accuracy on real clinical images is too variable for autonomous verification; its realistic role is as an adjunct to barcode scanning.

### Gaps
- No prospective controlled study shows reduced wrong-drug error incidence attributable to ML CDS.
- No smart-pump study reporting wrong-drug (as opposed to dose/rate) interception was found.
- NLP on free-text orders specifically for wrong-drug detection was not identified beyond Salazar 2019's use of NLP to find indications.

## 9. Systematic reviews of medication-error interventions reporting wrong-drug outcomes

### Takeaway
Broad reviews (Ciapponi 2021 Cochrane, 65 studies; Keers 2014; Berdot 2015; Nuckols 2014; Nguyen 2018; Marufu 2021; Eze 2025) show moderate-certainty reductions in overall medication errors for CPOE/CDS, barcoding and medication reconciliation but almost never report wrong-drug errors as a separate outcome; the LASA-specific reviews (Larmené-Beld 2018; Bryan 2020; Bhat 2026) find only laboratory evidence. Manias 2020, Lapkin 2016 and Khalil could not be located in this session.

### Cited Findings
- Ciapponi 2021 (Cochrane CD009985.pub2; 65 studies—51 RCTs, 14 ITS; 110,875 participants; search 16 Jan 2020): interventions studied were medication reconciliation (most common), electronic prescribing, "barcoding for correct administering of medications", organisational changes, feedback, education, improved dispensing systems; MR vs none OR 0.55 (0.17–1.74) for medication errors (low certainty), ADEs OR 0.38 (0.18–0.80); pharmacist MR OR 0.21 (0.09–0.48); CPOE/CDSS vs paper OR 0.74 (0.31–1.79); improved CPOE/CDSS OR 0.85 (0.74–0.97); prioritised alerts may prevent ADEs — [DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD009985.pub2, PMID 34822165, PMC8614640](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD009985.pub2). (Wrong-drug errors are not a separately reported outcome in the abstract.)
- Keers 2014 (Drug Saf; 6 RCTs + 7 CTs): ADC RR 0.72 (0.53–1.00); CPOE RR 0.51 (0.40–0.66); BCMA-eMAR RR 0.71 (0.53–0.95); simulation education RR 0.17; pharmacist-led training RR 0.76 — [DOI 10.1007/s40264-014-0152-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40264-014-0152-0).
- Berdot 2015 (Int J Nurs Stud; 7 studies, TOE method): pooled OR 0.72 (0.39–1.34), NS; all high risk of bias — [DOI 10.1016/j.ijnurstu.2015.08.012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijnurstu.2015.08.012).
- Nuckols 2014 (CPOE): RR 0.46 for medication errors — [DOI 10.1186/2046-4053-3-56](https://doi.org/10.1186/2046-4053-3-56). Roumeliotis 2019 (JGIM) — [DOI 10.1007/s11606-019-05236-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-019-05236-8).
- Nguyen 2018 (Ther Adv Drug Saf; 102 neonatal studies): >90% reported reductions; median 50–70% reduction across themes; range from +16% to −100%; "no single intervention appeared clearly superior" — [DOI 10.1177/2042098617748868](https://doi.org/10.1177/2042098617748868).
- Marufu 2021 (J Pediatr Nurs; 18 paediatric studies): pooled OR 0.36 (0.21–0.63) — [DOI 10.1016/j.pedn.2021.08.024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pedn.2021.08.024). Eze 2025 (J Clin Nurs; 26 adult studies, 14 pooled): OR 0.70 (0.51–0.97), I² 94%; workflow smart technologies most effective subgroup — [DOI 10.1111/jocn.70109](https://doi.org/10.1111/jocn.70109).
- LASA-specific: Larmené-Beld 2018, Bryan 2020, Bhat 2026 (Section 1); Ostini 2012; Ryan 2023 (Section 6).
- Bryan 2020 (Br J Clin Pharmacol review "The problem of look-alike, sound-alike name errors: drivers and solutions"): LASA prevalence estimates 0.00003–0.0022% of prescriptions, 7% of near misses, 6.2–14.7% of medication error events; solutions include reducing interruptions, typographic tweaks, barcoding and CPOE — [DOI 10.1111/bcp.14285](https://doi.org/10.1111/bcp.14285).

### Inferences
- Because most trials define outcomes as total medication errors or MAEs, the apparent absence of wrong-drug effects in reviews reflects reporting, not proof of no effect; the few studies breaking out wrong-drug errors (DeYoung 2009 null; Küng 2021, Denny 2026, Helmons 2012 positive) are all barcode studies.

### Gaps
- Manias 2020 (Ther Adv Drug Saf), Lapkin 2016 and Khalil reviews were not retrievable via PubMed author searches in this session and are not summarised here.
- No review was found that pools wrong-drug error outcomes across intervention types.
