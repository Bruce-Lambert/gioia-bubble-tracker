## Part A, Q1 — In reporting systems, what proportion of wrong-drug / LASA errors reach the patient and cause harm (NCC MERP E–I)? Are they more or less harmful than other error types?

### Takeaway
Across voluntary reporting systems the share of wrong-drug or name-confusion events that cause harm is low in absolute terms (≈1–2% of reports in MEDMARX-type systems; ≈12–26% of wrong-drug *claims* or *fatal-report* subsets depending on denominator), but wrong-drug is consistently the **second most common error type among fatal medication-error reports** (16% of FDA fatal reports 1993–98) and the **single most common allegation in pharmacist liability claims** (37–52%). Whether wrong-drug errors are "more harmful" than other types depends entirely on the denominator: per report they look no worse than average; per *fatal* event or per *paid claim* they are over-represented.

**FDA AERS fatal medication-error reports (landmark anchor)**

**Are wrong-drug errors more harmful than other types? (direct comparisons)**
- FDA fatal reports: wrong drug = 16% of fatal errors vs ~11–14% of all reports in MEDMARX/PA-PSRS-type systems ("unauthorized/wrong drug" 11% of MEDMARX senior errors 2002; 14% of pediatric MEDMARX errors; 17% of radiology errors 2004) → modest over-representation among deaths (comparison across different databases; interpret cautiously). — Phillips 2001 (above); [EurekAlert MEDMARX seniors](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/527578); [UVA pediatric MEDMARX review](https://med.virginia.edu/pediatrics/wp-content/uploads/sites/237/2015/12/200811.pdf); [Drug Topics radiology report](https://www.drugtopics.com/view/radiologists-take-issue-new-usp-report-drug-errors)
- ISMP 2016 practice-level data (Pharmacy Times coverage): wrong drug accounted for 39% of opioid events, 33% of insulin events, 39% of antithrombotic events, and was the top cause for antibiotics; wrong dose dominated most other classes (snippet). — [Pharmacy Times, Top medication safety errors of 2016](https://www.pharmacytimes.com/view/top-medication-safety-errors-of-2016)
- Liability data: wrong drug is the largest single allegation (36.8%–52%) in pharmacist claims (see Q3), and death was the most common injury (>12% of claims) in HPSO 2023 report — see Q3.

### Inferences
- Harm share per *report* (≈1.4–2%) is driven by the reporting-system mix (mostly near misses); harm share per *wrong-drug event that reached the patient* is unmeasured in most systems — Ro & Jones 2025 is the only recent analysis that explicitly restricts to reached-patient wrong-drug events and should be obtained.
- Over-representation of wrong-drug among fatal reports (Phillips) and paid claims (HPSO/CNA) while under-representation in overall report volume is consistent with wrong-drug errors having a heavier severity tail than dose/omission errors, especially when high-alert classes (NMBAs, opioids, insulin/heparin, chemotherapy) are involved.

### Gaps
- No published analysis found that gives a full NCC MERP A–I distribution specifically for wrong-drug errors from MEDMARX, PA-PSRS 2016–2020, NRLS, FAERS, Danish or Dutch systems. The 2008 USP report (1.4% harm among LASA records) is the closest. PA-PSRS 2023 wrong-drug analysis (Ro & Jones 2025) likely has this but page was blocked.
- ISMP QuarterWatch (FAERS-based) wrong-drug analyses 2015–2017 not located; QuarterWatch was discontinued after 2019–2020 (not verified here).
- No Danish Patient Safety Database or Dutch CMR national LASA/wrong-drug proportion found.
- Dyer C, BMJ 2001;322:257 "Doctors suspended after injecting wrong drug into spine" (vincristine, England) — PMID 11157519, no abstract; cited only as a marker.

## Part A, Q2 — Documented fatal or catastrophic wrong-drug cases that shaped practice and policy (dates, outcomes, citations)

### Takeaway
A small number of sentinel cases — vincristine intrathecal (≈120–130+ deaths worldwide), Emily Jerry (2006), heparin/Hep-Lock infant overdoses (2006–2007), the Vanderbilt vecuronium-for-Versed death (2017) and resulting criminal conviction (2022), and the tranexamic-acid-for-bupivacaine intrathecal series (21 cases to 2019; FDA labeling changes 2020, 2025) — account for most of the regulatory and legislative response to wrong-drug risk in the 2006–2026 period.

### Cited Findings

**Vincristine (and other vinca alkaloids) given intrathecally — wrong route, often syringe/product selection**
- Babaee A et al. Brain & Spine 2025;5:104292 (systematic review): 31 published cases of inadvertent intrathecal vinca alkaloid (29 vincristine, 2 vindesine); **only 6/31 (19.4%) survived the acute phase; 0/12 survived without CSF irrigation vs 6/15 (40%) with irrigation; all survivors had severe neurological deficits** (e.g., paraplegia). — According to PubMed, PMID 40546273, [DOI 10.1016/j.bas.2025.104292](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bas.2025.104292)
- Pongudom S, Chinthammitr Y. J Med Assoc Thai 2011;94 Suppl 1:S258–63: fatal case despite lavage; among 16 reported cases with drainage/irrigation 56.3% survived ≥30 days, 37.5% >6 months. — According to PubMed, PMID 21721457
- ISMP Medication Safety Alert! (Sept 2013) tally: deaths/neurological devastation from intrathecal vinca alkaloids — **prepared in syringes = 120; in minibags = 0**; ISMP 2020–2021 Best Practices cite ">130 cases reported worldwide"; NCCN cites 125 deaths since the 1960s (snippet/secondary). WHO Drug Alert No. 115 (2007) states vincristine should only be given IV via minibag. — [WHO Drug Alert 115 PDF](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/pvg/drug-alerts/da115---alert_115_vincristine.pdf); [ONS "Just Bag It" campaign (NCCN, 2016)](https://oncnursingnews.com/view/just-bag-it-campaign-promotes-safe-vincristine-administration); [ISMP 2020–2021 Best Practices (hosted copy)](https://txchglobalhope.moodlecloud.com/pluginfile.php/1959/mod_label/intro/ISMP%202020-2021%20Best%20Practices%20Recommendations.pdf); [PMC4959612](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4959612)

### Inferences
- The cases with the largest policy footprint share three features: a high-alert drug (NMBA, concentrated electrolyte, heparin, vinca alkaloid, TXA), a storage/selection failure (ADC override, mis-stocked bin, similar vial cap), and a death — rather than name similarity alone. Pure name-confusion cases (Celebrex, Zyprexa, Kapidex, Brintellix) produced renames/relabels but few documented deaths.
- The criminalization pathway (Cropp 2009 → Vaught 2022) produced a counter-reaction (Kentucky 2024) that is itself now a policy outcome of wrong-drug errors.

### Gaps
- No verified fatal case for hydroxyzine/hydralazine, Brevibloc/Brevital, Zyprexa/Zyrtec, or Celebrex/Cerebyx.
- Exact current worldwide vincristine death tally differs by source (120 syringe cases per ISMP 2013; >130 per ISMP 2020; 125 per NCCN).
- Vanderbilt civil settlement terms are confidential; CMS survey report (Nov 2018) not retrieved.

## Part A, Q3 — Costs and legal/liability consequences attributable to wrong-drug or LASA errors

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed estimate isolates the national economic cost of wrong-drug/LASA errors specifically; the available numbers are (a) general medication-error cost estimates (IOM 2006 $3.5 billion hospital-only; NHS England £98.5 million/yr for definitely avoidable ADEs, 1,708 deaths), and (b) liability data where wrong-drug is the leading pharmacist allegation (36.8% of HPSO/CNA paid claims 2012–2016, avg payment $124,407; 43.8% in the 2013 dataset; 52% in a Pharmacists Mutual 500-claim study) and medication cases carry higher death rates than other malpractice (32% vs 18%, CRICO).

### Cited Findings

**Macro cost of medication errors (context; not wrong-drug-specific)**
- IOM, *Preventing Medication Errors* (July 2006): ≥1.5 million preventable ADEs/yr in US; hospital-only cost **$3.5 billion (2006 $)** built from 400,000 in-hospital preventable ADEs × ~$5,857 (=$2.3 billion in 1993 $); Medicare outpatient ADE costs ~$887 million (2000). — [NAP chapter 2](https://books.nap.edu/read/11623/chapter/2); [Senate Finance Committee statement 2006](https://www.finance.senate.gov/chairmans-news/chairman-grassley-comments-on-the-institute-of-medicine-report-on-medication-errors); [Pharmacy Times 2006](https://www.pharmacytimes.com/publications/issue/2006/2006-09/2006-09-5839)
- Elliott RA, Camacho E, Jankovic D, Sculpher MJ, Faria R. BMJ Qual Saf 2021;30(2):96–105 (England): **237 million medication errors/yr**; 38.4% in primary care; 72% little/no harm potential; 66 million potentially clinically significant; definitely avoidable ADEs cost **£98,462,582/yr, 181,626 bed-days, 1,708 deaths** (primary-care ADEs → admission £83.7 m, 627 deaths; secondary care £14.8 m, 1,081 deaths). — According to PubMed, PMID 32527980, [DOI 10.1136/bmjqs-2019-010206](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2019-010206)
- Walsh EK, Hansen CR, Sahm LJ, Kearney PM, Doherty E, Bradley CP. Pharmacoepidemiol Drug Saf 2017;26(5):481–97 (systematic review, 16 studies, 2004–2016): **mean cost per error ranged €2.58 to €111,727.08 (2015 €)**; only 1 study met all quality criteria; 12/16 hospital-based; one study measured litigation costs. — According to PubMed, PMID 28295821, [DOI 10.1002/pds.4188](https://doi.org/10.1002/pds.4188)
- Alrwisan 2011 (above) cites NHS cost of medication incidents "£500 million annually" (secondary citation within abstract). — PMID 21240481.

**Malpractice/liability analyses**
- Rothschild JM, Federico FA, Gandhi TK, Kaushal R, Williams DH, Bates DW. Arch Intern Med 2002;162(21):2414–20: New England insurer 1990–1999; ADEs = **6.3% (129/2,040) of claims**; 73% preventable; **46% life-threatening or fatal**; mean defense cost $64,700–74,200 (non-preventable or outpatient preventable) vs **$376,500 for preventable inpatient ADEs**. — According to PubMed, PMID 12437399, [DOI 10.1001/archinte.162.21.2414](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.162.21.2414)
- CRICO Strategies / Candello 2016 CBS report "Medication-related Malpractice Risks" (3,067 cases, 2010–2014): 1 in 9 malpractice cases involved a medication error; **32% of medication cases involved patient death vs 18% of other cases**; analgesics 18%, anticoagulants 17%, antibiotics 13%; ordering/prescribing 39%, monitoring 56% (overlapping). Wrong-drug share not broken out in summaries. — [CRICO press release May 2017](https://rmfcd2-prod.rmf.harvard.edu/News-and-Blog/Press-Releases-Home/Press-Releases/2017/May/New-report-identifies-opportunities-to-reduce-patient-safety-risks-related-to-medication-error); [AHRQ PSNet record](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/medication-related-malpractice-risks-2016-crico-strategies-national-cbs-report)
- Higden J, Folcarelli P, Schaffer A (CRICO). J Nurs Adm 2024;54(11):631–7: **231 nursing medication-related malpractice cases closed 2017–2021**; administration most frequent; opioids and epinephrine most involved; indemnity paid in 56.3%, **average indemnity $366,002**. Explicitly framed by the Vaught prosecution. — According to PubMed, PMID 39475893, [DOI 10.1097/NNA.0000000000001502](https://doi.org/10.1097/NNA.0000000000001502)
- Reiner G, Pierce SL, Flynn J. J Am Pharm Assoc 2020;60(5):e50–e56 (HPSO/CNA pharmacist professional liability, closed 2012–2016, payment ≥$1): 184 closed claims from 1,264 incidents; **average total payment $124,407; wrong-drug dispensing 36.8% of claims (down from 43.8% in the 2013 dataset); wrong dose 15.3% (down from 31.5%)**. — According to PubMed, PMID 32217085, [DOI 10.1016/j.japh.2020.02.027](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.japh.2020.02.027)
- HPSO 2023 pharmacist claim report: wrong-drug allegations most common; death the most common resulting injury (>12% of claims) (secondary). Pharmacists Mutual study of 500 pharmacist malpractice claims: **wrong drug 52%, wrong strength 27%, wrong directions 7.4%** (secondary law-firm summary; undated; verify with Pharmacists Mutual). — [Insurance Journal Sept 2023](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/national/2023/09/22/741288.htm); [yourlawyer.com summary of Pharmacists Mutual](https://www.yourlawyer.com/lawsuits/pharmacist-malpractice-2/); [Pharmacists Mutual "Not Only How, But Why"](https://phmic.com/?p=6805)
- Anaesthesia claims (UK NHSLA 1995–2007): 93 claims, total £4,915,450; 62 drug-administration-error claims (£4,283,677); half alleged wrong drug (16 NMBAs); respiratory depression requiring ICU cost £2,752,853 across 13 claims. — PMID 19849678 (above).
- Fitzgibbon DR et al. Anesthesiology 2016 (ASA Closed Claims, implantable pain devices): medication administration errors caused death/brain damage in 13 (32%) of IDDS maintenance claims. — PMID 27054366, [DOI 10.1097/ALN.0000000000001122](https://doi.org/10.1097/ALN.0000000000001122)

**Criminalization debate (legal consequences)**
- Cropp (2009) and Vaught (2022) are the two US criminal convictions most cited; commentary in US Pharmacist (2009) and PSQH (2009) argued injustice/system failure; ISMP, ECRI, ANA, AACN (not verified), AORN condemned criminalization 2019–2022; Kentucky HB 159 (2024) first decriminalization statute. See Q2 citations. Dekeseredy 2024 (qualitative) documents nurses' fear of reporting after Vaught (PMID 41661846).

### Inferences
- A defensible back-of-envelope for wrong-drug cost would multiply a wrong-drug share (≈11–20% of reported medication errors; 16% of fatal FDA reports) by general medication-error cost estimates — but no source does this, so any such number is an inference, not a citation.
- Liability data suggest wrong-drug errors are disproportionately *paid* claims in pharmacy (≥1/3 to 1/2), implying higher per-event cost than their share of incident reports.

### Gaps
- No economic estimate specific to LASA/wrong-drug errors in FDA naming guidance, Lambert's papers, IOM 2006, Elliott 2021 or Walsh 2017.
- Studdert malpractice analyses (e.g., NEJM 2006) not retrieved; not medication-specific.
- CRICO wrong-drug percentage not available from summaries; full CBS report would be needed.

## Part B, Q1 — FDA: DMEPA, proprietary name review and its evolution, POCA, Name Differentiation Project, labeling guidance, post-marketing renames, biologic suffixes, shortages

### Takeaway
FDA's wrong-drug program evolved from the 2001 Name Differentiation Project (Tall Man letters for 16 generic pairs) through the PDUFA IV era (2007–2012: 2008 "Contents of a Complete Submission" draft and 2008 pilot concept paper, both finalized 2016/abandoned 2011), the 2014 draft and **Dec 2020 final "Best Practices in Developing Proprietary Names"** guidance (with public POCA), the **2013 draft/May 2022 final container-label guidance**, and the **Jan 2017 biologics four-letter suffix** policy; post-marketing renames (Kapidex 2010, Brintellix 2016) and the TXA labeling actions (2020, 2025) are the clearest examples of FDA responding to reported wrong-drug errors.

### Cited Findings
- **Name Differentiation Project (2001)**: FDA Office of Generic Drugs, March–May 2001, issued **142 letters covering 159 applications** asking manufacturers of **16 look-alike generic name pairs** to adopt Tall Man lettering voluntarily; FDA page now titled "FDA List of Established Drug Names Recommended to Use Tall Man Lettering"; a 2023 analysis counts 21 generic-generic pairs on the FDA list as of Nov 2020. — [FDA Name Differentiation Project](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-errors-related-cder-regulated-drug-products/name-differentiation-project); [archived 2008 FDA page](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20090117013029/http:/www.fda.gov/cder/drug/MedErrors/nameDiff.htm); [Springer 2023 analysis (Ther Innov Regul Sci)](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s43441-023-00526-0)
- **Rejection rates**: FDA "historically rejected about a third" of proposed proprietary names (2006, RPM Report); CDER approved 86% of names in first half FY2015. Sponsors may submit up to 2 names per application; target review ~90 days (sponsor slide deck; PDUFA goal letters govern). — [Pink Sheet 2006 "Name of the Game"](https://pink.pharmaintelligence.informa.com/articles/2006/12/01/the-name-of-the-game-adjusting-to-a-tougher-fda-brand-name-review-process); [Pink Sheet "FDA Rejecting Fewer Proprietary Names"](https://pink.citeline.com/PS079808/FDA-Rejecting-Fewer-Proprietary-Names); [Sanofi presentation to EMA](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/presentation/fda-practices-j-sanit-hugot_en.pdf)
- **PDUFA IV (FDAAA signed 27 Sept 2007)** committed FDA to a proprietary-name pilot: concept paper "PDUFA Pilot Project — Proprietary Name Review" draft for 5–6 June 2008 meeting; final published 7 Oct 2008 (73 FR 58604; docket FDA-2008-N-0281); enrollment from FY2009; **2011 FR notice: no results meeting because of "insufficient participation."** — [FDA concept paper page](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/pdufa-pilot-project-proprietary-name-review-concept-paper); [FR 7 Oct 2008](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2008-10-07/html/E8-23715.htm); [FR 11 Oct 2011](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2011-10-11/pdf/2011-26099.pdf)
- **"Contents of a Complete Submission for the Evaluation of Proprietary Names"**: draft Nov 2008 (FR 24 Nov 2008); **final 6 Apr 2016** (a PDUFA IV goal); an HHS-hosted "Jan 2025 update" file exists (changes not verified). — [FDA guidance page](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/contents-complete-submission-evaluation-proprietary-names); [FR 24 Nov 2008](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2008-11-24/html/E8-27896.htm); [ProPharma summary Apr 2016](https://www.propharmagroup.com/fda-complete-submission-content-proposed-proprietary-name/); [HHS Jan 2025 file](https://www.hhs.gov/guidance/sites/default/files/hhs-guidance-documents/FDA/8945fnl_Jan-2025-update.pdf)
- **"Best Practices in Developing Proprietary Names for Drugs"**: draft May 2014 (covered Rx and OTC); **final for human prescription drugs 8 Dec 2020** (docket FDA-2014-D-0622) issued with a separate OTC draft; describes DMEPA safety review (misbranding review by OPDP/OND/ONPD); applies to all human Rx products; does not address established/proper names. — [FDA guidance page (Rx final)](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/best-practices-developing-proprietary-names-human-prescription-drug-products-guidance-industry); [FDA In Brief 8 Dec 2020](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/fda-brief/fda-brief-fda-provides-guidances-proprietary-naming-drugs-changes-naming-analysis-program); [FDA PDF](https://fda.gov/media/139634/download); [Policy & Medicine on 2014 draft](https://policymed.com/2014/05/fda-draft-guidance-best-practices-in-developing-proprietary-names-for-drugs.html)

## Part B, Q6 — Pharmacy-practice regulation: state boards, NABP, USP <17>, USP <1066>, indication-on-label movement

### Takeaway
Pharmacy-practice regulation addressing wrong-drug risk is diffuse: USP General Chapter <17> (Prescription Container Labeling, USP 36–NF 31, Nov 2012) standardizes patient-centred labels and allows the purpose for use when the prescriber includes it; the "indication on prescription" movement (Schiff NEJM 2016 "sixth right") has evidence that indications discriminate 59% of LASA pairs and intercept name-confusion errors at CPOE, but uptake is ≈7% of outpatient prescriptions; no state board rule text on LASA storage or NABP Model Act LASA language was retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- **USP <17> Prescription Container Labeling**: published in USP 36–NF 31 (Nov 2012), following a 2009 advisory panel; patient-centred organization (patient name, drug name/strength, directions prominent); explicit timing instead of "take as directed"; **purpose for use included when prescriber provides it unless patient prefers otherwise**; larger fonts, sentence case, high contrast; cited 46% of patients misunderstanding ≥1 label instruction. — [USP <17> page](https://usp.org/health-quality-safety/usp-nf-general-chapter-prescription-container-labeling); [EurekAlert 2012](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/518091); [PSQH news](https://www.psqh.com/news/pharmacies-across-the-country-urged-to-adopt-patient-centered-labels/)
- **Indication-based prescribing**: Schiff GD, Seoane-Vazquez E, Wright A. "Incorporating Indications into Medication Ordering — Time to Enter the Age of Reason." NEJM 2016;375(4):306 — indication as "sixth right"; narrows drug/form/dose choices and reduces wrong-drug risk. Cheng et al. JAMIA 2018: of 278 ISMP LASA pairs, **59% had no overlap and 21% partial overlap** in high-level indications (20% complete overlap, mostly same-ingredient pairs) — PMID 29800453, [DOI 10.1093/jamia/ocy043](https://doi.org/10.1093/jamia/ocy043). Galanter 2014: indication alerts intercepted 1.4 name-confusion errors per 1,000 alerts — PMID 25025346. 2019 analysis: indications on only **7.41% of 4 million outpatient prescriptions** (secondary). ISMP supports indication on labels (e.g., "for mood") to help pharmacists catch LASA errors; objections: label crowding, privacy. — [US Pharmacist Aug 2016](https://www.uspharmacist.com/article/project-seeks-to-include-medication-purpose-on-prescriptions); [Betsy Lehman Center](https://betsylehmancenterma.gov/news/overcoming-barriers-to-indication-based-prescribing); [PMC6487970](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6487970); [Patient Safety Solutions Mar 2022](https://www.patientsafetysolutions.com/docs/March_1_2022_Including_the_Indication_on_Prescriptions.htm)
- USP 2008 MEDMARX LASA report also recommended adding indication to prescriptions, prohibiting verbal orders for locally confused sound-alikes, and Tall Man lettering in software/labels. — [MDedge "USP asks physicians' help"](https://mdedge.com/content/usp-asks-physicians-help-heading-medication-errors)
- **Emily's Law (Ohio 2009)** — technician competency/background checks (see Part A Q2) is the clearest state-law response to a wrong-product compounding death.
- **Automated dispensing cabinets**: Ruutiainen et al. Eur J Hosp Pharm 2021 (Finnish ICU ADC): **~70% (355/488) of ADC items had LASA risk; 20% of those were high-alert; 16% stored unsafely adjacent** — PMID 33452109, [DOI 10.1136/ejhpharm-2020-002531](https://doi.org/10.1136/ejhpharm-2020-002531). AJN 2025 alert on LASA errors while restocking ADCs — PMID 41128571. Lorenzi et al. 2026 on plastic KCl ampoules LASA — PMID 41698798.
- **CPOE display**: Quist et al. AJHP 2017 found inconsistent brand/generic name display across 10 CPOE systems at 6 sites, creating selection/duplication risk — PMID 28336760, [DOI 10.2146/ajhp151051](https://doi.org/10.2146/ajhp151051). Adelman et al. JAMA 2019 RCT (wrong-patient, not wrong-drug) — PMID 31087021.
- **Machine-learning CDSS**: Chen et al. 2023 (Taiwan academic hospital, 1,206,895 outpatient orders): 28,536 alerts (2.36%), 48.9% accepted, 470 intercepted errors (16.4 per 1,000 alerted orders), including LASA errors — PMID 37924770, [DOI 10.1016/j.cmpb.2023.107869](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmpb.2023.107869)

### Inferences
- The most evidence-backed wrong-drug countermeasures in pharmacy practice are informational (indication on order/label) and physical (segregated storage, minibags, cap/label differentiation) rather than typographic.

### Gaps
- No state board of pharmacy administrative-code text on LASA storage separation, NABP Model Act LASA provisions, or USP <1066> (Physical Environments That Promote Safe Medication Use) content was retrieved; hospital policies citing "state rules may be stricter" were found but not the rules themselves.
