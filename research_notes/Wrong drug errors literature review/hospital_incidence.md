# Incidence and Prevalence of Wrong-Drug Medication Errors in Hospital Inpatient Settings (2006–2026, with pre-2006 landmarks)

Scope note: hospital inpatient only (general wards, inpatient pharmacy dispensing, nursing administration). Anesthesia/OR/ICU/ED, community pharmacy, long-term care and ambulatory are covered by other researchers; a few ICU/outpatient-pharmacy figures appear below only where they come from the same dataset as inpatient figures or are needed as context, and are flagged. "Wrong drug" is coded inconsistently across sources: ASHP/NCC MERP observational taxonomies usually fold it into "unauthorized drug" (drug given with no valid order, which also captures wrong-patient doses), incident-reporting systems use "wrong drug/medicine", and dispensing studies use "wrong drug/incorrect drug/drug-selection error". The coding used by each source is stated with each figure. Sources were retrieved via PubMed (metadata and PMC full text), Firecrawl and web search; all PubMed-derived facts are attributed to PubMed records with DOI links.

## Key Question 0 (baseline): What did the seminal pre-2006 studies establish about wrong-drug error frequency?

### Takeaway
The landmark 1990s–early-2000s studies established that most hospital medication errors are dosing, omission and timing errors; wrong/unauthorized-drug errors were a small minority of observed administration errors (about 4% of errors in Barker 2002) and a small share of errors causing preventable ADEs, but dispensing-stage errors (where wrong-drug is the leading type) were systematically under-detected by chart review and incident reports.

### Cited Findings
- Bates 1995 (JAMA 274:29-34; USA; 4,031 adult admissions, 11 units, 2 tertiary hospitals, 6 months): 247 ADEs and 194 potential ADEs; extrapolated 6.5 ADEs and 5.5 potential ADEs per 100 non-obstetric admissions; errors resulting in preventable ADEs occurred at ordering 56%, administration 34%, transcription 6%, dispensing 4%; 48% of ordering-stage errors were intercepted vs 0% at administration. PMID 7791255 — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7791255/)

### Inferences
- In the US observational baseline (Barker 2002), unauthorized/wrong-drug errors were roughly 4% of administration errors, or about 0.75% of doses (4% x 19%), an order of magnitude below wrong-time and omission errors; later observational reviews (Berdot 2013, below) reproduce this pattern.
- Because dispensing errors are the stage where wrong-drug is the dominant error type and are almost never captured by chart review or incident reports (Flynn 2002, Beso 2005), reporting-system shares of wrong-drug errors likely understate their true frequency.

### Gaps
- The full MEDMARX type distribution for all hospital records (not just seniors) was not retrievable from a primary document in this session; the 11% "unauthorized drug" figure is from the USP press release and applies to the 65+ subgroup.

## Key Question 1: What proportion of hospital medication errors are wrong-drug errors in each major reporting system and in direct-observation studies?

### Takeaway
Across systems, wrong-drug/wrong-medicine errors are consistently a secondary category: roughly 3-11% of voluntarily reported hospital medication incidents (8.8% of all NHS England medication incidents in 2017; about 2.9% of PA-PSRS medication error reports coded "wrong drug" in 2023; 11% "unauthorized drug" in MEDMARX 2002 seniors' records; 11% "another drug than prescribed" in a Norwegian hospital database), but 19-23% of reported hospital dispensing errors (Scotland, London). In direct observation, "unauthorized drug" (the ASHP proxy) has a median rate of 0.3% of total opportunities for error, i.e., typically 2-8% of observed administration errors.

### Inferences
- Observational data converge on a wrong/unauthorized-drug administration rate of about 0.2-1% of doses (Berdot median 0.3% TOE; Westbrook 0.4/100; Tesar 0.44%; Barker ~0.75%), which is 3-8% of observed administration errors. Reporting systems give a higher share (8.8% NRLS all stages; 11% Norway), plausibly because wrong-drug events are more salient and more often reach the patient than wrong-time errors.
- The stage mix matters: in dispensing-specific report sets wrong drug is the leading type (19-23%), whereas in prescribing-stage serious events wrong drug is below wrong patient (<4.4%, PA 2016).
- Hospital-affiliated outpatient pharmacy reports (PA 2017, 19.6%) are comparable to inpatient dispensing report sets but reach the patient far more often (89% vs the ~20% undetected share in Cina 2006).

### Gaps
- Keers 2013 full text (the 91-study review) could not be retrieved; its table of wrong-drug subtype frequency per study is not reproduced here.
- No primary document gave the all-record wrong-drug share for MEDMARX (1998-2008) or for ISMP MERP overall; ISMP figures are second-hand (Pharmacy Times) and class-specific.
- Cousins 2012 and Härkänen 2019/2021 abstracts do not report the wrong-drug/wrong-medicine category count for NRLS; the only NRLS wrong-medicine proportion found is the 2017 FOI figure (8.8%).
- No Danish Patient Safety Database (DPSD) analysis with a wrong-drug breakdown was found; no Japanese national (JQ) or Chinese national wrong-drug proportion was found.

## Key Question 2: What are the wrong-drug error rates at pharmacy dispensing and at nursing administration?

### Takeaway
Hospital pharmacy dispensing error rates cluster around 1.5-3.6% of doses/items (pooled 1.6% across settings), of which about 0.02-0.75% leave the pharmacy undetected; wrong drug is the single most common dispensing error type in essentially every study (14/14 in Aldhwaihi's review) and accounts for ~36% of undetected potential ADEs (Cina 2006). At nursing administration, wrong/unauthorized drug occurs in about 0.2-1% of doses observed.

### Cited Findings
Pharmacy dispensing
- Cina 2006 (Jt Comm J Qual Patient Saf 32:73-80; Brigham and Women's, 735-bed academic hospital; direct observation, 7 months): 140,755 technician-filled doses observed; 3.6% (5,075) contained errors; pharmacists detected 79% at verification, so 0.75% of doses would have left pharmacy with undetected errors; 23.5% of undetected errors were potential ADEs (28% serious, 0.8% life-threatening); most common potential ADE types: incorrect medication 36%, incorrect strength 35%, incorrect dosage form 21%. PMID 16568920 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/s1553-7250(06)32010-7). Aldhwaihi 2016 restates Cina's prevented dispensing error rate as 2.9% and unprevented as 0.57%. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2147/IPRP.S95733)
- Poon 2006 (Ann Intern Med 145:426-34; same hospital; before/after barcode-assisted dispensing): 115,164 pre and 253,984 post doses observed; target potential ADEs fell 74% and all potential ADEs 63%; configurations scanning every dose cut target dispensing errors 93-96% and potential ADEs 86-97%; the configuration scanning only one of several identical doses cut target dispensing errors only 60% and had a 2.4-fold increase in target potential ADEs (IV dopamine/heparin events). ### Inferences
- Dispensing: a 3.6% gross error rate with ~36% of undetected potential ADEs being wrong medication (Cina 2006) implies on the order of 1-2 undetected wrong-drug/strength/form doses per 1,000 doses leaving a large hospital pharmacy before barcode scanning; Poon 2006 suggests full-scan barcoding removes >90% of these.
- Administration: the wrong-drug administration rate (~0.3-0.5% of doses) is remarkably stable across countries and decades (US 2002, France 2012, Australia 2006-2010, Czech Republic 2021), suggesting it is driven by irreducible selection/identification failures rather than local practice.

### Gaps
- Bohand 2009 and most unit-dose dispensing studies do not report the "unauthorized/wrong drug" count in the abstract; full texts were not retrieved.
- Direct-observation studies rarely separate "wrong drug" from "unauthorized drug"/"wrong patient"; only Westbrook 2020 and Tesar 2025 do so explicitly.

## Key Question 3: How did wrong-drug error rates change after CPOE, barcode medication administration, automated dispensing cabinets or EHR adoption? Did wrong-drug errors specifically fall?

### Takeaway
Barcode verification at the bedside (Poon 2010) and in the pharmacy (Poon 2006) is the only intervention with direct evidence of large wrong-drug reductions (wrong-medication administration errors fell 57%, from 1.0% to 0.4% of administrations; target dispensing errors fell 93-96%); electronic medication systems without barcoding did not reduce wrong-drug administration (Westbrook 2020: 0.4 to 0.5 per 100), and CPOE both reduces handwriting-related wrong-drug prescribing and creates new drop-down/pick-list wrong-drug selection errors.

### Cited Findings
- Poon 2010 (N Engl J Med 362:1698-707; Brigham and Women's; 14,041 administrations, 3,082 transcriptions; 35 adult units): nontiming administration errors 11.5% (776/6,723) without vs 6.8% (495/7,318) with barcode eMAR (41.4% relative reduction); potential ADEs 3.1% to 1.6% (50.8% reduction); transcription errors 6.1% to 0. PMID 20445181 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMsa0907115). Subtype data from the same paper, as tabulated by Shah et al. (Can J Hosp Pharm 2016): wrong medication 1.0% to 0.4% (RRR 57.4%, 95% CI 39.2-79.3%, p<0.001), wrong dose 2.0% to 1.1% (RRR 41.9%), wrong route 0.3% to 0.1% (RRR 68%), documentation errors 2.9% to 0.6%; wrong-time 16.7% to 12.2%. — [CJHP PDF](https://www.cjhp-online.ca/index.php/cjhp/article/download/1594/2418/9914)
- Poon 2006 (pharmacy barcode dispensing): 93-96% reduction in target dispensing errors with all-dose scanning; only 60% with single-dose scanning and 2.4x increase in target potential ADEs. PMID 16983130 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-145-6-200609190-00006)

### Inferences
- Wrong-drug administration errors are specifically responsive to bedside barcode verification (Poon 2010) but not to e-prescribing/eMAR alone (Westbrook 2020), consistent with the mechanism (product-order match at the point of care). Where wrong-drug errors originate upstream of the scan (ward preparation, Sakushima 2015; ADC pocket misfills), BCMA does not catch them.
- Reporting-system studies cannot quantify whether CPOE lowered wrong-drug prescribing; they document that CPOE introduces pick-list/drop-down wrong-drug selection as a new error mode (PA 2016, Westbrook 2013, Cheung 2013, Schiff 2015).

### Gaps
- No study was found that reports wrong-drug prescribing order rates before vs after CPOE with a per-order denominator; the CPOE evidence is either overall-error RRs (Keers 2014) or qualitative failure-mode analyses.
- Franklin 2007 and Helmons 2009 wrong-drug subtype counts were not retrievable (full texts not accessed).

## Key Question 4: What do large-scale electronic detection studies (retract-and-reorder, indication-based algorithms, Pennsylvania CPOE reports) show about wrong-drug order frequency?

### Takeaway
Electronic detection has quantified wrong-patient orders precisely (roughly 40-185 retract-and-reorder events per 100,000 orders, highest in inpatient and NICU settings) but wrong-drug orders only indirectly: the Lambert/Schiff indication-mismatch algorithm triggered on 0.5% of 488,481 orders with a 12.1% PPV (61 confirmed LASA errors), and the authors cite an underlying wrong-drug rate of "roughly one per thousand prescriptions"; a validated wrong-drug-specific RAR measure with published rates was not found.

### Cited Findings
- Adelman 2013 (JAMIA 20:305-10; Montefiore, 4 hospitals): the Wrong-Patient Retract-and-Reorder (RAR) tool identified 170 of 223 events as wrong-patient orders (PPV 76.2%, 95% CI 70.6-81.9%); an estimated 5,246 electronic orders were placed on wrong patients in 2009; in an RCT of 901,776 ordering sessions, ID-reentry reduced RAR odds (OR 0.60, 0.50-0.71). PMID 22753810 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/amiajnl-2012-001055)
- Adelman 2019 (JAMA 321:1780-7; 3,356 clinicians; 12,140,298 orders in 4,486,631 order sessions): wrong-patient RAR sessions per 100,000: overall 90.7 (restricted) vs 88.0 (unrestricted); inpatient 185.6 vs 185.1; ED 157.8 vs 161.3; outpatient 7.9 vs 8.2; no effect of limiting open records. PMID 31087021 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2019.3698)
- Adelman 2017 (Pediatrics 139(5); NICU vs non-NICU paediatric units; >850,000 NICU orders, >3.5 million non-NICU orders, 7 years): baseline wrong-patient orders 117.2 vs 74.9 per 100,000 orders (OR 1.56); ID reentry reduced NICU rate to 60.2 and combined with distinct newborn naming to 45.6 per 100,000. PMID 37924770 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmpb.2023.107869)

### Inferences
- If wrong-drug orders occur at ~1 per 1,000 orders (Lambert 2019's working estimate) and wrong-patient orders at ~0.9-1.9 per 1,000 inpatient order sessions (Adelman 2019), the two near-miss types are of similar magnitude in CPOE, yet only wrong-patient has an NQF-endorsed automated measure.
- Automated indication-mismatch detection finds wrong-drug errors invisible to incident reporting (31% of confirmed pairs never previously published), but low PPV (12-17%) limits it to a trigger tool.

### Gaps
- No published, validated "wrong-drug retract-and-reorder" measure with incidence per 100,000 orders was found in PubMed or Firecrawl searches; Devin 2022's adapted RAR did not break out wrong-drug.
- The Galanter 2014 PLoS One interception figures come from a search summary; the primary article was not opened.

## Key Question 5: What do 2020-2026 studies (EHR-era, COVID-era, large multi-hospital) show about wrong-drug rates?

### Takeaway
Recent direct-observation studies in fully electronic hospitals (Australia 2020, Netherlands 2022, Czech Republic 2025) still find wrong/unordered-drug administration in roughly 0.4-1.1% of doses, and 2023-2026 reporting-system analyses (Pennsylvania 450 verified wrong-drug events in one year; Saudi, Korean, Norwegian systems) continue to show dose errors dominating with wrong drug a persistent minority dominated by insulin, antibiotics, electrolytes and opioids.

- Ro & Jones 2025 (PA-PSRS 2023): 450 verified wrong-drug events reaching patients from 127 facilities; insulin 10.3% of involved medications; 97.3% incidents, 12 serious events. — [DOI 10.33940/001c.134046](https://doi.org/10.33940/001c.134046)

### Inferences
- No 2020-2026 study shows a secular decline in the wrong-drug administration rate relative to pre-EHR observational baselines (0.4-0.5/100 in 2020-2025 vs ~0.75/100 in Barker 2002 and 1.0/100 in Poon's 2010 paper-MAR baseline), although hospitals that use barcode scanning at the bedside were under-represented among the recent observational studies (Westbrook's EMS had no barcoding; Jessurun's hospitals had barcode only for parenterals in one site).
- COVID-era-specific wrong-drug incidence data were not found; the one 2021 EHR audit-log study (Devin) does not isolate wrong-drug.

### Gaps
- No multi-hospital US direct-observation study of wrong-drug administration rates published 2020-2026 was identified; US evidence in this period is reporting-system based (PA-PSRS).
- No study explicitly comparing pandemic vs pre-pandemic wrong-drug rates in inpatients was found.

## Key Question 6 (supplementary): Pediatric inpatient and neonatal wrong-drug data

### Takeaway
Paediatric and neonatal inpatient medication error rates are high (prescribing 13-28% of orders; administration 19-37% of doses on wards and up to 59-68% in NICUs), but every paediatric review identifies dosing (often 10-fold) as the dominant error type; wrong-drug is rarely quantified separately, while wrong-patient order errors are measurably more frequent in NICUs (117 vs 75 per 100,000 orders).

### Cited Findings
- Ghaleb 2006 (Ann Pharmacother 40:1766-76; systematic review, 32 studies): dosing error (often 10-fold) the most common type; antibiotics and sedatives most involved. PMID 16985096 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1345/aph.1G717)
- Ghaleb 2010 (Arch Dis Child 95:113-8; 5 London hospitals, 11/10 wards): 391 prescribing errors = 13.2% of orders (95% CI 12.0-14.5); 429 MAEs = 19.1% of administrations (17.5-20.7); incomplete prescriptions most common prescribing error; drug-preparation errors most common MAE. PMID 20133327 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/adc.2009.158485)
- Alghamdi 2019 (Drug Saf 42:1423-36; 35 PICU/NICU studies 2000-2019): PICU median 14.6 errors per 100 orders (IQR 5.7-48.8) and 6.4-9.1 per 1,000 patient-days; NICU 4-35.1 per 1,000 patient-days and 5.5-77.9 per 100 orders; dosing errors most frequent subtype; anti-infectives most involved. PMID 31410745 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40264-019-00856-9)
- Gates 2019 (Drug Saf 42:1329-42; 71 studies): most studies assessed prescribing; electronic charts generally lower error prevalence. PMID 31290127 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40264-019-00850-1)
- Hicks 2006 (J Pediatr Nurs 21:290-8; MEDMARX 5-year pediatric harmful errors): 816 harmful outcomes involving 242 medications; 11 medications accounted for 34.5%; wrong dosing and omission common. PMID 16843213 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pedn.2006.02.002)
- Stavroudis 2010 (J Perinatol 30:459-68; MEDMARX NICU 1999-2005): 6,749 NICU error reports from 163 facilities; administration ~half; prescribing-phase and high-alert-drug errors more likely harmful. PMID 20043010 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/jp.2009.186)
- Alexander 2009 (Pediatrics 124:324-32; MEDMARX pediatric cardiovascular 2003-2004): 821 reports; improper dosing at administration most common; 4% harmful. PMID 19564316 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2008-2073)
- Alghamdi 2021 NRLS NICU/PICU; Henry Basil 2022/2024 NICU; Westbrook 2024, Li 2024, Mumford 2025 paediatric wards; Chen 2026 meta-analysis (see KQ5).
- Adelman 2017 and 2019 NICU wrong-patient RAR rates (see KQ4).
- Toni 2020 (Klin Padiatr 233:10-6; German children's hospital internal reports 2000-2014): 229 drug-related-problem reports; 72.5% medication errors; 44.5% at dispensing (especially patient confusion and wrong dose), 38.0% administration, 11.4% prescribing; 61.4% of medication errors were dispensing errors. PMID 32851632 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1055/a-1161-9151)
- Liu 2023 (Taiwan children's hospital): 255 errors per 2,591,596 prescriptions; wrong dose 56.9%. PMID 36728168 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/PTS.0000000000001087)

### Inferences
- In paediatrics, the absolute wrong-drug rate is probably similar to adults (not separately measured), but its share of errors is smaller because dose/preparation/rate errors are so frequent, particularly for IV drugs.

### Gaps
- No paediatric or neonatal inpatient study reporting a wrong-drug (different active ingredient) rate per order or per dose was identified; paediatric wrong-drug evidence is limited to case series within MEDMARX class analyses.
