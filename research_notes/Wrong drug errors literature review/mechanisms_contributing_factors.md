## Key Question 1. Psycholinguistic and cognitive mechanisms: how orthographic similarity, phonological similarity, frequency/familiarity, neighborhood structure and context produce name confusion (laboratory and epidemiological evidence)

### Takeaway
A three-decade program (chiefly Bruce Lambert and colleagues, with Filik/Darker/Gerrett/Gale in the UK, Schell, Irwin, and Or/Wang/Liu in Hong Kong) shows that wrong-drug errors behave like ordinary word-recognition errors: objectively computed orthographic and phonological similarity is a dose-dependent risk factor (odds ratios up to several hundred-fold at high similarity), rare/unfamiliar names are perceived and remembered worse than frequent ones, and when a substitution occurs it is almost always toward the more frequently prescribed "neighbor" (frequency bias). Laboratory memory/perception error rates for name pairs and for individual names significantly predict real-world pharmacy error rates (37–45% of variance; r = 0.50), validating these mechanisms as causal rather than merely correlational artifacts of reporting.

### Inferences
- The convergent picture is a lexical-access account: a degraded or ambiguous input (blurred/handwritten/spoken-in-noise/briefly glimpsed name) activates a neighborhood of similar stored names, and the most frequent/familiar candidate wins — explaining both the similarity dose–response and the directional frequency bias (rare → common substitutions).
- Because similarity helps short-term recall but hurts discrimination (Lambert 2003 AJHP), interventions that act only on perception (capitalization, colour) can leave the memory-based component of confusion untouched; this is why Tall Man effects are inconsistent across tasks.
- Nomenclature policy (INN stems, prefix/suffix sharing, trade-dress "A/V/X/Z" letter preferences) structurally generates dense neighborhoods, so confusability is partly designed into the lexicon rather than being purely a user failing.

## Key Question 2. Handwriting/illegibility, verbal and telephone orders, and abbreviations as inputs that degrade the signal

### Takeaway
Degraded input modalities are the classic proximal trigger: misreading the prescription was the single largest cause of community-pharmacy dispensing incidents in the UK (24.5%), handwritten names were misidentified by pharmacists at high rates even with no time pressure, spoken names are confused as a function of noise and familiarity, and roughly 5% of 643,151 MEDMARX reports were attributable to abbreviations. Verbal orders remain under-studied relative to the concern they generate.

### Inferences
- Handwriting and speech are noisy channels; the lab data (Lambert 2003, 2010) show the error is not random but biased toward frequent neighbors — so illegibility converts to wrong-drug errors most dangerously for rare drugs with common look-alike neighbors.
- Ashcroft's "selecting the previous drug from the patient medication record" (11.4%) is an early documented instance of a *computer-mediated* selection slip in pharmacy software, foreshadowing the drop-down phenomena in KQ3.

### Gaps
- No controlled study quantifying the proportion of verbal-order errors that are sound-alike wrong-drug errors was retrieved (Wakefield 2009 QSHC analysed presence of sound-alike drugs in verbal orders but the quantitative result was not available in the abstract).
- Modern (2015–2026) quantitative studies of handwriting-attributable wrong-drug errors are sparse, consistent with the shift to e-prescribing.

## Key Question 3. Technology-induced wrong-drug selection: CPOE/e-prescribing pick lists and search, automated dispensing cabinets, barcode workarounds, pharmacy storage, dispensing robots, and system interfaces

### Takeaway
Electronic systems replaced illegibility with selection slips: in audited hospital e-prescribing, 42% of prescribing errors were system-related and 43% of those were drop-down selection errors; across US CPOE reports, "incorrect medication" is the second most common ordering error (16.3% of 12,830 NPSD reports); and a few typed letters ("met") can surface look-alike names adjacent on screen — ISMP judged that such wrong-selection errors may now rival or exceed those from handwritten orders. The same mechanisms (alphabetical adjacency, truncated names, inconsistent brand/generic display, system "confirmation" of the wrong pick, overrides, unscannable barcodes) recur in ADCs, BCMA and pharmacy robotics.

### Inferences
- The dominant technology-induced mechanism is a *selection slip* in a list whose ordering is alphabetical (hence similarity-sorted): the user's intended name and its orthographic neighbor are adjacent, the match is judged on the first few letters, and the system then validates the choice (printing a label, opening a drawer, populating a dose) — giving false confirmation and removing the cross-check that a pharmacist's reading of a full handwritten name once provided.
- The Leapfrog tool measures decision-support interception of unsafe *orders* (interactions, dosing, allergies), not whether the right drug was selected; hence large national datasets on CPOE "safety performance" say little about wrong-drug selection, which instead shows up in incident databases (NPSD 16.3% "incorrect medication") and chart audits (Westbrook selection errors).
- Indication-based alerts intercept wrong-drug errors because they reintroduce clinical context at the moment of selection; the 59%-no-indication-overlap figure (Cheng 2018) sets an upper bound on how many LASA pairs context could in principle disambiguate.

## Key Question 4. Packaging, labeling and product appearance: look-alike containers, vial caps, label design, and generic substitution / appearance changes

### Takeaway
Packaging and labeling confusion is estimated to account for about one-third of medication errors (vs ~25% for names) in US reporting data, and name confusion is "compounded" when packaging, strength, route or indication are also similar. Human-factors analyses show injectable labels frequently fail design standards, manufacturers' typographic treatments of names are unstandardized, and colour (not shape) differentiation drives faster, more accurate strength identification. For patients, the generic-substitution regime that lets bioequivalent pills vary in colour/shape creates a distinct appearance-based confusion pathway with measurable effects on persistence (OR 1.27–1.66) and perceived wrong-drug receipt (21%).

## Key Question 5. Human-factors and organizational contributors: interruptions, workload/staffing, fatigue, inexperience, confirmation bias, handoffs, multitasking, and error taxonomies (Reason, Rasmussen, HFACS, SHERPA)

### Takeaway
Direct-observation studies quantify the environmental drivers of selection slips: each interruption raises nurses' clinical administration error risk by ~12.7% and doubles major-error risk at four interruptions; interruptions roughly double ambulatory dispensing error rates; and workload, staffing, lighting and LASA stock are the recurrent contributors across dispensing-error reviews. Analyses using Reason's framework classify wrong-drug picks mainly as skill-based slips (picking the neighbor) or assumption-based mistakes, with confirmation bias ("seeing what you expect") as the cognitive glue — the system (ADC, label, screen) then confirms the expectation.

### Inferences
- Under Reason's scheme most wrong-drug picks are skill-based slips (right intention, wrong object) occurring in a visually dense field; interruptions and workload raise slip probability by consuming the working memory needed to hold the intended name and compare it against the candidate; confirmation bias then prevents the post-selection check from catching the slip — which is why experience offers little protection (Westbrook 2010).
- The organizational layer (alphabetical storage policies, override culture, staffing, vendor contracts, trade-dress law) supplies the latent conditions that make these slips frequent and recurrent.

### Gaps
- No HFACS or Rasmussen-specific analysis of a large wrong-drug incident series (other than Patel 2026 neuraxial and the Grissinger override advisory) was identified; most RCA-type evidence is embedded in general dispensing-error studies.
- Fatigue/shift-length effects specific to wrong-drug selection (as opposed to medication errors in general) were not isolated in any retrieved study.
- Handoff/communication contributions to wrong-drug errors were present only as themes (Aldhwaihi 2016; McMullan 2024 unprofessional-behaviour reports mention "inadequate information during handover," PMID 38597879) without wrong-drug-specific quantification.

## Key Question 6. 2020–2026 developments: eye-tracking of drug-name reading, AI/NLP similarity models, EHR usability studies of medication search, and the Leapfrog evaluation tool

### Takeaway
Recent work refines rather than overturns the mechanistic account: eye-tracking shows capitalized name segments and location/photo cues change gaze behaviour and cut selection errors (e.g., 5.3%→0.7% in ICU syringe selection), and that "visual invasion" by nearby non-target drugs precedes dispensing errors; AI/NLP work has moved from single similarity metrics to combined/learned measures (21–25 features, RNN classifiers) and to real-time ML decision support that intercepts LASA errors (16.4 interceptions per 1,000 alerts); large EHR safety-report analyses attribute 36–97% of HIT-associated medication errors to usability issues (data entry, visual display, feedback); and name-overlap analysis shows why a fixed five-character search rule is an imperfect fix. The Leapfrog tool, by design, does not test wrong-drug selection.

### Inferences
- Eye-tracking converges with the 2004 Filik finding: effective cues work by redirecting fixations to the discriminating portion of the name or to location/photograph information, and errors are preceded by gaze capture ("visual invasion") by neighbors — a direct observation of the proximity mechanism in KQ1/KQ3.
- Combined/learned similarity measures outperform single metrics but still correlate only modestly with expert judgement and have not yet been validated against real-world error rates the way Lambert's psycholinguistic tests have; the one validated real-time ML system (MedGuard) works by adding indication/problem-list context rather than by name similarity alone.
- EHR safety-report corpora consistently indict visual display, data entry and feedback — the same interface attributes that produce adjacent-name selection slips — but these datasets rarely code "wrong drug" separately, so the specific contribution of search/pick-list design remains under-quantified.

### Gaps
- No 2020–2026 eye-tracking study of CPOE/pharmacy-system *search and drop-down* interaction (as opposed to shelves, syringes or racks) was found.
- No transformer/LLM-based model of drug-name confusability validated against incident data was retrieved; PubMed searches on "LASA + deep learning" are polluted by unrelated acronyms (lipid-associated sialic acid, LASA datasets).
- Leapfrog CPOE/EHR Evaluation Tool: no wrong-drug/LASA test category identified; any "wrong drug" findings attributed to Leapfrog in the brief could not be sourced.
- Pennsylvania 2016 advisory on CPOE drug-name confusion not located (see KQ3).
