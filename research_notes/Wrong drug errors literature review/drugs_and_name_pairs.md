## Key Question 1. Which name pairs appear most often in error databases? (ISMP Confused Drug Names list; ISMP MERP; USP MEDMARX; PA-PSRS; FDA case series/renames; newer 2015–2026 pairs)

### Takeaway
No single authoritative, continuously updated *ranked* list of confused pairs exists; the most frequently cited rankings come from the Pennsylvania Patient Safety Reporting System (PA-PSRS), where morphine/HYDROmorphone was the #1 pair (295 of 8,400 wrong-drug reports with both drugs named, 2007) and insulin products became the dominant class by 2023 (93 of 900 medications in 450 wrong-drug events), while the ISMP List of Confused Drug Names (updated through June 2024, ~528 pairs; >80 pairs added in 2023, 20 of them cephalosporins) is a cumulative, unranked catalogue of pairs that have appeared in ISMP newsletters and FDA/ISMP tall-man lists. USP's 2008 MEDMARX analysis (26,000+ records, 2003–2006) implicated 1,470 drugs in 3,170 LASA pairs, with cefazolin the single most-confused drug.

**USP MEDMARX / MERP analyses**
- USP's 8th annual MEDMARX Data Report (released Jan/Feb 2008; Hicks RW, Becker SC, Cousins DD, "MEDMARX Data Report: A report on the relationship of drug names and medication errors in response to the Institute of Medicine's call to action") reviewed "more than 26,000 records submitted to the MEDMARX database from 2003 to 2006" and found "1,470 different drugs are implicated in medication errors due to brand and/or generic names that looked or sounded alike"; USP called for an "indication for use" on prescriptions. — [Drug Store News, "Report shows more than 1,400 errors due to similar-sounding drug names" (2008)](https://drugstorenews.com/pharmacy/report-shows-more-1400-errors-due-similar-sounding-drug-names); [AHRQ PSNet entry for the report (870 hospitals)](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/medmarx-data-report-report-relationship-drug-names-and-medication-errors-response-institute)
- Same report (per MDedge/Dermatology News interview with Diane Cousins, USP): "USP compiled a list of more than 3,000 drug pairs that look or sound alike [3,170 per Drug Topics], a figure that is nearly double the number of pairs identified in USP's 2004 report [1,750 per Drug Topics]"; "more than 26,000 look-alike and/or soundalike (LASA) errors, of which 1.4% (384) resulted in harm or death"; "virtually every time, all of the top 10 [most prescribed drugs] appeared within the USP similar names list"; "the drug most commonly confused with others was cefazolin ... confused with 15 other drugs, primarily antimicrobials"; other major pairs: lisinopril/enalapril and trazodone/chlorpromazine; seven reported fatalities: two Reminyl/Amaryl, disodium EDTA/calcium disodium EDTA, rocuronium/vecuronium, primidone/prednisone, phenytoin/phenobarbital, Norcuron/Natrecor; Ferro-Sequel → "Serrosequel" → Seroquel; brand vs generic mix-ups 57% vs 43%. — [MDedge, "Similar Drug Names a Growing Cause of Errors," 1 Apr 2008](https://mdedge.com/content/similar-drug-names-growing-cause-errors-us-pharmacopeia-seeks-add-indication-use); [Drug Topics, 31 Jan 2008](https://www.drugtopics.com/view/usp-study-finds-drug-name-confusion-causes-drug-errors)

- Hicks et al., MEDMARX 2002 annual data: 192,477 records from 482 institutions; 2% harm. — [Hicks RW, Cousins DD, Williams RL. Am J Health Syst Pharm 2004;61(10):993-1000](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajhp/61.10.993)
- MEDMARX PCA analysis (Jul 2000–Jun 2005): 919,241 records from 801 facilities; 9,571 (1%) PCA-related; 624 harmful; 17.3% "unauthorized or wrong drug"; "similar drug names and product packaging (11.6%)" implicated. — [Hicks RW et al. AIDS Patient Care STDS 2005;19(12):803-12](https://doi.org/10.1089/apc.2005.19.803)

**FDA MedWatch/FAERS name-confusion case series and renames**
- FDA Consumer (July–Aug 2005): cases reported to FDA include an 8-year-old who died after methadone was dispensed instead of methylphenidate; clozapine for olanzapine; Flomax for Volmax; Serzone for Seroquel; iodine for Lodine; Toradol/tramadol; Avandia/Coumadin (cursive "A" read as "C"). "The last time the FDA changed a drug name after it was approved was in 2005, when the diabetes drug Amaryl was being confused with the Alzheimer's medication Reminyl, and one person died. Now the Alzheimer's medicine is called Razadyne." USP/USAN changed "amrinone" to "inamrinone" after amrinone/amiodarone errors. FDA "reviews about 400 brand names a year ... About one-third are rejected." — [Rados C. FDA Consumer, July–Aug 2005, "Drug Name Confusion: Preventing Medication Errors" (archived)](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20080916015107/http://www.fda.gov/fdac/features/2005/405_confusion.html)
- Kapidex → Dexilant (announced 4 Mar 2010): "After receiving reports of dispensing errors between KAPIDEX and the products Casodex (bicalutamide) and Kadian (morphine sulfate extended-release), Takeda, in coordination with the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), determined that ... a name change would be the best way to minimize future medication errors." — [Takeda press release, 4 Mar 2010](https://www.takeda.com/en-us/newsroom/news-releases/2010/kapidex-dexlansoprazole-renamed-dexilant-in-us-to-avoid-name-confusion/)
- Brintellix → Trintellix: FDA's July 2015 Drug Safety Communication reported that name confusion between Brintellix and Brilinta "had resulted in 50 reports of prescribing and dispensing errors since Brintellix was first approved in September 2013"; "12 of the reports resulted in a patient being given the wrong drug"; FDA approved the name change on 2 May 2016 (five additional cases bringing the total to 55 were reported in news coverage). — [RAPS, 2 May 2016](https://www.raps.org/resource/confusion-pushes-fda-to-change-antidepressants-br.html); [Pharmacy Times summary](https://pharmacytimes.com/view/brintellix-changed-to-trintellix-to-curb-brand-name-confusion-with-brilinta); [Takeda release, 3 May 2016](https://www.takeda.com/newsroom/newsreleases/2016/brintellix-vortioxetine-renamed-trintellix-vortioxetine-in-us-to-avoid-name-confusion/)
- FDA Sentinel proof-of-concept: "We identified 27 (0.1%) potential errors among 21 208 Brintellix users; 16 appeared to be likely errors ... Fifty-one (0.3%) of the 16 779 Brilinta users were identified as potential errors, and four appeared to be likely errors." — [Cocoros NM et al. - Within-class confusion (shared USAN stems: cef-, -phrine, -tinib, insulin brand families) is now a prominent pattern in the newest field data (PA 2025), implying that generic stem design itself is a risk factor, a theme Lambert, Bryan and Karet also raise.

## Key Question 2. Which drug classes and high-alert drugs dominate harmful wrong-drug errors?

### Takeaway
Across reporting-system analyses, insulins and opioids are the two high-alert classes most often involved in wrong-drug errors (insulin 10.3% of all medications and 29.8% of high-alert medications in PA-PSRS 2023 wrong-drug events; opioids 21% of PA-PSRS name-confusion reports in 2004 and the top five pairs in 2007), with anticoagulants (heparin), neuromuscular blockers, chemotherapy (vinca alkaloids, anthracyclines, platinums, taxanes) and concentrated electrolytes accounting for the most catastrophic individual cases; the ISMP high-alert list rationale is explicitly that errors with these drugs "may or may not be more common" but their consequences are "clearly more devastating."

### Inferences
- Harm concentrates where name/packaging similarity coincides with (a) within-class potency differences (hydromorphone ≈7× morphine; U-500 vs U-100 insulin; carboplatin vs cisplatin) or (b) cross-class pharmacology with no indication overlap (NMB for sedative; KCl concentrate for heparin; insulin for vaccine). Reporting systems therefore show opioids/insulins as most *frequent* and NMBs/electrolytes/chemotherapy as most *lethal*.
- Reporting data are biased toward hospitals (PA-PSRS) and voluntary reporters; outpatient data (PA 2017; VA; chain-pharmacy PSO) shift the picture toward CNS drugs (tramadol/trazodone, bupropion/buspirone, alprazolam/lorazepam) and formulation pairs (metoprolol tartrate/succinate), which are rarely high-alert.

### Gaps
- No single empirical analysis quantifying the *proportion of harmful (NCC MERP E–I) wrong-drug errors by drug class* across a national database since MEDMARX 2008 was found; PA 2025 reports serious events (12/450) but by class only in text.
- The ISMP high-alert list's methodology is survey/consensus plus MERP review; ISMP does not publish counts of MERP reports per class.

## Key Question 3. The quantitative drug-name-confusion research program (Lambert and colleagues; Kondrak & Dorr; Filik; Gabriele; Emmerton & Rizk; Ciociano & Bagnasco; Bryan et al.; Ostini; Rataboli; FDA POCA and its validation)

### Takeaway
Lambert's program (1997–2026) established that objective orthographic/phonetic similarity measures discriminate confused from control pairs with ~91–95% accuracy (1997, 1999), that similarity, low familiarity/frequency and dense "neighbourhoods" raise error rates in memory and perception tasks (2001–2010), and—critically—that laboratory psycholinguistic error rates predict real-world chain-pharmacy error rates both for pairs (R² 0.37, cross-validated 0.45; 2017) and for individual names (r = 0.50; 2025); FDA's POCA (built on Kondrak & Dorr's BI-SIM/ALINE algorithms) is the regulatory operationalisation, with a 50–55% combined-score review threshold derived from matching ~75% of FDA-identified confused names, and recent POCA analyses show ISMP/FDA tall-man pairs have mean combined scores ≈58 (brand pairs) to 69.5 (generic pairs).

### Cited Findings

**Lambert foundational work (1997–2005)**
- Lambert 1997: bigram, trigram and Levenshtein-distance similarity compared between reported error pairs and random control pairs from USP DI; "Pairs of names whose measures of similarity exceeded present thresholds were between 25 and 523 times more likely to be involved in a medication error"; "A prognostic test that correctly identified 91% of all pairs as either errors or controls was developed. This test had a sensitivity of 84% and a specificity of 99%." — [Lambert BL. Am J Health Syst Pharm 1997;54(10):1161-71](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajhp/54.10.1161)
- Lambert, Lin, Chang & Gandhi 1999: "One of every four medication errors reported in the United States is a name-confusion error"; case-control study with 22 computerised measures on n = 1,127 case pairs ("pairs of names that appeared in published error reports or national error databases") and 1,127 controls; "Every measure of similarity proved to be a significant risk factor for error"; dose-response in odds ratios; "A three-predictor logistic regression model had crossvalidated sensitivity of 93.7%, specificity of 95.9% and accuracy of 94.8%." — [Lambert BL et al. Med Care 1999;37(12):1214-25](https://doi.org/10.1097/00005650-199912000-00005)
- Lambert, Chang & Lin 2001: recognition-memory experiments in 30 pharmacists and 66 students; "both orthographic (i.e., spelling) and phonological (i.e., sound) similarity increase the probability that experts and novices will make false recognition errors"; "One out of four medication errors voluntarily reported in the US involves this type of drug name confusion." — [Lambert BL, Chang KY, Lin SJ. Soc Sci Med 2001;52(12):1843-57](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0277-9536(00)00301-4)
- Lambert, Chang & Lin 2003 (free recall): familiarity and prescribing frequency enhanced recall; orthographically similar lists were easier to recall; inverted-U for phonological similarity among pharmacists. — [Am J Health Syst Pharm 2003;60(2):156-68](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajhp/60.2.156)
- Lambert, Chang & Gupta 2003 (visual perception, blurred names, 3 s): 45 pharmacists misidentified 60.6% of 160 typewritten names; 37 pharmacists misidentified 45.7% of 156 handwritten names; significant effects of stimulus frequency (protective) and neighbourhood density (harmful), with frequency × density interactions. — [Soc Sci Med 2003;57(10):1939-55](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0277-9536(03)00059-5)
- Lambert, Yu & Thirumalai 2004 (multiattribute drug product search): 10 query names, 5 retrieval methods, 15 pharmacists rating similarity; best single measure editex (17.4% precision averaged over 11 recall levels); four-measure regression explained 40.6% of variance in mean similarity ratings. — [J Med Syst 2004;28(1):31-56](https://doi.org/10.1023/b:joms.0000021519.75230.e5)
- Lambert, Lin & Tan 2005 "Designing safe drug names": "'wrong drug' errors ... may occur as many as 3.9 million times per year in the US" (from community pharmacy observational studies); frames name design as multi-objective optimisation; catalogues pre-approval tests (computerised similarity search, expert judgement, psycholinguistic memory/perception tests, simulated ordering/dispensing). — [Lambert BL, Lin SJ, Tan H. Drug Saf 2005;28(6):495-512](https://doi.org/10.2165/00002018-200528060-00003)

**Lambert 2006–2026**
- "Listen carefully" (2010): auditory perception experiments in physicians, pharmacists, nurses and lay people; accuracy rose with signal-to-noise ratio, subjective familiarity and national prescribing frequency; "For clinicians only, similarity to other drug names reduced identification accuracy, especially when the neighboring names were frequently prescribed. When one name was substituted for another, the substituted name was almost always a more frequently prescribed drug." — [Lambert BL, Dickey LW, Fisher WM, et al. Soc Sci Med 2010;70(10):1599-608](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2010.01.042)
- Primary package labels (2009): 918 labels (422 brand, 496 generic) from an Indiana pharmacy; boldface used in ~98% of brand labels, underlining 0.2%; "lack of standardization." — [Chermak TE, Lambert BL. J Am Pharm Assoc 2009;49(3):399-406](https://doi.org/10.1331/JAPhA.2009.07137)

### Inferences
- The field has moved from (i) static similarity metrics on reported pairs (1997–2006) to (ii) laboratory psycholinguistics (2001–2016) to (iii) validation of lab tests against proprietary real-world chain-pharmacy error rates (2017, 2025) and (iv) EHR-based automated detection and name-lexicon trend analysis (2014–2026). The 2025 individual-name study directly addresses FDA's pairwise-screening blind spot.
- POCA thresholds are empirically anchored to recall of FDA-identified confused names (75% at 50%), not to prospective prediction of error rates; the finding that 25% of confused brand pairs score ≤49 and that morphine/hydromorphone scores 56 indicates that name-similarity screens alone miss a material share of real confusions.
- The Tall Man evidence base remains laboratory-dominated and mixed (positive in perception tasks, null in the only large field time series), which is why Lambert and ISMP argue for multimodal responses.

### Gaps
- I could not locate a Lambert et al. 2005 paper specifically titled "Frequency of look-alike/sound-alike errors"; the 2005 frequency statements are in Lambert, Lin & Tan (Drug Saf 2005; 3.9 million wrong-drug errors/yr) and later restated as ~1/1,000 prescriptions (Schroeder 2017) and 0.13% (Lambert project page).
- No published Lambert "FDA POCA collaboration" paper or a Lambert-authored generic-stem study was found in PubMed; the closest are Karet (USAN) 2023, Bryan 2015 (INN stems) and Lambert's 2026 preprint on generic/proprietary name distinctiveness. ## Key Question 4. Empirical frequency estimates: what proportion of medication errors involve name confusion, and where do the 25%, 10%, 7–15% and per-prescription figures originate?

### Takeaway
The "25%/one in four" figure traces to USP/ISMP MERP voluntary-report analyses from the 1990s (an ISMP Medication Safety Alert of 19 April 2000 and a 1996 USP MERP report citing 22%), and was propagated by Lambert's 1999–2001 papers, PA-PSRS 2004 and later reviews; FDA's own figure is "about 10 percent" (FDA Consumer 2005); analyses of defined report sets give 6.2–14.7% of medication error events and 7% of near misses (Bryan 2020), while per-prescription rates range from 0.03 LASA errors per 1,000 (pediatric Medicaid) to ~1 wrong-drug error per 1,000 dispensed prescriptions (Lambert/Schroeder), and only 1.4% of 26,000 USP LASA reports involved harm or death.

Cureus 2023;15(7):e41801](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.41801); Thai 200-bed hospital: name confusion accounted for 64.62% of LASA errors, packaging 24.61%, appearance 10.77%; 46 pairs, most frequent simvastatin 10 mg/20 mg. — [Supapaan T et al. Pharm Pract 2024;22(2):2959](https://doi.org/10.18549/pharmpract.2024.2.2959); Finland, self-medication errors in community pharmacies: 37% of professional-related errors involved LASA medicines. — [Häyrinen A et al. Res Social Adm Pharm 2026;22(5):676-684](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sapharm.2026.02.008); Germany outpatient: in 11.4% of 255,770 prescriptions, drug pairings with critical orthographic similarity (Levenshtein index ≤0.4) were co-dispensed to the same patient. — [Schrader T et al. Z Evid Fortbild Qual Gesundhwes 2020;150-152:29-37](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.zefq.2020.01.006); Australia perioperative 4.1% of incidents LASA. — [Ryan 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/intqhc/mzaf018)

### Inferences
- The frequently quoted "25%" is a numerator-over-voluntary-reports statistic from the 1990s MERP era (likely rounding 22%) and should not be presented as a population rate; FDA's 10% and PA-PSRS's ~3.7% (34% of 11%) are also report-based and depend on classification rules.
- Per-prescription rates differ by two orders of magnitude between algorithmic claims-based detection of *true* LASA substitutions (0.03/1,000) and observed *any wrong drug* dispensing (~1/1,000), reflecting denominators, detection method and the inclusion of non-name causes.

### Gaps
- The 19 April 2000 ISMP Medication Safety Alert and the 1996 USP MERP report cited as the origin of 25%/22% were not retrievable; their numerators/denominators remain unknown (as Bryan et al. also note).
- The 2006 IOM attribution of 25% is unverified.
- The 2024 IG Solutions/other vendor claims (e.g., "Top 25 drug names creating confusion") are secondary and were not used.

## Key Question 5. Drug-name similarity in non-English contexts (Japan, China/Taiwan, Korea, Spain, Brazil, India, Arabic-speaking countries, others); brand vs generic; local LASA lists

### Takeaway
Non-English work is strongest in Japan (katakana-specific similarity indices used in pre-approval review and validated against dispensing errors), Spain (ISMP-España/COF database of >350 confusable pairs and a 2011 national Tall Man list of 107 names in 44 groups), Thailand (register-wide orthographic analyses of 18,108 brand names) and Taiwan (similarity metrics correlated with near-miss dispensing errors); India's problem is dominated by a flood of near-identical brand names, Arabic-speaking countries report LASA as the cause of ~18–22% of dispensing errors, and no national LASA list or similarity study from Korea or Brazil was located.

### Cited Findings
- Japan: "In Japan, similarity evaluation is performed prior to approval of new drugs in order to avoid potential confusion. However, existing indices do not take account of the difference between characters that contain voiced or semi-voiced and unvoiced sounds"; subjective similarity rose with shared initial characters and fell when voiced/semi-voiced marks differed. — [Yoshikawa K, Kizaki H, Imai S, Hori S. Biol Pharm Bull 2023;46(11)](https://doi.org/10.1248/bpb.b23-00396)
- Japan: 563 dispensing errors at an acute hospital (Apr 2015–Jun 2018) classified with the m2-vwhtfrag name-similarity index and efficacy similarity; "The highest proportion of the number of pairs with dispensing errors was 36% for the Efficacy-Same and Name-Similar group, and the lowest proportion was 0.022% for the Efficacy-Far and Name-Dissimilar group"; name similarity significantly raised error proportion in every efficacy category. — [Sagawa H et al. Biol Pharm Bull 2024;47(8)](https://doi.org/10.1248/bpb.b24-00365)
- Japan: a sound-alikeness similarity index incorporating IPA consonant similarity and pitch accents (Letter Sequence Kernel) achieved r ≈ 0.8 against experimental similarity; builds on Nabeta et al.'s look-alike index; oral prescribing is "basically prohibited" in Japan but emergencies force spoken orders. — [Nagata T et al., KES 2014 (Procedia Comput Sci)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/80e4e5e1d7285f6fab75c5e35b7a1b67/)

### Inferences
- Writing systems change the similarity problem: Japanese katakana indices must handle voiced/semi-voiced diacritics and pitch accent; Spanish and Thai lists are dominated by brand-name proliferation; English-language metrics (Levenshtein, bigram/BI-SIM, POCA) have been transplanted to Taiwan, Australia, UK and Thailand with modest validation.
- Low- and middle-income settings (India, Thailand, Sri Lanka) show that registration of near-identical brand names (same or different APIs) is itself a systemic cause, whereas US/EU work focuses on confusions between distinct molecules.

### Gaps
- No Korean LASA list or Korean-language similarity study was found in PubMed/Consensus/web searches; no Brazilian (ANVISA) national LASA list or Portuguese-language similarity study was identified (only the APSF article's Portuguese translation and Brazilian-authored reviews).
- Chinese-character (Hanzi) name-similarity research was not found; Chinese studies located address dispensing errors generally with LASA as a contributing factor.
- The ISMP-España/COF database itself (>350 pairs) is not publicly retrievable in full.
