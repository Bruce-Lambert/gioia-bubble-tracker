# Wrong-Drug Errors Persist Despite Two Decades of Safeguards

*A synthesis of the 2006–2026 literature on wrong-drug (including look-alike/sound-alike) medication errors, with landmark earlier work. Compiled 30 October 2026 from ten research-note files covering roughly 420 primary and secondary sources.*

## 1. Executive summary

Wrong-drug errors, in which a patient is prescribed, dispensed or given a product containing a different active ingredient from the one intended, have occupied a stable and stubborn niche in the medication-error landscape for twenty years. Across direct-observation studies of hospital administration the wrong- or unauthorized-drug rate sits at roughly **0.2–1% of doses** (median 0.3% of opportunities across 42 studies pooled by Berdot [39]; 0.4 per 100 administrations in Sydney in 2020 [43]; 0.44% in the Czech Republic in 2021 [45]), which is 3–8% of observed administration errors; in voluntary reporting systems wrong drug accounts for a larger share, about **3–11% of all hospital incidents** (8.8% of 166,285 NHS England incidents in 2017 [11]; 11% of PA-PSRS reports in 2004 [139]; 2.9% of 40,680 Pennsylvania reports coded "wrong drug" in 2023 [16]) and **19–23% of reported dispensing errors** [23,24]. In community pharmacy the best per-prescription estimate remains the Lambert group's "roughly one per thousand prescriptions" [86], consistent with Lambert's 2005 extrapolation of **up to 3.9 million US wrong-drug dispensing errors a year** [85]; regulator and insurer datasets put wrong drug at 14–44% of community-pharmacy incidents and at the top of pharmacist liability claims (36.8% of paid HPSO/CNA claims in 2012–2016 [104]). Anesthesia is the setting where wrong drug is proportionally largest: substitution is 20–30% of self-reported anesthetic drug errors across New Zealand, China, Australia and France [176,182,192,193] and 48% in Thai and Japanese series [179,183].

The drugs involved have shifted. In the 2000s opioid pairs dominated hospital reports (five of the six most frequent pairs among 8,400 Pennsylvania wrong-drug reports in 2007 were opioid products, led by **morphine/hydromorphone, 295 reports, 3.5%** [138]); by 2023 insulins were the leading class (**93 of 900 medications, 10.3%**, in 450 verified Pennsylvania wrong-drug events), followed by systemic antibacterials, electrolytes and opioids [16]. In outpatient pharmacy the dominant pairs are high-volume generics with shared stems or adjacent shelf positions, traMADol/traZODone above all (68% of tramadol wrong-drug reports [18]), hydroxyzine/hydralazine, glyburide/glipizide and metoprolol tartrate/succinate [18,68,86]. The lethal tail is concentrated in neuromuscular blockers (vecuronium-for-Versed, 2017), tranexamic acid given intrathecally in place of bupivacaine (10 of 21 published cases fatal to 2018; 8 of 22 more to 2022 [196,197]), vinca alkaloids given intrathecally (only 6 of 31 published cases survived the acute phase [292]), and concentrated heparin or electrolytes drawn from look-alike vials.

Mechanistically, the field has converged on a lexical-access account built largely by Lambert and colleagues: orthographic and phonological similarity are dose-dependent risk factors (odds ratios up to 523-fold at high similarity [227]; a three-predictor model with 94.8% cross-validated accuracy [228]), rare names are perceived and remembered worse than frequent ones, and substitutions run toward the more frequently prescribed neighbour [231,232]. Laboratory psycholinguistic tests predict real-world chain-pharmacy error rates for pairs (37–45% of variance [86]) and for individual names (r = 0.50 [87]). Electronic systems replaced illegibility with selection slips: **42.4% of prescribing errors were system-related and 43.4% of those were drop-down selection errors** in Westbrook's audit of two commercial systems [60]; "incorrect medication" is the second most common ordering error (16.3% of 12,830) in the AHRQ Network of Patient Safety Databases [72].

Prevention evidence is lopsided. Barcode verification is the only intervention with repeated real-world evidence of wrong-drug reductions (wrong-medication administration errors 1.0% to 0.4%, RRR 57.4% [51,52]; pharmacy wrong-drug repackaging errors 160 to 5 events [324]; target dispensing errors down 93–96% with all-dose scanning [48]). Indication-based decision support intercepts about 1.4 name-confusion errors per 1,000 alerts and reveals confused pairs absent from published lists [65]. Tall Man lettering, the most widely mandated countermeasure, rests on laboratory perception studies with mixed results and a single large real-world time series that found no change (1.68 million paediatric hospitalisations [152]); two systematic reviews rate it "marginally effective" at best [70,250]. Double checks, education and packaging redesign are consensus-based with weak or absent wrong-drug outcome data. Regulators have moved from naming review (FDA POCA, EMA Name Review Group) toward product presentation (FDA container-label guidance 2022; tranexamic acid boxed warning 2025) and, after the 2022 Vaught conviction, toward the legal environment for reporting (Kentucky's 2024 decriminalization statute). The largest evidence gaps are the absence of any validated wrong-drug incidence measure comparable to the wrong-patient retract-and-reorder metric, the lack of post-rename or post-Tall-Man surveillance, the near-total reliance on report-based denominators outside a handful of observational studies, and the invisibility of wrong-drug as a separate outcome in every major intervention review.

## 2. Scope, definitions, and method

This review covers publications from 2006 to October 2026 on wrong-drug medication errors, together with the landmark earlier studies (Bates and Leape 1995, Lambert 1997–2005, Barker and Flynn 2002–2003, Cina and Poon 2006) that later work builds on. "Wrong drug" is used in the Pennsylvania Patient Safety Authority's strict sense, one medication ordered, dispensed or administered in place of another with a different active ingredient [16], but the underlying literature codes the phenomenon inconsistently: ASHP/NCC MERP observational taxonomies fold it into "unauthorized drug" (which also captures wrong-patient doses), incident-reporting systems use "wrong drug/wrong medicine", dispensing studies use "selection error" or "drug-selection error" (often pooling wrong strength and wrong form), and the anesthesia literature uses "substitution", "syringe swap" and "ampoule swap" [176,191]. The coding used by each source is stated with each figure. Look-alike/sound-alike (LASA) name confusion is one cause of wrong-drug errors, not a synonym; where a source reports LASA-attributed errors rather than all wrong-drug errors this is flagged. Wrong-route events involving product selection (intrathecal vincristine, intrathecal tranexamic acid) are included because regulators and the case literature treat them together with LASA risk; methotrexate daily-for-weekly frequency errors are excluded.

Ten researcher note files were compiled between 30 October 2026 02:00 and 03:30 using PubMed metadata and PMC full text (via the PubMed MCP tool), the Consensus and Firecrawl research indexes, Firecrawl page and PDF retrieval, and general web search. **International Pharmaceutical Abstracts was not searched because it requires institutional access**; a journal-restricted PubMed sweep of 25 pharmacy-practice, nursing and informatics journals was run instead to approximate its coverage. Several hosts were unreachable from the research environment (ismp.org, home.ecri.org, fda.gov, who.int, gov.uk, patientsafety.pa.gov for some pages, apsf.org, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov for direct fetch, drugtopics.com, beckershospitalreview.com, jstage.jst.go.jp), so **some figures attributed to ISMP, FDA, WHO, APSF, the Joint Commission and the trade press come from search snippets or secondary coverage rather than verified primary text**. Every such figure is marked in the text and the reference list is annotated with "[abstract only]", "[secondary source]", "[Consensus-only]", "[web-only]" or "[grey literature]" where the notes carried those flags. Where the notes disagreed about an attribution (for example the page range of the Bryan 2021 interventions review, the count of ISMP 2026–2027 best practices, or the ISMP VERP wrong-vaccine percentage) the disagreement is stated rather than resolved silently. All figures are given with their denominators as reported; calculations made by the researchers (for instance 4,408/55,536 = 7.9%) are identified as such.

## 3. Incidence and prevalence by setting

### Hospital wards and reporting systems

The pre-2006 baseline established that wrong-drug errors are a minority of hospital medication errors but the dominant type at the dispensing stage, and that dispensing errors are systematically invisible to chart review and incident reports. In Barker's direct-observation study of 36 Georgia and Colorado facilities, **19% of 3,216 doses (605) were in error; unauthorized drug was 4% of errors**, behind wrong time (43%), omission (30%) and wrong dose (17%) [3]. The companion methods paper showed observation detected 300 of 457 pharmacist-confirmed errors on 2,556 doses, chart review 17 and incident-report review 1 [4]. Bates found errors leading to preventable ADEs arose at ordering (56%), administration (34%), transcription (6%) and dispensing (4%), with 48% of ordering errors intercepted and none at administration [1]; Leape's systems analysis of the same cohort traced 29% of errors to failures of drug-knowledge dissemination [2]. In the Pennsylvania re-analysis of Bobb's 1,111 prescribing errors (62.4 per 1,000 orders), "wrong medication" was 6.4% of clinically significant errors [6,17]. Lisby's Danish study found 43% of 2,467 opportunities in error, with "unordered drug" among the most common types [8].

Reporting systems give a higher wrong-drug share than observation (USP's MEDMARX had already shown omission and improper dose dominating 154,816 records for 1999–2001, with two-thirds of errors reaching the patient [9]), plausibly because wrong-drug events are salient and more often reach the patient than timing errors. NHS England's National Reporting and Learning System received 166,285 medication incidents in 2017, of which **14,597 (8.8%) concerned "use of the wrong medicine"**, against 20.1% wrong dose/frequency and 19.1% omitted doses (Cousins, from a Freedom of Information response [11]); among 55,536 prescribing-stage incidents that year 4,408 were "wrong drug/medicine", 7.9% by the researchers' calculation from an FOI release that could not itself be opened [11]. The 2005–2010 NRLS analysis of 526,186 incidents reports omitted/delayed medicine (16%) and wrong dose (15%) as the largest categories but does not give the wrong-medicine count in its abstract [12], and Härkänen's analyses of 517,384 administration incidents and 229 deaths likewise lead with omission (25.8% and 31.4%) without a wrong-drug figure [14,15]. In Pennsylvania, PA-PSRS classified **11% of medication-error reports as wrong drug in 2004**, 34% of which were attributed to name confusion [139]; in 2023 the system received 40,680 medication-error reports, a "Wrong Drug" subtype query with harm score ≥C returned 1,192 (2.9%, researchers' calculation), and **450 from 127 facilities met the strict definition** of two named drugs with different active ingredients [16]. The gap between 1,192 and 450 (about 38%) is a useful correction factor when comparing raw "wrong drug" counts with strict definitions. Elsewhere, Norway's St Olav's Hospital coded administration of another drug than prescribed as 11% of 1,604 errors [28] and the Norwegian national system 15% of 3,372 reports [29]; Hong Kong's Song found wrong drug 16.7% of 1,278 incident reports [394]; Korea's KOPS (5,071 incidents) and Saudi national systems (71,332 and 19,645 events) report dose errors dominant without a wrong-drug breakdown [30,31]. Westbrook's audit of 3,291 Sydney records showed how little reporting captures: 12,567 prescribing errors generated 1.2 incident reports per 1,000, and none of 2,043 observed administrations with clinical errors was reported [35].

Among large observational syntheses, McLeod's UK review found adult administration errors in 5.6% of 21,533 non-IV opportunities and 35% of 154 IV opportunities [40], and Keers' review of 91 direct-observation studies found a median administration-error rate of 19.6% of opportunities including wrong-time and 8.0% excluding it, with **wrong time, omission and wrong dose consistently the three most common subtypes**, so wrong drug ranked fourth or lower; the subtype table is paywalled and was not retrieved [38]. Berdot's pooled analysis gives the single best figure: **unauthorized-drug error median 0.3% (IQR 0.1–0.8%) of total opportunities across 42 studies and 72,339 opportunities**, versus wrong dose 1.4%, omission 1.6% and wrong time 4.4%; in doses-observed studies the median was 0.7% (12 studies, 11,576 doses) [39]. Individual studies agree: Berdot's own Paris study found 16 unauthorized-drug errors in 1,501 opportunities (1.1%; 3.7% of 430 errors) and the observer intervened to stop an amoxicillin/clavulanate dose intended as amoxicillin [41]; Westbrook's 7,451 observed Sydney administrations yielded **wrong drug 0.4 per 100 administrations (95% CI 0.2–0.6)** at baseline and 0.5 after an electronic medication system without barcoding [43]; Jessurun's 2,576 Dutch administrations in EMR/CPOE hospitals showed 29 unordered-drug errors among 380 errors (7.6% of errors, 1.1% of administrations by researchers' calculation) [44]; Tesar's 6,356 Czech administrations gave **wrong drug 28 (0.44%)**, wrong dose 2.00%, wrong patient 0.05% [45]. Poon's paper-MAR baseline units had wrong-medication errors in 1.0% of 6,723 administrations [51,52]. The wrong-drug administration rate of 0.3–1% of doses is therefore remarkably stable across the United States (2002), France (2012), Australia (2006–2020), the Netherlands (2019) and the Czech Republic (2021), which suggests it is driven by a generic identification failure rather than local practice.

### Hospital pharmacy dispensing

Wrong drug is the single most common dispensing error type in essentially every hospital pharmacy study: all 14 studies reporting types in Aldhwaihi's review listed it first, followed by wrong strength (11/14) and wrong form (9/14) [13]. Cina's direct observation of **140,755 technician-filled doses at Brigham and Women's found 3.6% (5,075) in error; pharmacists caught 79%, leaving 0.75% of doses to leave the pharmacy undetected**, of which 23.5% were potential ADEs, and among undetected potential ADEs incorrect medication was 36%, incorrect strength 35% and incorrect form 21% [47]. Beso found errors in 2.1% of 4,849 items at the final check and 0.02% of 194,584 items reported outside pharmacy, mostly picking slips [7]. Aldhwaihi's 15 studies gave rates of 0.015–33.5% of items (prospective observation 0.79–33.5% versus incident reports 0.0147–0.13%) [13], and Um's 62-study meta-analysis pooled dispensing-error prevalence at **1.6% (95% CI 1.2–2.1%, I² 100%)** with items the denominator in 72.6% of studies [50]. Reported dispensing-error sets put wrong drug at 19.2% of 573 Scottish errors (name similarity the leading contributor, 15.5%) [23] and drug-selection errors at 23.2% of 911 London errors [24]. James's Welsh study found "wrong drug details on the label" in 4% of prevented but 14% of unprevented incidents, that is, wrong-drug errors escape the final check disproportionately [49]. US automation and technician-verification studies provide the tightest denominators: filling errors of 0.157% (24/15,271) with conventional unit dose, falling to 0.050% (13/26,200) only when barcode automation was combined with relabelling and training [328]; ADC refill errors (wrong drug, strength or form in the pocket) 0.91% of 6,829 pockets, 70% in multi-drug drawers [58]; and 0–0.5% of fills with carousel and robot systems [422]. Hospital-affiliated outpatient pharmacies reporting to PA-PSRS logged 1,044 events over 2005–2016 with **wrong drug the top type at 19.6% (205), 89.3% of which reached the patient** and 17.6% of those patients took at least one dose [18].

### Nursing administration

The administration-stage figures above (0.3–1% of doses) are nursing data. Two refinements matter. First, only Westbrook 2020 and Tesar 2025 separate "wrong drug" from "unauthorized drug" and "wrong patient" [43,45]; most observational studies cannot. Second, BCMA alters the picture: van der Veen observed 5,793 administrations in four Dutch BCMA hospitals and found non-ordered drugs were 8.0% of errors while workarounds occurred in 66% of administrations and tripled error odds (aOR 3.06) [46]; Sakushima's Japanese reports show wrong-patient injection errors fell from 17.4 to 4.5 per year after barcoding while wrong-drug errors barely moved (24.2 to 20.3 per year) because they originated in ward preparation before the scan [33]. Nurse surveys consistently rank look-alike drugs and packaging among the top perceived causes (Taiwan: wrong drug 33.7% of 184 recalled errors [401]; Korea: "wrong medication or dose" the most common nurse-reported adverse event in 4,864 nurses [402]), but incident data rarely confirm LASA as the dominant mechanism.

### Community pharmacy

Observation and reporting diverge by an order of magnitude. Flynn and Barker's observation of 4,481 prescriptions in 50 US pharmacies found **77 errors (98.3% accuracy; 6.5% clinically important)** and extrapolated 51.5 million errors across 3 billion annual prescriptions [84]; the wrong-drug count among the 77 could not be retrieved from any accessible source. Their 100-pharmacy hidden-shopper study found deviations in 22% of prescriptions [88]. Self-report studies give far lower all-cause rates: Ashcroft's 125,395 items in 35 UK pharmacies yielded near misses at 22.33 and errors at 3.99 per 10,000 items, with **selection errors (wrong drug, strength or form) 60.3% of 330 incidents** and similar drug names the stated cause in 16.8% [89]; Chua found 0.08% errors and 0.48% near misses in 51,357 items with wrong strength then wrong drug the leading types [90]; Knudsen's 40 Danish pharmacies recorded 1 error reaching patients per 10,000 prescriptions, 64.9% arising at transcription, with wrong medicine 20.1% of 234 classified transcription errors [93]; Franklin's earlier audit of about 3,000 items found content errors in 1.7% and labelling errors in 1.6% [91], and her stepped-wedge study found content errors in 1.4% of 16,357 items, mostly missing measuring devices and wrong quantities, so wrong drug was a small unreported residual [92]. Regulator-mandated reporting fills in the type distribution: Nova Scotia's 98,097 quality-related events had incorrect drug as the third type at 14.22% (13,951), 82% not reaching the patient and 0.95% with harm [94]; Ontario's AIMS programme (31,768 reports from 2,856 pharmacies, 2018–2021) put **incorrect drug at 19.5%**, with 25% of events detected by patients [102]; New Hampshire Board QRE reports were 40% incorrect medication, 68% occurring with one pharmacist on duty [103]; UK NPA medication-safety-officer reports placed wrong drug at 24–33% in 2017–2019 (secondary coverage) [421]. The per-prescription wrong-drug estimate used throughout the Lambert program is **about 1 in 1,000 prescriptions**, derived from a national chain's PSO incident data cross-validated on a second chain [86], or 0.13% (5 million per year against 3.9 billion prescriptions) on Lambert's project page [408]; combining this with Flynn's 1.7% all-cause rate implies wrong drug is roughly 6–8% of all dispensing deviations, an inference not a citation. Technician product-verification trials in Tennessee community pharmacies provide the lowest measured undetected product-selection error rates: 0.085% versus 0.063% at one year and 0.05% versus 0.01% at two years [353,105]. Disguised-observation studies in the Gulf and Jordan report far higher rates: 6.7% of 464,222 UAE prescriptions with LASA cited in 22.3% of errors [97], and 24.6% of 150,442 Jordanian prescriptions, where wrong drug was the most common counselling error (41.9%) [98].

### Mail-order and telepharmacy

Evidence is thin and old. The only published audit of a highly automated mail-service pharmacy found **16 errors among 21,252 prescriptions (0.075%), none from the mechanical fill** and 14 involving label directions; the authors were Medco employees [131]. Medco's Six Sigma programme reported 33% and 69% relative reductions in wrong-drug-selection and sound-alike/look-alike nonconformances without baseline rates [132]. Varadarajan's observation of 3,337 orders in a mail-service pharmacy serving health facilities found 0.48% with errors against zero incident reports, and named shelf proximity of LASA drugs and inadequate lighting as causes [135]. North Dakota telepharmacy QRE rates (1.3% remote versus 0.8% comparison; 1.34% versus 1.43%) are comparable to traditional pharmacies but have no wrong-drug breakdown [133,134]. Pennsylvania's outpatient data show 37.1% of 62 home-delivery events were wrong-patient errors [18]. No 2020–2026 mail-order dispensing-accuracy study was found.

### Ambulatory and primary-care prescribing

Wrong-drug prescribing is a small fraction of ambulatory prescribing errors. In the English PRACtICe study of 6,048 prescription items from 15 practices, 4.9% had a prescribing or monitoring error; of 247 prescribing errors, **incorrect drug accounted for 10 (4.0%), or 0.17% of items**, behind incomplete information (30.0%) and dose/strength (17.8%) [108]. US outpatient studies do not report a wrong-drug category: Gandhi's 1,879 prescriptions had a 7.6% error rate dominated by dose (54%) and frequency (18%) [109]; Nanji's 3,850 computer-generated prescriptions received by a chain had an 11.7% error rate, 60.7% omitted information [111]; Kaushal's e-prescribing adopters fell from 42.5 to 6.6 errors per 100 prescriptions while illegibility errors went from 87.6 per 100 to zero [110]. E-prescribing quality incidents reported to two US databases in 2011–2015 ranked incorrect drug at 15% of 550 reports and drug selection as the third most frequent of 589 portal events (Consensus only) [403]. Hickman's analysis of outpatient CPOE orders discontinued as erroneous found 1,133 of 250,806 orders (0.45%) withdrawn, with wrong drug the second reason (18.5%, 40 of 312 responses) after wrong patient (27.8%) [113]. The product-matching step between e-prescription and pharmacy system is a distinct wrong-drug hazard: automated product selection succeeded for only 79.8–84.5% of e-prescriptions [106], and in 699,662 comparable e-prescription/dispensing records 75 of 662 flagged mismatches (11.3%) were unintended, 42.7% involving the ingredient (abstract only) [107]. In Saudi ambulatory pharmacies pharmacists identified 203 incorrect-drug prescriptions among 2,073 prescribing errors (10%), detected mainly by matching drug to indication [114].

### Long-term care

Wrong-product errors are 4–6% of nursing-home medication errors but disproportionately serious. Barker's 36 facilities included 12 skilled nursing facilities and found no difference in error rates by setting [3]. North Carolina's web-based reporting from 25 homes recorded 2,731 error instances with **wrong product 38 (6%)** alongside wrong patient (6%) and wrong strength (6%) [117]; the state-wide MEQI analysis of 5,823 reports found serious errors more likely with wrong product given (RR 2.22, 95% CI 1.8–2.8), wrong patient (RR 4.39) and overdose (RR 1.49) [118]; disproportionality analysis flagged oxycodone and morphine with name confusion [119]; and opioid errors were more often wrong-drug errors than non-opioid errors across 32,176 incidents [120]. Gurwitz's cohorts located preventable ADEs mainly at ordering and monitoring [115,116]. UK care-home studies measured different things: CHUMS found 69.5% of 256 residents had at least one error with dispensing errors in 9.8% of opportunities and noted the similar appearance of de-blistered tablets as a hazard [121]; Szczepura's barcode MAR data on 188,249 administration attempts recorded attempts to give medication to the wrong resident in 0.1% of attempts [122]. Automated dose dispensing for care-home and home patients accounted for 6.2% of Dutch community-pharmacy incidents, most arising at prescription entry or bag filling after regimen changes [27]. No MEDMARX analysis specific to long-term care with a wrong-drug percentage was located.

### Home and self-administration

No study measures the incidence of a patient actually taking the wrong drug at home with a dose denominator. Survey prevalence is high: 75% of 382 polymedicated Spanish patients over 65 reported a medication error in the past year, with similar appearance of the medication among the leading causes [123]; wrong medicine ranked third among self-administration error types in Aldila's systematic review [124]. Generic appearance change is the distinctive home pathway: **29% of 11,513 post-MI patients experienced a pill colour or shape change within a year**, raising non-persistence odds by 34% (colour) and 66% (shape) [128]; 72% of 814 patients who experienced a change relied on appearance to confirm the right drug and **21% believed they had received the wrong medication** [125]; 51% of 1,000 patients over 50 had experienced such changes [126]. French patients, by contrast, identified medicines mainly by name (brand 50%, generic 21%) [419]. Patient detection of dispensing errors is slow: in a Guangzhou hospital 376 patient-reported errors over 11 years took a mean 5.2 days to notice, 47.9% caused by similar packaging or names [280].

### Paediatrics and neonatal care

Paediatric error rates are high (Kaushal's landmark audit of 10,778 orders found 5.7% in error with 79% of potential ADEs at ordering and 34% involving dosing [5]; Ghaleb's 2006 review and Chen's 2026 meta-analysis of 61 studies in 29 countries put pooled prescribing errors at 28% of orders and administration errors at 32% of doses [416,417]; prescribing 13–28% of orders; administration 19–37% of doses on wards, 59–68% in NICUs) but every paediatric review identifies dosing as the dominant type and wrong drug is rarely quantified separately. Ghaleb found prescribing errors in 13.2% of orders and administration errors in 19.1% of doses in five London hospitals [80]; Alghamdi's 35-study review gave PICU medians of 14.6 errors per 100 orders with dosing most frequent [81]; Henry Basil's NICU meta-analysis pooled administration-error prevalence at 59.3% of opportunities [79] and her Malaysian observation of 1,093 doses found 68.0% with at least one error, led by wrong rate (21.2%), wrong preparation (17.9%) and wrong dose (17.0%) [78]. The Sydney paediatric programme found 37.0% of 5,137 administrations in error with no separate wrong-drug count, 3.2 incident reports per 1,000 clinical prescribing errors, and actual harm more likely from administration (1.83%) than prescribing (0.21%) errors [75,76,77]. Out-of-hospital paediatric exposures are dominated by double dosing: the National Poison Data System logged 696,937 medication errors in children under 6 over 2002–2012 (26.42 per 10,000 population), 27.0% from inadvertently giving a dose twice [129]. Wrong-patient order errors are measurably more frequent in NICUs (117.2 versus 74.9 per 100,000 orders) [63], and in Ethiopia direct observation of 1,115 paediatric orders found wrong drug in 109 (21.2% of 513 errors, about 9.8% of orders) [393]. MEDMARX paediatric analyses found 11 medications accounting for 34.5% of 816 harmful outcomes and high-alert drugs and prescribing-phase errors more likely to harm neonates [415,414]; NRLS neonatal and paediatric ICU incidents (25,567) were led by omission and wrong dose [82]; and a German children's hospital attributed 61.4% of medication errors to dispensing, especially patient confusion [83]. No paediatric study reporting a wrong-drug rate per order or dose with a different-active-ingredient definition was identified.

### Anesthesia and the operating room

Anesthesia is the only setting in which the clinician selects, prepares and administers without pharmacist or nurse interposition, and wrong drug is correspondingly prominent. Facilitated self-report puts drug administration error at **1 per 133 anesthetics (0.75%) in Webster's 10,806 New Zealand cases, with substitution 20% of errors and 69% of IV bolus substitutions between different pharmacological classes** [176]; 0.11% in Fasting's 55,426 Norwegian anesthetics (28 syringe swaps, 15 involving a muscle relaxant) [177]; 1 in 274 in South Africa with substitution the most common type and 36.9% of errors from ampoule misidentification [180]; 0.49% in New Orleans [181]; 0.73% in a Chinese hospital with substitutions 20% [182]. Thai and Japanese series put wrong drug at 48.8% of 41 and 48% of 50 drug errors respectively [179,183], although in Irita's 4,291,925 Japanese anesthetics ampoule or syringe swaps caused only 4.3% of drug-related critical incidents and no deaths [178], and the French agency's anesthesia reports were 69% wrong-drug errors [193]. Direct observation gives rates two orders of magnitude higher: Nanji observed 3,671 administrations in 277 operations and found **193 events (5.3%) involving an error or ADE, 44.8% of operations affected**, with labelling errors (24.2%), wrong dose (22.9%) and omission (17.6%) the most common types; the exact wrong-drug count is in a table not reproduced in the open-access text [185]. Merry's randomised trial recorded 9.1 versus 11.6 errors per 100 administrations, mostly recording errors [186]. Bratch's integrative review summarises the pattern: 1 in 200 anesthetics by self-report, error "in almost every anaesthetic" by observation, drug error about 10% of anesthesia incidents in large databases [187]. In incident databases, substitution was 28.1% of 462 webAIRS medication errors [192] and syringe swaps 18.9% plus wrong ampoule 20.8% of 896 AIMS drug errors [191]; 94% of 133 South African anaesthetists had given a wrong drug, nearly half involving muscle relaxants [189], and 85% of 687 Canadian anaesthetists reported at least one error or near miss, the commonest being a muscle relaxant given instead of a reversal agent [190]. Wahr's expert review condensed 138 candidate recommendations to 35 [188]. Bowdle's Seattle series shows a secular decline with barcode-based systems: errors 0.62% of anesthetics in 2002–2003, 0.39% in 2014 and 0.23% after barcoding, with vial-swap errors significantly reduced [184]. Regional Australian incident data put LASA at 18.7% of 246 pharmacy/perioperative incidents, packaging a factor in 65.2% [225].

### Intensive care

In ICUs wrong drug is a smaller fraction than in anesthesia because most administrations are nurse-executed against a written order with pharmacy involvement. The SEE-2 study of 113 ICUs in 27 countries found 74.5 parenteral administration errors per 100 patient-days, three-quarters omissions, with wrong drug one of five tracked categories whose count is not in the abstract [201]; Rothschild's Critical Care Safety Study found medications in 61% of 277 serious errors with slips and lapses 53% of performance failures [202]; Kiekkas' review of six observational ICU studies ranked wrong dose, wrong time/rate and omission above wrong drug [203]. The ICU analogue of wrong drug is increasingly the wrong smart-pump library entry: 5.3 per 100 NICU administrations in Schnock's observation of 905 infusions [204], and **10,017 incorrect medication selections cancelled out of 290,807 pump-library alerts (3.45%)** in a 12-month log review [205]. Finnish ICUs logged 663 wrong-fluid-product selection incidents over 2007–2017, 26% with patient consequences [207], and about 70% (355/488) of one ICU automated dispensing cabinet's stock carried a LASA risk with 16% stored adjacent to the confusable product [208]. Only 30% of ICUs used ISO 26825 syringe labels in their original form in 2012 [206].

### Emergency department and EMS

In EDs wrong drug is about 11% of pharmacist-detected prescription errors (11 of 99 errors in 3,136 Canadian ED prescriptions, 3.2% error rate) [209] and 12.8% (32/250) of serious ED medication events reported by 101 Pennsylvania hospitals over 2011–2020, where wrong drugs resulted most often from name confusion (14) or storage confusion (6) and the confused pairs included cefepime→diltiazem, diltiazem→diazepam, levetiracetam→lamotrigine, morphine→hydromorphone and verapamil→midazolam [148]. Observed ED administration-error rates of 3–6% are dominated by wrong dose and fall 74–81% after BCMA (6.3% to 1.2%; 2.96% to 0.76%) without a wrong-drug breakout [211,212]. Patanwala's multicentre ED pharmacist study found wrong dose the most common intercepted type (44.2%) and 32.7% of interceptions arising from verbal orders [210]. In eight Spanish paediatric EDs, 218 errors in 99,797 visits (0.2%) were split between dosing (51.4%) and wrong or improper drugs (46.8%) [213]. In EMS, wrong medication was 4% of paramedics' self-reported errors [214], whereas paediatric dosing errors ran at 34.7% of 360 administrations with epinephrine worst (60.9%; mean IV 1:1,000 overdose 808%) [215], a wrong-concentration selection problem driven by two stocked concentrations that a height-based dosing tool cut by 90% [216].

### Vaccines

"Wrong vaccine administered" was the third-largest VAERS error group, **3,372 of 21,843 errors (15%) in 2000–2013**, with mix-ups between varicella/zoster, DTaP/Tdap and pneumococcal conjugate/polysaccharide vaccines; 92% of reported adverse events were non-serious [166]. It was the most common ISMP VERP type at about 23% of 1,754 outpatient reports in 2016 and roughly 25% of 1,987 reports in 2022–2023 (both figures from secondary coverage; the primary ISMP analyses were not reachable) [173]. EudraVigilance held 7,097 vaccination-error reports for 2001–2016, their share of all vaccine reports rising from 0.4% to 4.0% [172], and GSK's zoster-vaccine surveillance classed 22.9% of 15,638 reports as vaccination errors [406]. In England and Wales wrong vaccine was 14.3% of 1,745 paediatric immunisation incidents [168] and 33.3% of 158 vaccine-error enquiries to a regional advice line, with shared first letters implicated in 13 reports [169]. MEDMARX vaccine reports showed wrong-vaccine errors clustered in look-alike/sound-alike groups [167]; VAERS 2010–2018 recorded 114 influenza doses given to infants under 6 months, most often confused with the hepatitis B birth dose [407]; 113 reports of GSK's RSV vaccine given to pregnant persons in error accumulated in six months [171]. Morse-Brady's review pooled vaccination-error prevalence at 1.15 per 10,000 doses, the only per-dose estimate found [170]. In the COVID-19 era "incorrect dosage or product" was 25.9% of 734 CDC error inquiries [174] and incorrect vaccine product 20.1% of 209 errors observed covertly in 8,743 Jordanian doses [175].

### Veterinary medicine

In the largest veterinary incident series (560 analysed reports from three US hospitals, 5.3 incidents per 1,000 visits), drug errors were 55–69% of reports and **wrong drug 62 of 344 drug errors (18.0%)**, higher in the large-animal teaching hospital (34.5%) than private small-animal practice (11.2%) [130]. ASPCA poison-control data show 68 of 79 otic-product-in-eye exposures arose from mistaking otic for ophthalmic products (86%) [420]. No veterinary study has a per-prescription wrong-drug denominator.

### Non-Western countries

Wrong drug is consistently among the top three error types outside North America and Western Europe, but its share depends heavily on detection method. Incident reports give 15–20% (Hong Kong 16.7% of 1,278 [394]; Norway 15% for comparison [29]); Japanese psychiatric hospitals recorded wrong-drug administration as the most common potential-ADE type (35.7% of 221), more frequent on units with fewer RNs or patients with similar names [395]; Iran's nephrology ward audit found wrong drug selection 19.8% of 86 prescribing errors [396] and the national "never events" database attributed 38.4% of 515 cases to the name or type of medication [34]; Saudi Hajj hospital prescriptions showed wrong drug 23.25% of 43 errors with LASA cited in 18.6% [397]; preventable ADEs in Pakistani antibiotic recipients were attributed to wrong drug in 40.1% [398]. Japan's JADE study recorded 8.7 medication errors per 1,000 patient-days in 3,459 admissions [32], its community near-miss system logged 45,483 reports with LASA names the characteristic factor at entry and dispensing [101], and Malaysian outpatient pharmacies found wrong drug the top filling error (44 of 111 near misses, 39.6%) [99]. Alsulami's Middle East review found prescribing error rates of 7.1–90.5% with dispensing errors inadequately evaluated [36], and 54% of Iranian nurses reported committing errors in a 36-study meta-analysis [418]. In Colombia's outpatient dispensing surveillance (14,873 reports), name confusion raised the odds of an error reaching the patient (OR 1.28, 95% CI 1.05–1.56) [312]. Per-order dispensing error rates in Asian hospital pharmacies are very low (0.33 per 10,000 orders in Chongqing; 0.0063% falling to 0.0014% at a 2,202-bed Taiwanese centre where wrong drug was the most common baseline type and fell 51–81% across automation stages [327]). Direct observation in LMIC wards gives rates an order of magnitude higher (Ethiopia 9.8% of orders [393]). Mekonnen's African review found prescribing errors in a median 57.4% of prescriptions without pooling wrong drug [392]. Eshraghi's review of 22 community and outpatient dispensing studies (13 from developing countries) found look-alike/sound-alike drugs implicated in 47.9% of errors, with GRADE confidence very low [100].

Three features stand out. Opioid products occupy five of the top six positions and the most commonly cited single drug was oxycodone/acetaminophen; many pairs are same-ingredient formulation confusions (oxycodone/OxyContin, Vicodin/Vicodin ES) that the 2025 strict definition would exclude; and the top 25 pairs together account for less than a quarter of applicable reports, so the distribution has a very long tail. The 2004 Pennsylvania advisory had already reported that insulin products were involved in 9% and opiates in 21% of name-confusion reports, that 32% of opiate look-alike reports were morphine/hydromorphone, and that 6% of all name-confusion reports were alprazolam/lorazepam [139]. Pennsylvania's 2016 opioid trend analysis of 41,727 opioid events (2005–2014) found 4,958 (11.9%) were wrong-drug events and documented **a 66.4% fall in opioid wrong-drug reports and a 79.4% fall in morphine/HYDROmorphone mix-ups (N = 567 reports naming both)**, consistent with the tall-man and opioid-safety interventions of that decade [140].

By 2023 the picture had changed. Ro and Jones' analysis of 450 verified wrong-drug events found **insulin the most frequently reported class (93 of 900 medications, 10.3%), then antibacterials for systemic use (90), electrolyte solutions (62), opioids (51) and cardiac stimulants**; the three most frequent individual medications were all insulins (lispro, regular, glargine), which the authors contrast with the opioid dominance of two decades earlier. Pairs named in the text are mostly same-class or same-stem: insulin with insulin, epinephrine with norepinephrine, hydromorphone with other opioids, cefazolin with other cephalosporins, hydroxyzine with hydralazine and hydrochlorothiazide, Solu-Cortef/Solu-Medrol, Humalog/Humulin, and single-entity versus combination products (oxycodone/oxycodone-acetaminophen, ampicillin/ampicillin-sulbactam, albuterol/albuterol-ipratropium, amoxicillin/amoxicillin-clavulanate); normal saline and heparin were confused with a wide range of products; the Table 3 footnotes imply Cardene/Cardizem, Tobrex/Tobradex, Subutex/Suboxone and Premarin/permethrin [16]. The frequency tables themselves (Tables 1–2) are published as images and their counts could not be extracted, which is the single most important unverified item in this section. In hospital-affiliated outpatient pharmacies (2005–2016), 205 wrong-drug reports involved 105 drugs in 147 combinations; tramadol was the most frequent (22, 10.7%), 15 of them tramadol/trazodone, then metoprolol (11, eight tartrate/succinate), oral-contraceptive products (16, 7.8%) and insulin products (15, 7.3%) [18]. Serious ED events in Pennsylvania named 24 pairs including cefepime→diltiazem, diltiazem→diazepam (twice), levetiracetam→lamotrigine, levetiracetam→ketamine (twice), metronidazole→fentanyl, morphine→hydromorphone and verapamil→midazolam [148], and tenecteplase/alteplase errors were traced to the spoken abbreviation "TPA" [149].

USP's 2008 MEDMARX report (Hicks, Becker, Cousins) reviewed more than 26,000 records from 2003–2006 attributed to brand or generic name confusion, implicating **1,470 drugs in 3,170 LASA pairs (versus 1,750 pairs in USP's 2004 analysis)**, with 1.4% (about 384) resulting in harm or death and seven possible deaths (two Reminyl/Amaryl, disodium EDTA/calcium disodium EDTA, rocuronium/vecuronium, primidone/prednisone, phenytoin/phenobarbital, Norcuron/Natrecor); cefazolin was the single most confused drug (15 partners), lisinopril/enalapril and trazodone/chlorpromazine were other major pairs, and brand/generic mix-ups split 57%/43%. All of these figures come from press coverage of a 400-page report that is not online and should be treated as secondary [141]. MEDMARX class analyses add wrong-medication shares of 32% for antiretrovirals (lamivudine the most frequent product) [20], 17.3% for PCA errors (similar names and packaging implicated in 11.6%) [21], and wrong-drug errors in 24% of harmful oxycodone events versus 11% for other opioids, typically immediate- versus sustained-release confusion [22]. ISMP's own practice-level 2016–2017 tallies, reported only through Pharmacy Times, put wrong drug at 33% of insulin, 39% of anticoagulant and 39% of opioid error reports, with hydromorphone/morphine (3.5%) and hydrocodone/oxycodone (2.4%) the most confused opioid pairs [410].

Field data with per-patient denominators are rarer. Zacher's VA screen of 20 pairs (April 2014–March 2015) found potential-error rates of 3.05% of treated patients for tamsulosin/terazosin, 2.91% glipizide/glyburide, 2.20% tramadol/trazodone, 1.53% bupropion XL/SR, 1.48% metoprolol tartrate/succinate and 1.31% bupropion/buspirone, but chart-confirmed actual error rates were tiny: lamivudine/lamotrigine 0.003%, morphine/hydromorphone 0.02%, carbamazepine/oxcarbazepine 0.03% of treated patients [68]. Basco's paediatric Medicaid screen of 11 pairs across 1,420,091 prescriptions produced 395 alerts and 43 true LASA errors (about 0.03 per 1,000 prescriptions) [151]. FDA's Sentinel claims algorithm found 27 potential Brilinta/Brintellix errors among 21,208 Brintellix users (16 likely) and 51 among 16,779 Brilinta users (4 likely) [150]. Galanter's indication alerts intercepted 176 name-confusion errors across 39 pairs, led by fluticasone/fluticasone-nasal (100, 57%), metoprolol/metoclopramide (16), hydralazine/diphenhydramine (6) and nitroprusside/nitroglycerin (6); **all but three pairs were absent from the ISMP list** and 51% of pairs shared the first three letters, pointing to pick-list mechanisms [65]. ISMP Canada's community analysis of 342 LASA incidents named Celebrex/Celexa, Losec/Lasix, Lamictal/Lamisil, Yaz/Yasmin and zopiclone/Zoloft [155]. Lambert's eight-year EHR algorithm confirmed 61 errors including valganciclovir/valacyclovir (15), cycloserine/ciclosporin (9), sulfadiazine/sulfasalazine (5) and hydralazine/hydroxyzine (5), nine of 29 pairs never previously published [64]; 11 of 16 cycloserine orders over seven years were intended as cyclosporine [67]. The chain-pharmacy dataset behind Schroeder's validation study comprised 109 pairs (88 brand and 126 generic names) with hydroxyzine/hydralazine and glyburide/glipizide as exemplars; absolute rates were scaled by an undisclosed constant and are not public [86].

### High-alert classes

ISMP defines high-alert medications as drugs whose errors "may or may not be more common" but whose consequences are "clearly more devastating"; the first list (Davis and Cohen 1989) already included IV lidocaine, vincristine, hypertonic saline, morphine injection, insulin and concentrated potassium chloride [146]. In Pennsylvania's 2023 data 312 high-alert medications appeared in 197 of 450 reports, **57.4% of those reports involved confusion between two high-alert drugs, and insulin was 29.8% of all high-alert medications**; 7 of 12 serious events involved high-alert drugs (4 insulin, all hypoglycaemia requiring intervention; 2 neuromuscular blockers) [16]. Harm therefore concentrates where name or packaging similarity coincides with within-class potency differences (hydromorphone about seven times morphine; U-500 versus U-100 insulin; carboplatin versus cisplatin) or with cross-class pharmacology lacking indication overlap (paralytic for sedative, potassium chloride concentrate for heparin, insulin for vaccine).

**Insulin.** Insulin brand-family confusion (Humalog/Humulin, Novolog/Novolin, Humalog Mix 75/25/Humulin 70/30, Humulin R U-100/U-500) appears throughout PA-PSRS 2004, 2007 and 2023 [139,138,16], in the FDA/ISMP tall-man lists [137], and in community risk models [96]. ISMP documented 50 hospital employees given insulin instead of influenza vaccine in 2016 and a second mass incident in Oklahoma in 2019 [423]. Geller's national ED surveillance estimated 5,636 US ED visits per year (2012–2017) for insulin mix-up errors, 77.5% rapid-acting taken instead of long-acting, with the mix-up share falling 60% as pen use rose (abstract via Consensus only) [136]. Greene's nursing-home data give insulin a serious-error RR of 2.35 [118].

**Opioids.** Opioids were 21% of Pennsylvania name-confusion reports in 2004, five of the top six pairs in 2007, and 4,958 wrong-drug events over 2005–2014 [139,138,140]. Oxycodone immediate/sustained release and hydrocodone/oxycodone combination products dominate outpatient and nursing-home reports; oxycodone and morphine were disproportionately reported with name confusion in North Carolina homes and opioid errors there were more likely than non-opioid errors to be wrong-drug errors [119,120]. In anesthesia opioids follow neuromuscular blockers as the second most involved class in AIMS and Wake Up Safe [191,194]. Tramadol/trazodone remains the single most frequent outpatient pair [18,68].

**Neuromuscular blockers.** NMBs are the most frequently swapped class in every anesthesia dataset that reports class: nearly 50% of 303 wrong-drug administrations in Gordon's South African survey, with 5 deaths [189]; 15 of 28 syringe swaps in Norway [177]; 13 of 41 Thai incidents (31.7%) [179]; 32% of 311 Pakistani perioperative errors [424]; 16 of about 31 wrong-drug NHS litigation claims, awake paralysis the most frequent outcome [195]. The USP expert committee statement of 2006 recorded continuing harm from NMB errors involving wrong drugs, look-alike packaging and sound-alike names [147]; Patel's review found 20 reports of NMBs given neuraxially, syringe swap the primary cause [199]; seven newborns received 10 mg IM atracurium at a Taiwanese clinic, one died and four had developmental delay [200]. In Pennsylvania 2023, 13 NMBs appeared in 11 reports (rocuronium 7, succinylcholine 4, vecuronium 2) [16]. ISMP's Confused Drug Names list carries Narcan/Norcuron, rocuronium/Romazicon and Zemplar/Zemuron [137].

**Chemotherapy.** FDA tall-man pairs include vinBLAStine/vinCRIStine, CISplatin/CARBOplatin and DAUNOrubicin/DOXOrubicin; ISMP adds DOCEtaxel/PACLitaxel, Taxol/Taxotere, Alkeran/Leukeran/Myleran, cycloPHOSphamide/cycloSPORINE and epiRUBicin/eriBULin [145,137]. Kovacic's Alberta oncology formulary screen flagged 186 of 3,320 possible pairs by bigram similarity [425]. Oncology LASA risk-reduction practice was reviewed by Schulmeister in 2006 [426]. The catastrophic chemotherapy wrong-drug events are route-selection errors with vinca alkaloids (section 7).

**Anticoagulants, electrolytes, vasoactives.** Heparin/Hespan, Avandia/Coumadin, Plavix/Pradaxa, Levemir/Lovenox, Aggrastat/argatroban and apixaban/axitinib are listed pairs [137]; heparin was involved in 3.6% of roughly 300,000 reports to three US programmes in 2005 [19]; DOAC incidents in a UK hospital were 16.1% wrong drug [400]. ISMP described potassium chloride concentrate found in a heparin ADC bin in 2024, both in 250 mL bags with similar livery [146]; Finnish potassium incidents were 24% wrong medication [427]. Epinephrine/ephedrine and epinephrine/norepinephrine vial swaps recur from Orser 1994 through Pennsylvania 2006 to Saudi Arabia 2025 [16,428,429].

### The ISMP list and its evolution

The ISMP List of Confused Drug Names "consists of those name pairs that have been published in the ISMP Medication Safety Alert! Acute Care, the ISMP Medication Safety Alert! Community/Ambulatory Care, and the FDA and ISMP Lists of Look-Alike Drug Names with Recommended Tall Man Letters", is ordered alphabetically with each pair appearing twice, and is "Updated through June 2024" [137]. Its growth tracks the market: Cheng extracted 278 pairs from the version current in 2017 [144]; Shah's FDA analysis evaluated 224 pairs containing a proprietary name after excluding 99 generic/generic pairs [142]; the 2023 update added more than 80 pairs, 20 of them cephalosporins, bringing the total to about 528 (secondary coverage) [137]. Newly added pairs in the 2024 edition include Cabenuva/cabotegravir, Neo-Synephrine/neostigmine, talquetamab-tgvs/teclistamab-cqyv, and terbinafine/terbutaline; newer-era entries include bamlanivimab/belimumab, the Tanzeum/Toujeo/Tradjenta/Tresiba/Trulicity cluster, Farxiga/Fetzima, linaCLOtide/linaGLIPtin, apixaban/axitinib, dabigatran/vigabatrin, neratinib/nilotinib/niraparib and IDArubicin/idaruCIZUmab; Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, Zepbound, Rybelsus and Paxlovid do not appear [137]. FDA's Name Differentiation Project list, begun in 2001 with 16 generic pairs, now comprises 23 entries including the 2020s additions migALAstat/migLUstat and risperiDONE/rOPINIRole [145]. The list should be read as a catalogue of reported pairs, not a ranking, and Galanter's finding that 36 of 39 intercepted pairs were absent from it shows how incomplete any list is for a given institution [65].

### Non-English contexts

Non-English work is strongest in Japan, where katakana-specific similarity indices (htfrag, vwhtfrag, m2-vwhtfrag) have been developed since 2006 and used in pre-approval review [411]; Sagawa applied m2-vwhtfrag to 563 dispensing errors and found **36% of efficacy-same, name-similar pairs had errors versus 0.022% of efficacy-far, name-dissimilar pairs** [157], and Yoshikawa showed voiced/semi-voiced kana marks change subjective similarity in ways existing indices ignore [158]. Spain's ISMP-España/COF database holds more than 350 confusable pairs and the 2011 national Tall Man list covers 107 names in 44 groups (ADRENalina/ATROPina, BUpivacaína/MEpivacaína/ROpivacaína, cefAZOLina/cefTRIAXona/cefTAZIdima), with only 9 of 90 hospitals using Tall Man in 2010 [154]. Taiwan's Tseng found look-alike alphabetical trade names caused 66.3% (134/202) of wrong-medicine near misses [156]. Thailand's register of 18,108 tablets and capsules contains 0.39% name matches across companies with different active ingredients, "AMINE", "HYDRO" and "PREDNIS" being the most frequent shared strings [159,161]; a national survey of 128 hospitals collated 2,510 LASA pairs with ceftriaxone/ceftazidime the top injectable pair (Consensus only) [159]. In Sri Lanka 64.15% of 385 sampled proprietary names were identical, look-alike or sound-alike to another registered name [160]; Korean reimbursement data flagged 2.6% look-alike and 3.2% sound-alike products (web-only, unverified) [431]; India's problem is a flood of near-identical brand names catalogued by Rataboli [162]; in Germany 11.4% of 255,770 outpatient prescriptions involved co-dispensed pairs with Levenshtein index ≤0.4 [163]; and FDA found 27 US brand names identical to 38 foreign brands with different ingredients [164]. The LMIC pattern is proliferation of multi-source generics under proprietary names and combination products (84% of Delhi nurse confusions were identical brand names for the same generic [430]), which US/UK generic-pair tall-man lists address poorly. No Chinese-character or Arabic-script similarity study was found.

## 5. Where in the medication-use process errors arise

### Prescribing and CPOE pick-lists

In the paper era wrong-drug prescribing was a problem of handwriting and nomenclature: Lesar attributed 13.4% of 696 clinically important prescribing errors to nomenclature factors (wrong drug name, dosage form or abbreviation) [254], and Bobb's "wrong medication" was 6.4% of clinically significant errors [6,17]. Computerised order entry removed illegibility and introduced selection. Koppel's 2005 JAMA study first catalogued 22 CPOE-facilitated error types [257]; An Irish maternity hospital's adapted retract-and-reorder tool found 259 confirmed near-miss prescribing errors per 100,000 orders, 76.1% duplicates, none of them reported by prescribers [74]. Westbrook's audit of 629 admissions on two commercial systems found **42.4% (493/1,164) of prescribing errors were system-related, drop-down selection errors accounted for 43.4% of these (34.2 per 100 admissions), one system generated four times more selection errors than the other (OR 4.17), and only 13.4% of system-related errors had been detected before the audit** [60]. Schiff's analysis of 63,040 CPOE-related MEDMARX errors derived 21 recurring scenarios; when testers tried to enter 375 erroneous orders on 13 CPOE systems at 16 sites, 79.5% could be entered, 28.0% easily and 28.3% with minor workarounds and no warning [59]. Amato's six-site analysis of 2,522 CPOE-related reports found the system actively facilitated 13.1% and failed to prevent 86.9% [258]. In the AHRQ Network of Patient Safety Databases (2010–2020), **incorrect medication was 16.3% (2,086) of 12,830 ordering errors**, second to incorrect dose (29.7%), and among 5,282 events reaching patients with known severity there were 12 deaths and 4 severe harms; the authors prioritise wrong-dose and wrong-drug CPOE interventions [72]. A Spanish tertiary centre attributed 77.7% of 714 errors in 85,857 prescriptions to CPOE, with wrong medication selection 20.9% of CPOE-related errors [264]; Korb-Savoldelli's review of 14 studies found CPOE-related errors were 6.1–77.7% (median 26.1%) of all prescription errors and every study reported wrong-drug errors [265]; Carayon's ICU pre-post study found "orders of wrong drug" was one of four ordering error types that increased after EHR implementation [266]. Systematic reviews by Brown (34 studies) and Tolley (47 paediatric studies) both name drop-down menus and auto-population as key error themes [260,261]; Raban's technology-related error mechanism (TREM) classification formalises "selection errors" as one of six categories [262]. Quist's survey of 10 CPOE systems at six institutions found widespread inconsistency in brand versus generic display, sometimes on the same screen, and search behaviour that required knowing the first component of a combination product [259]. Flynn's name-overlap simulation of 20,020 lists drawn from 2,249 names showed maximum left-to-right character overlap ranged 4–29 (mode 14) and 3–10 for high-alert names (mode 6), so a fixed five-letter search rule is sometimes insufficient and sometimes excessive [263]. ISMP has stated that wrong-selection errors after typing the first few letters ("met" surfacing metformin and metronidazole; a fatal metolazone/methotrexate case) may now rival or exceed errors from handwritten orders and recommends at least five letters (secondary coverage) [272]. In outpatient settings pharmacy staff attributed most e-prescription errors to drop-down menus and auto-population [112], and English GPs named mis-selection from pick-lists as a cause of prescribing error [108]. The Dutch Central Medication incidents Registration, which covered 90 of 93 hospitals and 44.8% of community pharmacies by 2011 [25], gives the cleanest cross-setting contrast: 16.1% of 4,161 incidents were IT-related, **41.0% (150/351) of community-pharmacy IT incidents were "choosing the wrong medicine"** through name confusion and poor screen design, whereas 55.3% of hospital IT incidents concerned human-machine input during CPOE [26].

### Transcription and order entry

Transcription is where community-pharmacy wrong-drug errors originate. Knudsen found 64.9% of 365 errors reaching Danish patients arose at transcription, with wrong medicine 20.1% of classified transcription errors [93]; Nova Scotia's most common origin was order entry [94]; Ashcroft recorded "selecting the previous drug/dose from the patient medication record" as the cause of 11.4% of incidents, an early computer-mediated slip [89]; Panich states that wrong-drug-product errors in community pharmacies "often originate at the transcription stage" and that 15–20% of e-prescriptions still require manual product selection [106]; Whitaker's interviews show current automation matches the dispensed product to the product chosen at data entry, not to the e-prescription [432]. In hospitals, Lisby found transcription the stage with the highest error rate (56% of 558 opportunities) [8], and Shawahna measured transcription error rates of 16.9% on 6,583 inpatient and 13.8% on 5,329 discharge medications in a Palestinian hospital [318]. Poon's bedside barcode eMAR eliminated transcription errors entirely (6.1% to 0) [51].

### Dispensing

Dispensing is the stage at which wrong drug is the leading error type (section 3). The mechanisms are picking slips in alphabetically organised stock, look-alike packaging, and verification that confirms rather than challenges. Beso's human-error analysis classed most final-check errors as slips in picking products or mistakes in assumptions about products, with storage, labelling, interruptions and a culture of inevitability as latent conditions [7]; Anto showed selection errors cluster where a low-frequency drug resembles a high-frequency one [24]; Irwin's experiments showed proximity of several similar names in an array degrades selection accuracy and that alphabetical storage therefore increases risk [244]; Balchaites mapped 21 of 30 Irish community errors to selection, 17 from the shelf and 4 at computer entry (conference abstract via Consensus) [433]. Pharmacy robotics and barcode-assisted filling shift residual error upstream to order entry and to cell refilling: ISMP described a robot cell refilled with trazodone instead of tramadol because one bottle scan satisfied the check for a batch [18,272]; Takase's robotic dispensing cut prevented errors from 0.204% to 0.044% and unprevented from 0.015% to 0.002% [326]; Cousein's unit-dose robot plus ADC reduced ward wrong-drug errors by 93.7% (1.9% to 0.01% of opportunities) [325].

### Automated dispensing cabinets

ADCs introduce their own selection pathway. Pennsylvania's 2005 advisory identified alphabetical pick-lists as a frequent cause of look-alike selection, described a diazepam/diltiazem case in which the cabinet seemed to "confirm" the wrong choice, and noted a profiled cabinet would have blocked the removal [270]; Grissinger's 2015 override analysis (5,399 reports) found unauthorized-medication events most common, then wrong drug and wrong dosage form [271]. The archetype is the 2017 Vanderbilt case: a nurse typed "VE" into an ADC override search, the system returned vecuronium rather than Versed (midazolam), and the patient died [297]. Helmons found 70% of incorrect ADC pocket contents were in multi-drug drawers and barcode-assisted refilling cut refill errors from 0.91% to 0.21% of pockets [58]; Ruutiainen's ICU audit found 70% of stock with LASA risk and 16% stored adjacent to the confusable product [208]; ADC restocking errors continue to be reported [434]. ISMP's 2019 ADC guidelines recommend a five-letter minimum for override searches, segregation of paralysing agents and tiered override prevention (secondary coverage) [272]. Osman's 2026 systematic review of 16 ADC studies found reductions in omission and wrong-drug/wrong-dose events that "varied widely", largely observational [57].

### Administration

Administration is the final and least interceptable stage: Bates found 0% of administration errors intercepted versus 48% of ordering errors [1]. The observed wrong-drug administration rate of 0.3–1% of doses is stable across decades (section 3). The anesthesia literature distinguishes the syringe swap (correctly labelled syringe picked in error) from the ampoule or vial swap (wrong product drawn up), and the countermeasures differ: Fasting found colour-coded labels reduced ampoule swaps but not syringe swaps between same-size syringes [177]; Neo's simulation found a barcode labelling system reduced wrong-ampoule errors (4.9% versus 33.3%) but not syringe swaps [219]; only pre-administration scanning addresses the syringe swap [186,184]. Smart-pump library selection is the infusion-era analogue [204,205]. Ward preparation sits upstream of BCMA: Sakushima's wrong-drug injection errors did not fall after barcoding because they arose during preparation [33], whereas Küng's barcode-assisted preparation linked to the eMAR reduced wrong-medication preparation errors (OR 0.38) [323].

### Barcode gaps

BCMA catches the drug–order mismatch only when the scan happens and the barcode is right. BCMA alert frequencies range from 0.18% to 42% of scans and not all are clinically meaningful [335]. Koppel catalogued 15 workaround types and 31 causes including unreadable or missing barcodes, malfunctioning scanners and non-barcoded products, with nurses overriding alerts for 4.2% of patients and 10.3% of medications charted [268]; van der Veen found workarounds in 62.7–66% of 5,793 administrations (most commonly not scanning at all, 36%) with aOR 3.06 for error [46]; Williams' 16-month log study of 613,868 administrations found medication scanning rates of 5.6–67% by ward [269]; Zheng's review noted that medications labelled with wrong barcodes and unreadable barcodes themselves caused confusion [435]; Galt's Nebraska survey recorded scanners mismatching NDC codes and producing wrong drug-product identification [436]. Poon's pharmacy study is the clearest demonstration that partial scanning fails: a configuration scanning only one of several identical doses cut target dispensing errors by 60% and increased target potential ADEs 2.4-fold, against 93–96% reductions with all-dose scanning [48]. BCMA also does not exist where some of the worst events happen: none of 34 US children's hospitals used BCMA in their operating rooms in 2014 [437], scanning was unavailable in the imaging area where the Vanderbilt patient was treated [297], and ISMP's 2026–2027 Best Practice 24 on scanning actual product barcodes and avoiding proxy barcodes found 42–47% of 93 surveyed hospitals only partially compliant [380].

## 6. Contributing factors and mechanisms

### Psycholinguistics and the name-similarity research program

The quantitative account of why names are confused was built largely by Lambert and colleagues between 1997 and 2026, with convergent contributions from Filik, Darker, Gerrett and Gale in the UK, Schell, Irwin, Or and Wang in Hong Kong, and Kondrak and Dorr in computational linguistics. Lambert's 1997 case–control comparison of published error pairs with random controls on bigram, trigram and Levenshtein measures found that **pairs exceeding similarity thresholds were 25 to 523 times more likely to have been involved in an error**, and a prognostic test classified 91% of pairs correctly (sensitivity 84%, specificity 99%) [227]. The 1999 Medical Care study scaled this to 1,127 error pairs and 1,127 controls across 22 computerised orthographic and phonetic measures: every measure was a significant risk factor with a dose–response in the odds ratio, and a three-predictor logistic model achieved cross-validated sensitivity 93.7%, specificity 95.9% and accuracy 94.8% [228]. (The research notes record that a brief in the research assignment referred to a "Lambert 2005 Med Care" paper; no such paper exists, the Med Care paper is 1999 and the 2005 paper is "Designing safe drug names" in Drug Safety [85].) Kondrak and Dorr's BI-SIM orthographic and ALINE phonetic measures outperformed earlier metrics and became the basis of FDA's confusable-name detection tool [237].

Laboratory experiments then located the effect in ordinary word-recognition processes. In recognition memory, orthographic and phonological similarity independently raised false-recognition probability in 30 pharmacists and 66 students [229]; in immediate free recall, familiarity and prescribing frequency improved recall, orthographically similar lists were recalled better (similarity cues retrieval of degraded traces), and phonological similarity showed an inverted-U in pharmacists, which shows similarity helps recall but hurts discrimination [230]. In visual perception of blurred names shown for three seconds, **45 pharmacists misidentified 60.6% of 160 typewritten names and 37 pharmacists 45.7% of 156 handwritten names**, with a protective effect of prescribing frequency, a harmful effect of neighbourhood density and frequency-by-density interactions [231]. In auditory perception, accuracy rose with signal-to-noise ratio, subjective familiarity and national prescribing frequency; for clinicians, similarity to frequently prescribed neighbours reduced accuracy, and "when one name was substituted for another, the substituted name was almost always a more frequently prescribed drug" [232]. This frequency bias has epidemiological confirmation: Anto's 911 London dispensing errors showed selection errors were more likely when a low-frequency drug was orthographically similar to a high-frequency one [24]. The convergent model is lexical access under degraded input: a blurred, handwritten, spoken-in-noise or briefly glimpsed name activates a neighbourhood of stored names and the most frequent candidate wins, which explains both the similarity dose–response and the direction of substitution.

The decisive validation step was to show that laboratory confusability predicts real-world confusion. Schroeder's 80 participants (doctors, nurses, pharmacists, technicians and lay people) completed backward masking, progressive demasking, speech-in-noise and short-term memory tests on 123 pairs; **laboratory error rates predicted pair-specific real-world error rates from a national chain of more than 1,000 stores, the best model explaining 37% of variance, and cross-validated on a second chain explaining 45%**, with short-term memory error rate (β 0.45), backward-masking near-miss rate (β 0.20) and familiarity (β −0.26) the final predictors [86]. The 2025 study extended validation from pairs to individual names: 19 pharmacists and 18 technicians tested on 77 names produced mean error rates correlated with log real-world name-level error rates from the chain's PSO (r = 0.50, p < 0.0001), addressing the pairwise blind spot in regulatory screening since a name like hydroxyzine is confused with hydralazine, Hydrocet, Thorazine and hydrochlorothiazide at once [87]. Lambert's 2016 statistical framework supplies prediction limits for declaring a candidate name "unacceptably confusing" in such tests [233]. A 2026 preprint on the US name lexicon finds mean trigram surprisal of new molecular entity names rose 18% from the 1980s to the 2020s and half of 2020s names contain letter sequences found in no common English word, while nearest-neighbour similarity fell, with the caution that distinctiveness does not necessarily imply safety [236].

The structure of nomenclature itself generates dense neighbourhoods. Bryan's analysis of 7,987 INNs found inconsistent compliance with WHO stem guidance and that stem-sharing pairs often have Levenshtein distance below 5, a built-in tension between stems-as-meaning and dissimilarity-for-safety [238]. Karet's POCA analysis of 95 generic–generic tall-man pairs gave a mean combined score of 69.5 (range 46–90; 94% ≥55, 48% ≥70), with each name averaging 11.2 high-similarity conflicts with marketed names, more than USAN would now accept for a candidate; 63% of these names predate 1980 and 31% share a USAN stem [143]. Shah's FDA analysis found 68% of ISMP brand pairs share a first letter, 52% a string of three or more letters, 91% differ in length by two letters or fewer, and 25% score ≤49 on POCA, "suggesting additional factors or product characteristics" contribute [142]. Her and Toh's 20,000 simulated case–control studies showed matching product attributes (strength, form, class) raise the odds that a pair appears on the ISMP list (univariate OR 4.2–55.5) [165]. Pennsylvania's 2023 pairs, dominated by cef-, -phrine and insulin families, make the same point from field data [16].

### Frequency bias, visual search and the Tall Man literature as mechanism evidence

The Tall Man experiments reveal what drives perceptual confusion as much as whether the intervention works. Filik's eye-tracking showed participants were less likely to pick a similarly named distractor pack when name appearance was altered, with gaze data mirroring errors [239]; but Tall Man helped discrimination only when participants knew its purpose and did nothing for memory confusability [240]. Darker found all-uppercase text gave the same perceptual gain as Tall Man, implicating character size [242]; Schell found high-similarity mismatches were missed regardless of enhancement and that case-based enhancement increased false alarms [243]; Or and Wang found a graded similarity effect with boldface-plus-Tall-Man and red outperforming Tall Man alone [245]; DeHenau found familiarity with both names mitigated the benefit [246]; Liu found enhanced text improved differentiation but not recognition memory [247]. The proximity mechanism is directly demonstrated by Irwin: one versus four similarly named non-targets on screen reduced accuracy and lengthened response times, time pressure hurt non-pharmacists, Tall Man had no effect, and the authors concluded alphabetical storage increases selection risk [244]. Wang and Or's shelf mock-up and eye-tracking in 80 nurses found text enhancement had no effect on search, whereas identical name format on prescription and package and an attention-directing cue helped [248]. Tsuji's eye-tracking of pharmacists at a drug rack observed "visual invasion" by neighbouring non-targets preceding errors, with error rates of 2.8% versus 11.1% with and without product photographs [253]. Mitobe found Japanese nurses discriminate similar pseudo-drug names worse than lay people because top-down knowledge intrudes [252]. Wang's meta-analysis of 20 trials found Tall Man, red, boldface and contrast all reduced omission errors in differentiation tasks (SMD −0.44 to −1.03) with no effect on commission errors or response time [249].

### Handwriting, verbal orders and abbreviations

Degraded input is the classic proximal trigger. Misreading the prescription was the largest single cause of community-pharmacy incidents in Ashcroft's 35 UK pharmacies (24.5%), ahead of similar names (16.8%) [89]; poor handwriting was cited as the main cause of 30.7% of Jordanian dispensing errors [98] and 23.1% in Lebanon [438]; Hoffman and Proulx noted that poor handwriting and clinical similarity exacerbate brand-name confusion such as Celebrex/Cerebyx/Celexa [153]. Brunetti attributed nearly 5% of 643,151 MEDMARX reports to abbreviations [255]. Verbal orders remain under-studied relative to the concern they attract: Wakefield found a "paucity of research evidence" and wide variation in hospital policies [256]; the quantitative evidence is Lambert's auditory work [232], Patanwala's finding that 32.7% of ED pharmacist interceptions involved verbal orders [210], Pennsylvania's demonstration that every tenecteplase/alteplase event with a misheard order involved the spoken abbreviation "TPA" [149], and Japan's "finger-pointing and calling" countermeasure tested by Mitobe [252]. Modern quantitative studies of handwriting-attributable wrong-drug errors are sparse, consistent with the shift to electronic prescribing.

### Technology-induced errors

The dominant technology mechanism is a selection slip in an alphabetically ordered (hence similarity-sorted) list, judged on the first few letters, after which the system validates the choice by printing a label, opening a drawer or populating a dose, giving false confirmation and removing the cross-check a pharmacist's reading of a complete handwritten name once provided (section 5). Three features aggravate it. First, alphabetical adjacency: ISMP Canada's analysis of 32 bisoprolol/bisacodyl incidents among 88,703 reports cited proximity in selection menus and shared mnemonics [274], and NHS England's 2025 alert on 315 penicillin/penicillamine allergy-record errors (one fatal) arose because a "penicill" search returns only penicillamine and because penicillamine precedes penicillin in drop-downs [273]. Second, inconsistent name display and search logic across systems [259,263]; Howe's analysis of Pennsylvania-region reports with explicit language linking harm to EHR usability found medication events predominated [290]. Third, usability failures generally: 36% of 9,000 paediatric safety reports involved a usability issue contributing to the medication event [288]; 97.3% of 1,508 HIT-associated medication errors in 2,700 reports from 595 facilities described usability issues in data entry, workflow support or alerting [289]; and a national Leapfrog analysis found frontline-rated usability positively associated with EHR safety performance across 112 hospitals [439]. Two cautions apply. The Leapfrog CPOE evaluation tool tests drug–drug, drug–allergy, dosing and similar categories and does not include a wrong-drug/LASA scenario, so its finding that CPOE systems failed to flag 33–53% of potentially fatal orders describes general decision-support gaps, not wrong-drug detection [286,287]. And a small controlled laboratory study found incorrect-medication selection in only 1 of 192 CPOE opportunities (0.5%) with no interruption effect (Consensus only) [404], while Wu's simulation found single-click selection more than doubled drop-down selection errors versus double-click (adjusted OR 2.38; Consensus only) [405].

### Packaging, labelling and product appearance

Berman's widely cited estimate attributes up to 25% of US medication errors to name confusion and 33% to packaging or labelling confusion [275]. Bryan notes that LASA potential is compounded when packaging, tablet appearance, strength, route or indication are also similar [69]. Hellier found colour, not shape, drives faster and more accurate strength identification [276]; Chermak's survey of 918 primary package labels found a wide, unstandardised variety of typographic techniques (boldface on 98% of brand labels, underlining 0.2%) [277]; Endestad's redesigned packages with a high-contrast substance-name band cut same-ingredient recognition failures from 41–68% to 8–16% [279]. In the perioperative setting packaging was a factor in 65.2% of LASA incidents [225], 83 of 263 French anesthesia reports underlined packaging similarity [193], and Ryan's scoping review found most interventions non-technological [226]. Patient-reported dispensing errors in Guangzhou were 47.9% attributable to similar packaging or names, and the authors recommended prioritising separation of similar packaging over similar names on shelves [280]. The generic-substitution regime is a regulatory root cause of appearance-based confusion: trade-dress law lets originators prevent generics from copying pill appearance, so bioequivalent products differ in colour and shape [278], with the adherence and perceived-wrong-drug consequences described in section 3 [125,127,128]. Tranexamic acid, bupivacaine and ropivacaine vials sharing a blue cap colour is the packaging mechanism behind the intrathecal TXA series [294].

### Human-factors and organisational contributors

Direct observation quantifies the environmental drivers of selection slips. In Westbrook's 4,271 observed administrations, interruptions occurred in 53.1%; **each interruption raised procedural failures 12.1% and clinical errors 12.7%, major-error risk rose from 2.3% with none to 4.7% with four, and experience gave no protection** [42]. Flynn's videotaped dispensing of 5,072 prescriptions found error rates of 6.65% for sets with an interruption versus 3.23% overall [281]. Ayanaw's scoping review of 51 studies attributes about 9.4% of dispensing errors to interruptions, with pharmacists interrupted from under 5 to over 20 times per hour (Consensus only) [440]; Darbishire observed about 19 interruptions per hour [95]. Workload, staffing, lighting and LASA stock recur across dispensing reviews, with high workload, interruptions, distractions and inadequate lighting objectively shown to increase errors [49,13]; New Hampshire's QRE data show 68% of errors occurred with one pharmacist on duty [103]; UAE predictors were volume ≥60 prescriptions a day (OR 2.1) and ≥4 items per prescription (OR 2.5) [97]; Chou linked errors to return from more than 72 hours' leave [280]. Raban's review found only weak evidence that interruption-reduction interventions reduce errors [282].

Within Reason's taxonomy as applied by Ferner and Aronson [283], most wrong-drug picks are skill-based slips (right intention, wrong object) in a visually dense field, with confirmation bias ("seeing what you expect to see", in the Ontario College of Pharmacists' definition) preventing the post-selection check from catching the slip [441]. Sutherland's hierarchical task analysis of paediatric ICU prescribing linked physical burden (fatigue, distraction, interruption) to omission and selection errors and psychological burden (inexperience, poor decision support) to knowledge errors [284]. Patel's HFACS analyses of neuraxial wrong-drug administrations identified skill-based errors and ampoule misidentification as the predominant unsafe acts [197]. The organisational layer supplies the latent conditions: alphabetical storage policies, override culture, vendor–hospital contracts that slow correction of known hazards, and trade-dress law [268,278]. Phipps' 2025 editorial frames the resulting policy choice as "bending metal or twisting arms", design versus exhortation [285]. Gaps remain: no study isolates fatigue or shift-length effects on wrong-drug selection specifically, no HFACS analysis of a large wrong-drug series outside the neuraxial literature exists, and handoff contributions appear only as themes.

## 7. Patient harm, severity, costs, and legal consequences

### Severity distributions

Per report, wrong-drug errors look no more harmful than average; per fatal event or paid claim they are over-represented. The anchor is Phillips' analysis of 5,366 FDA AERS medication-error reports from 1993–1998: 9.8% were fatal (469 reports), and among fatal reports **improper dose accounted for 40.9%, wrong drug 16.0% and wrong route 9.5%**, with performance or knowledge deficits in 44% and communication errors in 15.8% [291]. Against this, "unauthorized/wrong drug" was 11% of MEDMARX errors in seniors in 2002 [10] and 11% of PA-PSRS reports in 2004 [139], so wrong drug is modestly over-represented among deaths, although the comparison crosses databases. In MEDMARX as a whole 2% of 192,477 records in 2002 involved harm [10], and USP's 2008 LASA report found 1.4% of more than 26,000 name-confusion records resulted in harm or death (secondary) [141]. In Pennsylvania's 2023 verified wrong-drug events 438 of 450 (97.3%) were incidents and 12 (2.7%) serious events, 7 involving high-alert drugs: four insulin events (all hypoglycaemia requiring intervention), two neuromuscular-blocker events, two high-dose carvedilol events with hypotension and prolonged stay, and one haemolytic anaemia after an antibiotic to which the patient had a documented allergy [16]. Nova Scotia's community QREs carried harm in 0.95% [94]; Ontario's in 9.5% (90.5% no harm) [102]; Norway's hospital reports 5.2% severe harm and 0.8% fatal across all types [29]. Nursing-home data give the only direct within-system comparison: wrong product given carried RR 2.22 (95% CI 1.8–2.8) for a serious error, below wrong patient (4.39) but above overdose (1.49) [118]. In anesthesia, 58.9% of 462 webAIRS medication errors caused some harm (7.1% severe) [192], 15.2% of veterinary drug errors were adverse incidents with 4 deaths [130], and Vanderbilt-type NMB swaps produce awake paralysis or death. Only Ro and Jones restrict the denominator to wrong-drug events that reached the patient, so the harm share per reached wrong-drug event is otherwise unmeasured; no full NCC MERP A–I distribution for wrong-drug errors from MEDMARX, NRLS, FAERS or the Dutch and Danish systems was found.

### Fatal case series and sentinel cases

A small number of sentinel cases account for most of the regulatory response.

**Vinca alkaloids given intrathecally.** Babaee's 2025 systematic review found 31 published cases (29 vincristine, 2 vindesine); **only 6 (19.4%) survived the acute phase, 0 of 12 without CSF irrigation versus 6 of 15 with it, and all survivors had severe neurological deficits** [292]. ISMP's 2013 tally counted 120 deaths or neurological devastations when vincristine was prepared in syringes and none in minibags; later ISMP material cites more than 130 cases worldwide and NCCN 125 deaths since the 1960s (secondary; the counts differ by source) [442]. WHO Drug Alert 115 (2007) directed that vincristine be given only IV via minibag [293].

**Tranexamic acid for bupivacaine at spinal anaesthesia.** Patel's MEDLINE review to 2018 found 21 cases, 20 life-threatening and **10 fatal**, ampoule error the cause in 20 [196]; a second series for July 2018–September 2022 added 22 cases with 8 deaths (36%) and 4 permanent harms, 19 of 21 developing refractory status epilepticus, look-alike ampoules the predominant cause [197]; all four maternal deaths in Patel's 29 obstetric neuraxial errors were intrathecal TXA [198]. The FDA issued an alert and label revisions on 3 December 2020, an update on 10 February 2025 stating reports continue, and, according to secondary coverage not verified on fda.gov, a boxed warning and neuraxial contraindication in October 2025 [294]; WHO issued a Medical Product Alert on 16 March 2022; ISMP added IV tranexamic acid to its 2024–2025 Targeted Best Practices [380].

**Emily Jerry (Ohio, 2006).** A technician compounded chemotherapy with 23.4% sodium chloride instead of 0.9% saline; the two-year-old died on 1 March 2006. Pharmacist Eric Cropp lost his licence in April 2007, pleaded no contest to involuntary manslaughter in August 2009 and was sentenced to six months in jail, six months house arrest and probation; Emily's Law (Ohio SB 203, signed 7 January 2009) imposed age, examination and background-check requirements on technicians. The case appears only in trade and advocacy press, not the peer-reviewed pharmacy literature retrieved [295].

**Heparin 10,000 units/mL for Hep-Lock 10 units/mL.** In September 2006 a technician stocked adult heparin in a NICU cabinet at Methodist Hospital, Indianapolis; six infants were overdosed and three died. In November 2007 the Quaid twins and a third infant at Cedars-Sinai received 1,000-fold overdoses and survived. Baxter redesigned labels, the Quaids sued, and ASHP's 2008 IV Safety Summit led to the ASHP–ISMP National Alert Network announced in December 2009 [296,382].

**Vecuronium for Versed (Vanderbilt, 26 December 2017).** Nurse RaDonda Vaught typed "VE" into an ADC override search, selected vecuronium instead of midazolam for a 75-year-old before a PET scan, and the patient died; the vial carried a "Warning: Paralyzing Agent" cap and barcode scanning was unavailable in the imaging area. VUMC did not report the death to the state; CMS threatened Medicare termination in November 2018 and accepted a corrective plan (vecuronium removed from ADCs, paralytics shrink-wrapped). Vaught was indicted in February 2019, lost her licence in July 2021, was **convicted on 25 March 2022 of criminally negligent homicide and gross neglect** and sentenced on 13 May 2022 to three years' supervised probation with judicial diversion; the Tennessee Court of Appeals affirmed the licence revocation on 20 March 2025 [297,298]. ISMP, ECRI, ANA and AORN condemned the prosecution as a "dangerous precedent"; Dekeseredy's qualitative study documents nurses' subsequent fear of reporting [300]; **Kentucky HB 159, signed 26 March 2024, became the first US statute shielding health-care workers from criminal prosecution for unintentional medical errors** [299].

**Brand-name confusions that drove renames.** Celebrex/Celexa/Cerebyx (56 confusion reports by May 1999, 95 by August, 22 in which the wrong drug was taken, no deaths) [153]; Lamictal/Lamisil relabelling at FDA request (December 2001); Zyprexa/Zyrtec (79 mix-up reports since 1996 per Lilly's January 2005 letter, excessive sleepiness and hospitalisations, no deaths; label changed to ZyPREXA) [301]; Kapidex→Dexilant (announced 4 March 2010 after dispensing errors with Casodex and Kadian) [302]; Brintellix→Trintellix (FDA July 2015 communication reported 50 confusion reports since September 2013, 12 with the wrong drug given; 55 total by the May 2016 rename) [303]; Amaryl/Reminyl, with one death, led to Reminyl becoming Razadyne in 2005 [409]; Amaryl/Almarl mix-ups with deaths in Japan prompted a 2004 PMDA notice and a later rename (web-only) [390]; Durezol/Durasal produced one serious eye injury in 2011 for a wart remover that had never passed FDA name review [444]. The MHRA's January 2018 Drug Safety Update reported Yellow Card harm, including fatal cases, for clobazam/clonazepam, atenolol/amlodipine, propranolol/prednisolone, risperidone/ropinirole, sulfadiazine/sulfasalazine and amlodipine/nimodipine [305]. Pure name-confusion cases have produced renames and relabels but few documented deaths; the deaths cluster where a high-alert drug, a storage or selection failure, and a look-alike container coincide. No verified fatal case was found for hydroxyzine/hydralazine (a 2010 Louisville wrongful-death suit was located), Brevibloc/Brevital, Zyprexa/Zyrtec or Celebrex/Cerebyx [443].

### Costs

No peer-reviewed estimate isolates the economic cost of wrong-drug or LASA errors. The general anchors are the IOM's 2006 figure of at least 1.5 million preventable ADEs a year and **$3.5 billion in hospital costs** [306]; Elliott's model of 237 million medication errors a year in England, 72% with little or no harm potential, and definitely avoidable ADEs costing **£98.5 million, 181,626 bed-days and 1,708 deaths a year** [37]; Walsh's review finding mean cost per error from €2.58 to €111,727 across 16 studies, only one of adequate quality [307]; and Choi's attributable cost of $8,439–$8,898 per hospital error, with wrong medication among the four most frequent types [313]. Chou's patient-reported dispensing errors cost US$188,406 in medication loss over 11 years [280]. Any wrong-drug-specific cost figure would have to multiply a wrong-drug share (11–20% of reports; 16% of fatal FDA reports) by these totals and would be an inference.

### Liability and legal consequences

Wrong drug is the largest single allegation in pharmacist liability claims. In HPSO/CNA closed claims with payment (2012–2016), **wrong-drug dispensing accounted for 36.8% of 184 claims (down from 43.8% in the 2013 dataset) and wrong dose 15.3% (from 31.5%), with an average payment of $124,407** [104]; a Pharmacists Mutual review of 500 claims is reported second-hand as 52% wrong drug, 27% wrong strength and 7.4% wrong directions (undated, unverified) [311]. Rothschild's analysis of a New England insurer found ADEs in 6.3% of 2,040 claims, 73% preventable, 46% life-threatening or fatal, with preventable inpatient ADEs costing $376,500 in defence costs [308]; CRICO found 1 in 9 malpractice cases involved a medication error and 32% of medication cases involved death versus 18% of other cases (secondary) [309]; nursing medication claims closed 2017–2021 had an average indemnity of $366,002 [310]. In England, 62 anesthesia drug-error claims cost £4.28 million, half alleged a wrong drug, 16 involved a neuromuscular blocker, and fewer than half were judged preventable by an ideal double check [195]. Canadian disciplinary hearings (558 cases, 2010–2017) commonly cited dispensing the wrong drug under unskilled practice, with 4% revocations [314]. The criminalization pathway from Cropp (2009) to Vaught (2022) and the Kentucky counter-reaction (2024) is itself now a policy consequence of wrong-drug errors.

## 8. Prevention strategies and the evidence for each

### Tall Man (mixed-case) lettering

Tall Man lettering is universally recommended (FDA Name Differentiation Project 2001, ISMP and FDA lists, ACSQHC, ISMP Canada, HQSC New Zealand, WHO) and its empirical base is almost entirely laboratory perception tasks with mixed results. The laboratory record is summarised in section 6: positive in same/different discrimination and change detection [239,241,246], null in visual search among proximate names [244,248] and in recognition memory [240,247], equivalent to all-caps [242], and sometimes harmful through increased false alarms [243]. The best-looking clinical result is Lohmeyer's randomised in-situ simulation in 30 ICU nurses selecting from 10 syringes: **error rate 5.3% (8/150) without versus 0.7% (1/150) with Tall Man labels**, with longer dwell time and more fixations on the name, strongest with mid-to-end capitalisation [224]; it rests on nine errors. Shawahna's randomised cross-over in 25 nurses transcribing charts found Tall Man significantly reduced transcription errors on 20 similar names [318]. The single large real-world evaluation is Zhong's segmented regression of 1,676,700 hospitalisations in 42 US children's hospitals (2004–2012) across 12 pairs: **no statistically significant change in intercept or slope of LASA error rates after the 2007 Tall Man recommendation for any pair or for the combined rate** [152]. Lambert's accompanying editorial, "Incomplete and conflicting evidence suggest need for definitive study", noted the null may reflect non-implementation and that Tall Man addresses only visual perception [234]; Trbovich's editorial concluded the strategy "did not demonstrate effectiveness" [319]. Tseng's uncontrolled Taiwanese before–after found the share of near-miss wrong-medicine dispensings attributable to name confusion fell from 66.3% to 55.0% after Tall Man and other measures [156]. Systematic reviews agree: Larmené-Beld (16 studies, 13 laboratory) found 6 with lower error rates, 1 higher, and no effect in the one hospital study [250]; Bryan's PROSPERO-registered review found six eligible studies, all laboratory, Tall Man the only intervention tested in RCTs, "limited efficacy and no consensus", and proposed a "quasi-placebo effect" in which users benefit only when they know its purpose [70]; Wang's meta-analysis found a benefit for omission errors only [249]; Bhat's 2026 review of 25 experiments calls it "not effective as a standalone solution" (Consensus only) [251]; the earlier reviews by Ostini (32 publications, "very little intervention research") and Ciociano and Bagnasco ("LASA errors are not supported by reliable statistics") reached the same verdict [320,321]. ISMP's own 2023 survey of 298 practitioners found 94% use mixed case and 87% recalled an instance where it prevented an error, but also acknowledged that overuse "may reduce effectiveness since names no longer appear novel" [317]; ACSQHC's commissioned evaluation stated its data "do not establish that the proposed application of Tall Man lettering works on its own" and the HQSC de-listed PHENOXYMETHylpenicillin in 2023 after a near miss [316,384]. The definitive trial called for in 2016 has not appeared, and no evidence exists on Tall Man in electronic pick-lists specifically, where most CPOE wrong-drug errors arise. Lambert, Schroeder, Cohen and Paparella's 2022 editorial accordingly argues that reducing wrong-drug errors "requires a multimodal response" [235].

### Barcode medication administration and pharmacy barcode verification

Barcode verification is the intervention with the strongest and most specific real-world evidence. In the pharmacy, Poon's direct observation of 115,164 doses before and 253,984 after barcode-assisted dispensing found target dispensing errors leaving the pharmacy fell 85% (0.37% to 0.06%) and potential ADEs 63%; **configurations scanning every dose cut target dispensing errors 93–96% and potential ADEs 86–97%, while scanning only one of several identical doses cut errors 60% and increased target potential ADEs 2.4-fold** [48]. Helmons' barcode-assisted ADC refilling cut wrong drug/strength/form pocket errors from 0.91% to 0.21% [58]; Oldland's filling errors fell to 0.050% only when barcode automation was combined with relabelling and training [328]; Denny's barcode-plus-image-capture workflow in oral-liquid repackaging reduced **wrong-drug errors from 160 to 5 events (108,611 versus 63,684 doses), from a peak of 2.97 to 0 per 1,000 syringes** [324]; Küng's barcode-assisted preparation reduced wrong-medication preparation errors (OR 0.38) [323]; Takase's robotic system brought wrong-drug and wrong-strength dispensing "to almost zero" [326]; Yu's staged ADC→BCMA→smart-counter programme reduced wrong-drug dispensing, the most common baseline type, by 51%, 57% and 81% [327]; Cousein's unit-dose robot plus ADC cut ward wrong-drug errors 93.7% [325]. At the bedside, Poon's 14,041 observed administrations showed non-timing errors falling from 11.5% to 6.8% (RRR 41.4%), potential ADEs from 3.1% to 1.6%, transcription errors to zero, and, as tabulated by Shah's review, **wrong-medication errors from 1.0% to 0.4% (RRR 57.4%, 95% CI 39.2–79.3%)** [51,52]. Other before–after studies report aggregate reductions (Paoletti 54% [330]; Helmons 58% excluding timing in medical-surgical units but no change in ICUs [54]; Seibert accuracy 92% to 96% [331]; Bonkowski 6.3% to 1.2% and Owens 2.96% to 0.76% in EDs [211,212]; Thompson harmful errors 0.65 to 0.29 per 100,000 [332]) without wrong-drug breakouts, and DeYoung's ICU study found the 56% reduction was entirely wrong-time with "no significant differences in other error types" [329]. In anesthesia, Merry's randomised trial of a multimodal system with pre-administration scanning found 9.1 versus 11.6 errors per 100 administrations overall and 6.0 versus 9.7 when scanning was performed consistently [186]; Bowdle's errors fell from 0.39% to 0.23% of anesthetics with vial-swap errors significantly reduced [184]; scanning compliance was 8.7–25% without audit, feedback and incentives [220,221]. Franklin's closed-loop system in London cut prescribing errors from 3.8% to 2.0% of orders and administration errors from 7.0% to 4.3% of doses without a wrong-drug breakout [53]. Reviews give pooled RR 0.71 (95% CI 0.53–0.95) for BCMA with eMAR [55], cite 93–96% reductions in dispensing errors with barcode-assisted dispensing [322], note that barcoding reduces "targeted wrong dose, wrong drug, wrong patient, unauthorized drug, and wrong route errors" [333], and record that only barcoding is "strongly evidence-based" among LASA strategies [65]. The limits are workarounds [268,46,269], wrong or unreadable barcodes [435,436], scan coverage gaps in ORs, imaging and EMS, and the fact that wrong-drug errors upstream of the scan (ward preparation, ADC misfills) are not caught [33].

### CPOE design and clinical decision support, including indication-based prescribing

Generic CPOE roughly halves medication errors (RR 0.46 versus paper in Nuckols' 16 studies [343]; Keers RR 0.51 [55]; Cochrane OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.31–1.79, for CPOE versus paper and 0.85 for improved versus standard CPOE [344]) but creates the selection errors described in sections 5 and 6, and no study was found evaluating menu or pick-list redesign (list length, adjacency, auto-complete rules) on wrong-drug rates. Indication-linked decision support is the best-documented CPOE strategy against wrong drug. Galanter's indication alerts at UI Health intercepted **176 drug-name-confusion errors across 127,458 alerts (about 1.4 per 1,000 alerted orders)**, 36 of 39 pairs absent from the ISMP list, with residents intercepted more often than attendings (OR 0.35) [65], and 32 wrong-patient errors (0.25 per 1,000 alerts) [66]. Cheng showed indications could in principle disambiguate about 80% of ISMP pairs (59% no overlap, 21% partial) [144]; Seoane-Vazquez found FDA-approved indications differentiated 26 of 33 pairs (78.8%) [338]. Kron's six expert panels listed LASA avoidance among the anticipated benefits of indications-based ordering [339]. Garabedian's randomised usability study of 32 prescribers found **medication errors in 38 of 128 sessions with vendor systems versus 7 of 128 with an indications-based prototype**, at half the clicks [267]. The constraint is that indications are present on only 7.41% of 4.36 million outpatient prescriptions [337] and diagnoses are unavailable at order entry, which limited Lambert's algorithm (PPV 12.1%) to retrospective use [64]. Feather's and Pairman's reviews find indication-based prescribing interventions mostly measure indication documentation rather than error outcomes (abstract only) [340,341]; Schiff's 2016 NEJM Perspective framed indication as the "sixth right" [336] (the notes record this is NEJM, not JAMIA as one brief had it). LASA-specific pop-up alerts, as deployed by most vendors, have no controlled evaluation; Campmans found 86% of 269 Dutch pharmacy staff had noticed name-confusion alerts and 9% recalled one preventing a wrong-drug dispensing [354]; alert override rates generally run 46–96% [342]. Chen's machine-learning CDSS in Taiwan generated alerts on 2.36% of 1,206,895 prescriptions and intercepted 470 errors (16.4 per 1,000 alerted orders) including LASA pairs [71]. Syrowatka's Making Healthcare Safer IV review rates CPOE with medication decision support as reducing errors with moderate certainty while noting high override rates and decision-support-induced errors (abstract only) [345]. Leapfrog's national CPOE evaluation shows decision support improving (mean score 58.1% to 66.2% across 1,599 hospitals, 2017–2018) but does not test wrong-drug selection [287].

### Pharmacist verification and double checks

Double-checking has weak and inconsistent evidence. Alsulami's review found one clinical trial in which errors fell from 2.98 to 2.12 per 1,000 administrations and concluded there was "insufficient evidence to either support or refute" [346]; Koyama's 13-study review reached the same conclusion, noting only three studies distinguished independent from primed checks [347]; Westbrook observed 5,140 paediatric doses and found **mandated checks were independent in only 1.0% of cases, conferred no benefit (OR 0.89), and consumed 6.4 minutes each**, although optional checks that were performed were associated with fewer errors (OR 0.71) [348]; her 2026 randomised simulation in 82 nurses found independent double-checking raised detection from 66.3% to 77.7% overall (aIRR 1.11) but only among experienced nurses, with early-career nurses detecting more errors alone, and concluded it "may be unsuitable as a universal safety strategy" [349]. ISMP continues to endorse independent double checks for selected high-alert situations, citing detection of up to 95% of errors in idealised conditions [350]; Cranshaw judged fewer than half of anesthesia drug-error claims preventable by an ideal double check [195]. Pharmacist review recovers many errors (2.9 per 100 medications in four EDs, 90.3% before reaching the patient [351]; 78.1% of a French psychiatric hospital's errors intercepted at order review, where wrong drug was 29.4–30.3% of errors [399]) but wrong dose dominates recovered errors and no study isolates pharmacist review's effect on wrong-drug orders. Technician verification studies are a hidden wrong-drug literature because their endpoints are product-selection accuracy: pooled technician final-check accuracy 99.6% versus pharmacist 99.3% across 11 studies [352]; undetected community product-selection errors 0.01–0.085% [353,105]; technology-assisted technician verification non-inferior across 12,328 first doses and 8,000 sterile preparations [445].

### Naming review and renames

Pre-approval review is now systematised (section 9) and the Lambert group has validated psycholinguistic tests against real-world chain-pharmacy error rates [86,87], but **no study shows that regulatory name review has reduced the incidence of wrong-drug errors, and no post-rename surveillance of Kapidex→Dexilant or Brintellix→Trintellix has been published** [302,303]. Emmerton's adaptation of FDA's open-source algorithms to the Australian register found computed scores correlated only modestly with manual expert scores (r = 0.324; κ −0.162 for risk categories), so algorithmic and expert judgements of confusability diverge [355]. The biologic four-letter suffix convention has no published effect evaluation [359]. Within-class stem design is a recognised but unaddressed risk [238,143].

### Packaging, storage and ADC configuration

Consensus recommendations (colour differentiation, prominent active-ingredient placement, separation of LASA stock, locked-lidded ADC pockets, prefilled syringes, removal of concentrated products from carts) rest on case reports, audits and a few experiments. Endestad's redesigned packages cut confusion errors by about two-thirds [279]; Laxton's colour-coded compartmentalised trays improved syringe-error detection from 83% to 91% (d′ 1.50 to 2.28) [223]; Hirabayashi's coloured syringes reduced swaps from 5 in 5,901 to 0 in 6,078 procedures [222]; Fasting's colour-coded labels reduced ampoule swaps but not syringe swaps [177]; Haslam reported high latent error rates during the transition to international colour coding [448]; Larmené-Beld's review found colour coding had no effect on syringe swaps in one study [250]. Webster's prefilled-syringe system in 74,478 anesthetics reduced parenteral drug errors from 0.049% to 0.032% of administrations (35%), with 0 versus 11 major adverse outcomes (p = 0.055) [217]; Grigg's medication template halved swap, preparation and miscalculation errors (0.97 to 0.35 per 1,000 anesthetics) [218]; Ribed's FMEA-based re-engineering reduced the risk priority number of critical failure modes by 72.9% [459]. No controlled study with error outcomes exists for neuromuscular-blocker segregation, standard-concentration labelling or distinctive caps; the Vanderbilt corrective plan (vecuronium removed from ADCs, paralytics shrink-wrapped) is the most consequential unevaluated example [297]. ISMP's ADC guidelines and LASA guidance recommend separate physical locations and locked pockets over open-matrix drawers (secondary) [272], and Ruutiainen's audit demonstrates how often this is not done [208]. Only 19% of 104 European hospital pharmacies used Tall Man on parenteral labels, 29% colour coding and 27% barcodes in 2020 [412].

### Organisational and educational measures

Education meta-analyses are dominated by uncontrolled before–after studies: Härkänen pooled Hedges' g 1.06 across 14 nurse-education studies but the only strong-quality study showed the weakest effect [361]; Jaam found pharmacist-led education OR 0.38 (0.22–0.65) [362]; Keers found simulation-based nurse education RR 0.17 and pharmacist-led training RR 0.76 [55]; Berdot's controlled set found no effect (OR 0.72, p = 0.3) [56]; Sim's SEIPS-based programme improved culture without changing error rates [449]. No LASA-specific educational trial exists, and no study of read-back for verbal orders or of patient detection of wrong-drug errors was found. The Joint Commission's requirement for a locally maintained LASA list acknowledges that confusable pairs are institution-specific [375].

### AI, machine learning and emerging tools

Probabilistic outlier-detection decision support generates low-burden alerts (0.4–2.4% of orders) of which 70–85% are judged clinically valid and about 40% change orders: Schiff's MedAware evaluation across 747,985 patients [363], Segal's prospective ward deployment (alerts on 0.4% of orders, 85% valid, 43% changed orders) [364], Rozenblum's finding that 68.2% of alerts would not have fired in rule-based systems [365]. These catch "a drug this patient would never plausibly receive", the signature of a wrong-drug order, but report validity rather than wrong-drug incidence. Lester's randomised web-based verification task found pharmacists rejected incorrect drugs 96.1% of the time with uncertainty-aware AI versus 81.2% without [367]. Incident-report NLP shows "Wrong Drug" is among the most machine-learnable categories (Boxley's XGBoost on 3,861 reports [368]; Wong's deep network, accuracy 0.94 across the medication rights [374]; Krifors' o4-mini with 96% concordance on 200 reports [369]). Computer-vision pill recognition reaches 80–92% under optimal conditions but 21–90% on real clinical images [372]. A pharmacist working with a retrieval-augmented LLM co-pilot detected serious-harm errors 1.5-fold better than alone across 91 scenarios [413]. Large language models currently handle names and formulations poorly (GPT-4o 49% formulation-matching accuracy [371]) although Amazon Pharmacy's fine-tuned MEDIC model cut direction near-misses 33% in production [370]. Syrowatka's review found 94% of AI medication-safety studies assessed technical performance only [366]. No prospective controlled study shows reduced wrong-drug incidence attributable to machine learning, and no peer-reviewed neural drug-name-similarity model validated against error data supersedes POCA or the Lambert metrics [373].

### What the systematic reviews conclude

Broad intervention reviews (Cochrane [344]; Keers [55]; Berdot [56]; Nuckols [343]; Manias; Lapkin) almost never report wrong drug as a separate outcome; Manias and Lapkin were confirmed by full-text query to contain no wrong-drug figure [446,447]. The LASA-specific reviews (Larmené-Beld [250], Bryan [70], Bhat [251], Ryan [226]) find laboratory evidence only. The apparent absence of wrong-drug effects in reviews therefore reflects reporting, not proof of no effect; the few primary studies that break out wrong drug are barcode studies (DeYoung null; Poon, Küng, Denny, Helmons positive).

## 9. Regulatory, policy, and naming-review landscape

### United States: FDA

FDA's wrong-drug programme began with the Office of Generic Drugs' Name Differentiation Project in 2001, which sent 142 letters covering 159 applications asking manufacturers of 16 look-alike generic pairs to adopt Tall Man lettering voluntarily; the list now holds 23 entries [145]. FDA states it reviews about 400 proposed brand names a year and historically rejected about a third [409]. The Division of Medication Error Prevention and Analysis (DMEPA) uses the Phonetic and Orthographic Computer Analysis (POCA) tool, built on Kondrak and Dorr's algorithms, searching Drugs@FDA, RxNorm, biologic suffixes and USAN names; the public version launched in 2021. The review threshold is empirically anchored to recall rather than prediction: Shah reports that POCA at a combined score of 50% matched 75% of FDA-identified confused names, which set the original ≥50% review threshold, later revised so that ≥70% is "highly similar", 55–69% moderate and <55% low [142,143,357]. PDUFA IV (2007) committed FDA to a proprietary-name review pilot that collapsed in 2011 for "insufficient participation"; the "Contents of a Complete Submission for the Evaluation of Proprietary Names" guidance was drafted in 2008 and finalised in April 2016; the "Best Practices in Developing Proprietary Names" guidance was drafted in May 2014 and **finalised for prescription drugs on 8 December 2020**, instructing sponsors to report POCA matches at or above 55% and citing Lambert 1999 as its research basis [357]; the container-label and carton-labelling guidance was drafted in 2013 and **finalised 18 May 2022** [358]; the "Nonproprietary Naming of Biological Products" guidance (January 2017) attached meaningless four-letter suffixes to prevent inadvertent substitution [359]. Post-marketing actions are case-driven: Lamictal relabelling (2001), Zyprexa relabelling (2005), the Reminyl→Razadyne (2005), Kapidex→Dexilant (2010) and Brintellix→Trintellix (2016) renames, Durezol/Durasal and tranexamic acid alerts (2011, 2020, 2025), and the 2025 TXA boxed warning reported in secondary sources [294,301,302,303]. FDA's own figure for the share of reported medication errors attributable to name confusion is "about 10 percent" [409]; the widely quoted "25%/one in four" traces to 1990s USP/ISMP MERP voluntary-report analyses (an ISMP alert of 19 April 2000 and a 1996 USP report citing 22%, neither retrievable) propagated through Lambert 1999–2001, PA-PSRS 2004 and later reviews [228,229,139,69]; Bryan's defined-report-set estimates are 6.2–14.7% of medication-error events and 7% of near misses [69]. FDA's Sentinel system has been used to detect name-confusion dispensing from claims [150], and FDA staff have analysed the ISMP list [142], "as directed" prescriptions that remove a differentiating cue [450], and foreign brand names identical to US names [164]. FDA's January 2025 draft guidance on AI in regulatory decision-making is not specific to naming, and ISMP was awarded an FDA medication-error analysis contract in 2025 (press release).

### The Joint Commission and CMS

The Joint Commission issued a Sentinel Event Alert on LASA names in May 2001 citing USP MERP's estimate that about 15% of reports involved similar names, approved the LASA National Patient Safety Goal in 2004, and required from 2005 (Requirement 3C, later NPSG.03.03.01) that organisations identify and annually review a list of at least ten LASA pairs drawn from its own list and act to prevent interchange. In 2010 the requirement moved into Medication Management standard MM.01.02.01 (list, actions, annual review), the requirement to select from the Joint Commission's list was dropped, and the Commission now refers organisations to ISMP's list; effective January 2026 the NPSGs were restructured as National Performance Goals with medication-management items reorganised rather than removed (all from secondary coverage; the original alert text and manual wording were not retrieved) [375]. The Joint Commission and Joint Commission International served as the WHO Collaborating Centre that issued the 2007 Patient Safety Solutions. No CMS Condition of Participation specific to LASA was located; CMS acted in the Vanderbilt case through survey authority [297].

### ISMP and ECRI

ISMP maintains the de facto US reference lists: the Confused Drug Names list (about 528 pairs after the 2023 addition of more than 80, updated through June 2024) [137], the FDA/ISMP Tall Man list (revised January 2023 after a survey of 298 practitioners) [317], and the High-Alert Medications list (2024 edition added tranexamic acid because of look-alike vial mix-ups with anesthetics) [146]. It runs the National Medication Errors Reporting Program and the Vaccine Errors Reporting Program (launched September 2012), co-issues National Alert Network alerts with ASHP (announced December 2009 after the heparin cases; now within NCC MERP) [382], became an ECRI subsidiary on 2 January 2020 [381], and publishes biennial Targeted Medication Safety Best Practices for Hospitals (launched 2014 with six practices; 2024–2025 added IV tranexamic acid, transitions of care and vaccines; **2026–2027 added Best Practices 23 IV push, 24 scanning machine-readable codes on ID bands and products, and 25 culture of safety, for 25 in total**; Becker's report of "15 practices" conflicts with ISMP's own page and should not be used) [380]. Its ADC guidelines (2019) recommend a five-letter minimum override search, sequestration of paralysing agents and tiered override controls (secondary) [272]. ECRI's 2026 Top 10 Patient Safety Concerns lists manufacturer look-alike packaging and labelling at number 10, citing "up to 29% of events" reported to ISMP MERP (secondary) [451]. ISMP publicly opposed the criminalization of medication errors in 2019 and 2022 and welcomed Kentucky's 2024 law [297,299].

### WHO

WHO's LASA output runs from Patient Safety Solution 1, "Look-Alike, Sound-Alike Medication Names" (May 2007, issued by the Joint Commission-led Collaborating Centre, recommending name screening and physical separation) [376], through Drug Alert 115 on vincristine (2007) [293], the Medication Without Harm challenge (2017, target 50% reduction in severe avoidable medication harm) and its 2019 technical report on high-risk situations, the TXA Medical Product Alert (16 March 2022) [294], to the first LASA-specific technical report, "Medication safety for look-alike, sound-alike medicines" (ISBN 9789240058897, 20 October 2023), whose recommendations could not be retrieved [377]. A verification task in the notes establishes that **WHO's High 5s project never had a LASA standard operating procedure**: its five original problems were medication reconciliation, correct-site surgery, concentrated injectable medicines, handover communication and health-care-associated infection; three SOPs were implemented and the latter two abandoned; look-alike labelling appears only within the concentrated-injectables SOP rationale [378,379]. WHO's INN stem system is itself a documented LASA driver [238], and Kavanagh has shown UK "co-" combination names fall outside the INN remit and cause errors [391].

### Europe, UK, Australia, Canada, Japan and others

EMA's Name Review Group applies the CHMP guideline on invented names, revised in 2014 (effective 1 January 2015, maximum two proposed names) and again as revision 7 (adopted December 2023, in force 1 January 2024, adding a 50% similarity rule against INNs); it is the only regulator publishing a running tally, with **134 names accepted and 106 rejected in 2025 to November** and 44 accepted, 38 rejected at the February 2026 meeting, similarity to another invented name the commonest objection [356]. EMA's Good Practice Guide on risk minimisation and prevention of medication errors took effect 27 November 2015. The UK relies on MHRA Drug Safety Updates (April 2013; January 2018 naming six pairs with fatal cases) [305], NRLS/LFPSE reporting, and NHS England National Patient Safety Alerts, the latest being the November 2025 penicillin/penicillamine alert [273]; no NHS alert on LASA names as such was issued 2014–2026, and NHS England's 2022/23 research-needs document lists a multifaceted approach to LASA confusion as a research need. Australia's ACSQHC published a National Tall Man Lettering List in 2011 derived from Emmerton's risk matrix (35 "extreme risk" pairs among 250) [315], added monoclonal-antibody and kinase-inhibitor supplements in 2020, renamed it the National Mixed-Case Lettering List in April 2024, and ties compliance to the NSQHS Medication Safety Standard [316]. New Zealand's HQSC list held 224 names in October 2023 after a de-listing [384]. Canada has ISMP Canada's TALLman list (October 2015) and principles (March 2016), Health Canada's brand-name review guidance developed with Lambert and ISMP Canada [360], and bulletins such as the 2012 bisoprolol/bisacodyl analysis [274]. Japan screens katakana names before approval using JAPIC similarity programs with heavy weight on shared prefixes, issued the 2004 Amaryl/Almarl notice and a November 2024 PMDA warning on generic-name similarity (web-only) [390,411]. Brazil's ANVISA RDC 768/2022 defines Tall Man lettering on generic names (web-only) [389]; Malaysia's Ministry of Health issued a LASA handling guide in 2012; Iran's National Drug Naming Committee was dissolved for four years and re-established after error rates reportedly rose [385]; Saudi CBAHI accreditation compliance with the LASA standard rose from 40.79 in 2016 to 96.10 in 2023 across 161 hospitals, although only 33% of 78 Saudi hospitals had a LASA list in 2012 [386,387] and only 2% of 41 Khartoum hospitals in 2016 [388]. Lizano-Díez concludes that "a common policy worldwide on the authorization of unique names for innovative medicines does not exist" [304].

### Pharmacy practice standards

USP General Chapter <17> (USP 36–NF 31, November 2012) standardises patient-centred prescription labels and permits the purpose for use when the prescriber provides it [383]; the indication-on-prescription movement has evidence that indications discriminate most LASA pairs and intercept errors at order entry but uptake is about 7% [144,65,337]. No state board administrative code on LASA storage, NABP Model Act LASA language or USP <1066> text was retrieved; 16 of 50 state boards require continuous-quality-improvement programmes (Consensus only) [452]. Emily's Law (Ohio 2009) is the clearest state-law response to a wrong-product compounding death [295].

## 10. Evidence gaps and a research agenda

The literature's first and most consequential gap is measurement. Wrong-patient ordering has an NQF-endorsed automated measure (retract-and-reorder) with published rates per 100,000 orders across inpatient, ED, outpatient and NICU settings [61,62,63]; wrong-drug ordering has none. Lambert's indication-mismatch algorithm triggers on 0.5% of orders with a 12.1% positive predictive value and cannot run in real time for want of coded diagnoses [64]; Adelman's AHRQ-funded wrong-medication RAR measures exist only in grant deliverables and a 2026 methods framework [73], and a related AHRQ report stating that wrong-drug RAR events declined weekly after indication alerts could not be retrieved [458]. A validated, EHR-derived wrong-drug incidence measure, calibrated against chart review and reported per 100,000 orders by setting, would do for wrong drug what RAR did for wrong patient and would finally allow trials with an incidence endpoint. Nothing comparable exists for dispensing or administration either; outside Berdot's pooled 0.3%, Cina's 0.75% undetected and a handful of observational studies, every wrong-drug denominator in this review is a report count, and the Flynn 2003 wrong-drug numerator, the Keers 2013 subtype table, the Nanji 2016 wrong-drug count, the Truitt 2016 type-by-period table and the Ro and Jones 2025 pair tables all remain unextracted from paywalled or image-only sources [84,38,185,334,16].

Second, the intervention evidence is almost entirely laboratory or before–after, and the one intervention regulators most consistently mandate has the weakest outcome evidence. The definitive Tall Man trial called for in 2016 has not been run [234]; nothing tests Tall Man in electronic pick-lists, where most CPOE wrong-drug selections occur; no study evaluates pick-list design variables (list length, alphabetical adjacency, minimum-character search, brand/generic display) on wrong-drug rates despite Galanter's and Westbrook's mechanistic findings [65,60]; vendor LASA pop-up alerts have no controlled evaluation; and the natural experiments offered by the Kapidex and Brintellix renames and by the biologic suffix convention have no published post-change surveillance [302,303,359]. Barcode verification is the exception, yet its flagship administration studies report aggregate non-timing errors and the wrong-drug effect is known only through Shah's re-tabulation of Poon and through pharmacy repackaging and refill studies [52,324,58]; no pooled wrong-drug estimate for BCMA exists, and no study addresses the preparation-stage errors that bedside scanning misses [33]. Double checks, education, storage segregation, neuromuscular-blocker sequestration and packaging redesign are consensus-based, and the only randomised double-check evidence is a simulation showing benefit confined to experienced nurses [349]. A research agenda should prioritise cluster-randomised or stepped-wedge trials of pick-list and search design and of indications-first ordering with wrong-drug incidence as the endpoint, pre-specified wrong-drug outcomes in every BCMA and ADC evaluation, and mandatory post-rename surveillance using Sentinel-type claims algorithms [150].

Third, the mechanism program has outrun regulatory practice. Psycholinguistic tests now predict real-world pair and individual-name error rates [86,87], but POCA's thresholds are anchored to recall of already-confused names, 25% of confused brand pairs score below 50, and the chain-pharmacy error data that make validation possible are held by a single PSO under confidentiality, with absolute rates scaled by an undisclosed constant [142,86]. Open access to de-identified, denominator-bearing dispensing-error data from chains, PBMs and regulators (the Nova Scotia, Ontario and New Hampshire programmes show it is feasible [94,102,103]) is the single structural change most likely to accelerate the field. Within-class stem design (cef-, -phrine, -tinib, insulin brand families), now the dominant pattern in Pennsylvania's 2023 data [16], has no agreed metric and no regulatory remedy, and the 2026 finding that new names are becoming more distinctive while confusion persists [236] implies that pairwise orthographic screening is reaching its limits.

Fourth, whole settings and populations are unmeasured. No ICU study since 2010 reports a wrong-drug rate per administration from direct observation; no EMS study reports true drug swaps per administration; no paediatric or neonatal study reports a different-active-ingredient wrong-drug rate per order or dose; no mail-order or telepharmacy dataset since 2015 breaks out wrong drug; no study measures patients actually taking the wrong drug at home with a dose denominator or the proportion of dispensing errors first detected by patients; no veterinary or dental study has a per-prescription denominator; and no COVID-era study compares wrong-drug rates with the pre-pandemic baseline. Non-Latin-script name similarity has been quantified only for Japanese katakana; Chinese-character and Arabic-script confusion, and transliteration-driven confusion in bilingual dispensing, are unstudied, while LMIC data show the dominant mechanism there is brand-name and combination-product proliferation that US/UK generic-pair lists do not address [157,160,161,430]. Korean, Brazilian, Chinese and Saudi national LASA lists could not be retrieved, and Turkish, Egyptian and most sub-Saharan African wrong-drug proportions are absent from PubMed.

Fifth, harm and cost are unquantified at the level that matters. No analysis gives a full NCC MERP A–I distribution for wrong-drug errors from any national database; no economic estimate isolates wrong-drug or LASA costs; liability data show wrong drug is the leading pharmacist allegation but CRICO's wrong-drug share and the Pharmacists Mutual study are available only second-hand [104,309,311]. The frequently quoted "25%" and "250,000 Americans harmed annually" figures have no retrievable numerators and denominators and should be retired from the literature in favour of defined-report-set estimates (6.2–14.7% of events, 7% of near misses) and the per-dose and per-prescription figures assembled here [69,165]. Finally, the legal environment is now a research variable in its own right: the Cropp and Vaught prosecutions and Kentucky's 2024 statute create a natural experiment in whether criminal liability suppresses the reporting on which every figure in this review depends [297,299,300], and no study has yet measured it.
